# The man who built the BlackBerry

Mike Lazaridis was an electronics wizard from early childhood. At four he built a record player out of Legos and rubber bands; in high school his teachers called him to fix their broken TVs; he designed a better buzzer for quiz-bowl teams that paid for his first year of college. He dropped out of engineering school just before finishing, won an Emmy and an Oscar for a device that read bar codes on movie film, and then dreamed up the BlackBerry. Its fans ranged from Bill Gates to Oprah Winfrey, and President Obama refused to hand his over to the Secret Service. As of summer 2009 it held nearly half of the U.S. smartphone market. By 2014 its share was under 1 percent.

The standard explanation for a fall like that is that *BlackBerry failed to adapt*. Grant objects to the grammar. Adapting to a changing environment "isn't something a company does—it's something *people* do in the multitude of decisions they make every day." As cofounder, president, and co-CEO, Lazaridis ran the technical and product decisions. So the chapter's question is about a person: "Where did he go wrong?"

The obvious guess is that a sharper or more visionary leader would have seen the iPhone coming. The chapter's answer runs almost the other way. Lazaridis was exceptionally smart and, at the start, a model scientific thinker. What went wrong wasn't his intelligence; it was the way of thinking he slipped into when the question was whether the market still wanted the product he had built. Grant frames this as a general problem: most of us take pride in our knowledge and in staying true to our beliefs, which pays off in a stable world where conviction is rewarded, but in a fast-changing world we need to spend as much time rethinking as thinking.

# Three professions we slip into

Grant introduces his main tool with a different case. A family friend who was a financial adviser told Stephen Greenspan to invest in a retirement fund outside his employer's plan. Another friend, knowledgeable about investing, warned him the fund was risky. Greenspan weighed the warning against the data. His sister had been in the fund for several years and was pleased; so were several of her friends; the returns weren't spectacular but were consistently in the double digits; the adviser had put in his own money. Greenspan invested nearly a third of his retirement savings and watched it grow 25 percent. Then he lost it all overnight. The fund was Bernie Madoff's Ponzi scheme.

To explain how a careful person ends up there, Grant borrows from his colleague Phil Tetlock. As we think and talk, we slip into the mindsets of three professions. We go into **preacher** mode when our sacred beliefs are in jeopardy and deliver sermons to protect them. We go into **prosecutor** mode when we spot flaws in other people's reasoning and marshal arguments to prove them wrong. We go into **politician** mode when we want to win over an audience and campaign for its approval. The risk is that we get so busy preaching that we're right, prosecuting others who are wrong, and politicking for support that we never rethink our own views.

In Greenspan's decision all three showed up, each through a different relationship. His sister *preached* the merits of the fund. Her confidence led him to *prosecute* the friend who warned him, dismissing the warning as "knee-jerk cynicism." And he was *politicking* when his wish for approval swayed him toward yes: the adviser was a family friend he liked and wanted to please. The twist is that Greenspan is an expert on gullibility who had almost finished writing a book on why people get duped. Knowing the traps didn't keep him out of them. Looking back, he wishes he had analyzed the fund's strategy systematically instead of trusting its results, sought out more perspectives from credible sources, and experimented with smaller amounts over a longer period before betting so much of his savings. "That would have put him in the mode of a scientist."

| Mode       | You slip into it when...        | Changing your mind feels...   |
|------------|---------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Preacher   | sacred beliefs are threatened   | like moral weakness           |
| Prosecutor | others' reasoning looks flawed  | like admitting defeat         |
| Politician | you want an audience's approval | like caving to carrots/sticks |
| Scientist  | you are searching for the truth | like intellectual integrity   |

Read the table by row, and notice that the rows are modes, not personality types: Greenspan occupied three of them in a single decision. The right column shows why only the last row reliably changes course on evidence. For the preacher and the prosecutor, changing your mind is a loss. The politician does change, but in response to rewards and punishments from an audience, not to sharper logic or stronger data. The scientist treats updating as the point. And "scientist" here is a frame of mind, not a job: doubting what you know, being curious about what you don't know, and updating your views based on new data.

# Can scientific thinking be taught?

If scientist mode is something you can slip out of, maybe it's also something you can be trained into. A quartet of European researchers tested this with more than a hundred founders of Italian startups, in technology, retail, furniture, food, health care, leisure, and machinery, most of whom had yet to bring in any revenue. Over four months in Milan, every founder got the same entrepreneurship training: create a business strategy, interview customers, build a minimum viable product, refine a prototype. What they didn't know was that they had been randomly assigned to two groups. One group was also encouraged to see all of it through a scientist's goggles: the strategy is a theory, customer interviews help develop hypotheses, the minimum viable product and prototype are experiments to test those hypotheses, and the job is to measure the results rigorously and decide based on whether the hypotheses are supported.

Over the following year, the control group's startups averaged under $300 in revenue. The scientific-thinking group averaged over $12,000, brought in revenue more than twice as fast, and attracted customers sooner. Grant points to pivoting as the reason: the founders taught to think like scientists pivoted more than twice as often. Grant reports the "rate of pivoting to a new business model" as 49.2 percent for the scientific-thinking group versus 21.1 percent for the control group; the chapter doesn't spell out how the rate was measured. When their hypotheses weren't supported, they knew it was time to rethink. The control founders tended to stay wedded to their original plans, preaching the virtues of their past decisions, prosecuting the alternatives, and politicking by catering to advisers who favored the existing direction.

The experiment changes what "decisiveness" should look like. We celebrate entrepreneurs and leaders for being strong-minded and certain; here the founders who did best held their plans as testable guesses. Grant adds that in tournaments where executives compete to price products, the best strategists are actually slow and unsure: like careful scientists, they take their time so they keep the flexibility to change their minds. And a job title doesn't guarantee the mode: even great scientists slip into preaching. After upending physics, Einstein opposed the quantum revolution.

What makes this study persuasive is the random assignment with otherwise identical training, so the difference can be credited to the scientific framing rather than to who chose it. What limits it is that it's one group of early-stage Italian founders followed for a year, and the chapter reports only the group averages.

# Brilliant, and trapped by it

Back to Mike Lazaridis, because he's the case in which the same person is in scientist mode and then out of it. When he created the BlackBerry, he was thinking like a scientist. Existing wireless email devices had a stylus that was too slow or a keyboard that was too small; people had to clunkily forward work email to other inboxes. He generated hypotheses and sent his engineers to test them. What if people typed with their thumbs instead of their fingers? What if there was a single mailbox synchronized across devices? What if messages were relayed through a server and appeared on the device only after being decrypted?

Grant's contrast is between two roles rather than two periods: as the engineer designing the device he tested hypotheses; as the CEO deciding what the market wanted, he stopped. In the summer of 2007 he took apart the first iPhone and was stunned: "They've put a Mac in this thing." Grant's verdict: "If the BlackBerry's rise was due in large part to his success in scientific thinking as an engineer, its demise was in many ways the result of his failure in rethinking as a CEO." Lazaridis stayed confident that people wanted a wireless device for work email and calls, not a computer in their pocket with apps for entertainment. As early as 1997 he had told an engineer who wanted to add a browser to focus on email; a decade later he was still sure a powerful browser would drain the battery and strain wireless networks. "He didn't test the alternative hypotheses." By 2008 the company was worth over $70 billion, but the BlackBerry was still its only product and still lacked a reliable browser. In 2010, when colleagues pitched encrypted messaging that would also work on competitors' devices, he worried it would make the BlackBerry obsolete; the company abandoned instant messaging, leaving an opening WhatsApp later seized for upwards of $19 billion.

"Intelligence was no cure—it might have been more of a curse." Grant backs this with research. People who score higher on IQ tests are more likely to fall for stereotypes, because they're faster at recognizing patterns. In one study, people good at math were better at analyzing data about a skin-rash treatment. When the same data were labeled as being about gun laws, math skill helped only if the results supported the person's politics; when the pattern clashed with their ideology, math skill became a liability. Liberal math whizzes did worse than their peers at evaluating evidence that gun bans failed, and conservative ones did worse at evaluating evidence that gun bans worked. Two biases drive this: **confirmation bias**, seeing what we expect to see, and **desirability bias**, seeing what we want to see. They don't just block intelligence; they turn it into a tool for defending what we already believe. Smart people are also more prone to the "I'm not biased" bias. Read back onto Lazaridis, this is what his browser argument looks like: an expert's technical reasons (battery drain, network strain) enlisted to defend what he already believed. So being good at thinking can make you worse at rethinking. What protects you is being *actively* open-minded: searching for reasons you might be wrong, not for reasons you must be right.

# Two cycles

Grant's figure sets two loops side by side. They are two different states rather than steps in one process; in Grant's words, when we shift out of scientist mode, "the rethinking cycle breaks down, giving way to an overconfidence cycle." In the Lazaridis story, what holds him in the right-hand loop is pride in his invention.

```text
   RETHINKING CYCLE                  OVERCONFIDENCE CYCLE

   Humility ----> Doubt              Pride ---------> Conviction
      ^             |                  ^                  |
      |             v                  |                  v
   Discovery <--- Curiosity          Validation <---- Confirmation &
                                                      desirability biases
```

Read each loop clockwise from the top left. In the rethinking cycle, humility (knowing what you don't know) opens the door to doubt; doubt makes you curious about what information you're missing; the search produces discoveries; and discoveries show how much is left to learn, which renews the humility. In the overconfidence cycle, pride in what you know breeds conviction instead of doubt; conviction sets off the two biases; the biased search returns validation from the people we want to impress; and validation feeds the pride.

Run Lazaridis through the right-hand loop, using Grant's own mapping. Pride: "Taking pride in his successful invention gave him too much conviction." Conviction: his preference for the keyboard over the touchscreen, a BlackBerry virtue he loved to preach and an Apple vice he was quick to prosecute. Biases and validation: as the stock fell, Grant says, he "got caught up in confirmation bias and desirability bias, and fell victim to validation from fans." Two quotes show it. The 2011 one echoes the fans' validation; the 2012 one shows him seeing what he expected and wanted to see. In 2011 he said of the BlackBerry, "It's an iconic product... It's used by business, it's used by leaders, it's used by celebrities." In 2012, with the iPhone holding a quarter of the global smartphone market, he pointed at a touchscreen phone in a board meeting and said, "I don't get this. The keyboard is one of the reasons they buy BlackBerrys." Like a politician campaigning only to his base, he attended to the keyboard tastes of millions of existing users and neglected the billions of potential users drawn to touchscreens. The loop wasn't his alone. In 2011 an anonymous senior employee wrote an open letter: "We laughed and said they are trying to put a computer on a phone, that it won't work... We are now 3–4 years too late." And when Lazaridis finally did start reimagining the screen and software, some of his engineers didn't want to abandon their past work.

The Italian founders ran the left-hand loop. Treating their strategy as a theory was the humility; each customer interview was a chance for doubt and curiosity; a hypothesis that failed was a discovery, and it led to a pivot rather than a defense. Grant's point is that neither loop is a personality. Lazaridis ran the left loop as an engineer and the right loop as a CEO with a hugely successful product to protect.

Grant limits the claim himself. He doesn't think we should be open-minded in every circumstance; there are situations where preaching, prosecuting, or politicking makes sense. He argues only that most of us would benefit from being in scientist mode more of the time.

# Apple, and who does the rethinking

Grant's last case moves the question from one mind to the people around it. The legend is that Steve Jobs's lone conviction and clarity of vision gave birth to the iPhone. In fact, when a small group of engineers, designers, and marketers pitched turning the iPod into a phone in 2004, Jobs called it "the dumbest idea I've ever heard." He worried about cannibalizing the iPod and hated the cell-phone carriers. Some Apple engineers were already researching the idea, though, and they worked together to persuade Jobs that he didn't know what he didn't know.

How they persuaded him matters. Grant cites research that people who resist change are more open when a vision for change includes continuity: the strategy may evolve, but the identity will endure. So the engineers assured Jobs they weren't trying to turn Apple into a phone company. It would remain a computer company, adding a phone to products it already made; Apple already put twenty thousand songs in your pocket, so why not everything else? After six months of discussion, Jobs became curious enough to give the effort his blessing, and two teams raced to test whether to add calling to the iPod or turn the Mac into a miniature tablet that doubled as a phone. Four years after it launched, the iPhone accounted for half of Apple's revenue. Grant's summary: Jobs's employees had the vision, and "it was their ability to change his mind that really revived Apple."

Set beside the BlackBerry, the Apple story changes who does the rethinking. BlackBerry's engineers put their doubts in an anonymous letter when they were already years behind. Apple's engineers spent six months making doubt safe for Jobs's identity and turning their idea into a test between two teams. Grant also warns against making the BlackBerry story carry too much: when a company collapses, "we can never pinpoint a single cause of its downfall." The Lazaridis story illustrates the modes; it doesn't prove they alone sank the company.

Grant ends by applying the rule to himself. "Good judgment depends on having the skill—and the will—to open our minds. I'm pretty confident that in life, rethinking is an increasingly important habit. Of course, I might be wrong. If I am, I'll be quick to think again."

# What Stays With You

Grant's point is that the biggest threat to good judgment is less a lack of intelligence than the pride and identity we attach to what we already know: Mike Lazaridis thought like a scientist about how to build the BlackBerry and stopped when the question became whether the market still wanted it. The randomly assigned Italian founders and Jobs's engineers show the other side, that treating a belief as a hypothesis can be learned and can come from the people around a leader, and Grant asks for both the ability to test your views and the willingness to let them lose.
