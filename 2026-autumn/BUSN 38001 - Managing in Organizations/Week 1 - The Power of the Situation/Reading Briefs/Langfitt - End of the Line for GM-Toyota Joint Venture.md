# A plant closing that isn't the usual Detroit story

In late March 2010, a car factory in Fremont, California, was a week from closing, and 4,500 people were about to lose their jobs. The factory was New United Motor Manufacturing Inc., NUMMI for short, once run jointly by General Motors and Toyota. On the surface, NPR's Frank Langfitt says, it's "a familiar story about the fall of the American auto industry." His report argues that it isn't.

In the mid-1980s Toyota took over one of GM's worst plants, "a factory known for sex, drugs and defective vehicles," and turned it into one of GM's best "practically overnight." Along the way it even shared its production secrets with GM. "But GM would take another decade and a half to begin seriously implementing those lessons in its own factories." That delay, Langfitt argues, "explains a lot about why it took GM and other Detroit companies so many years to improve the quality of their vehicles."

Both companies needed the deal. GM had to build small cars, and the ones it built "were lousy and lost money." Toyota faced import restrictions from Congress, had to start building cars in the United States, and wanted an American partner who would teach it how to deal with American workers. Toyota settled on the rough bunch in Fremont. When NUMMI opened, Car and Driver greeted the rivals' partnership with the headline "Hell Freezes Over."

The report answers two linked questions. What changed at Fremont to turn the same plant around? And why did GM, which had the lesson in hand, take so long to use it? His contribution is to put the whole arc in one place, from the 1984 takeover to the 2010 closing, through the people who lived it: Fremont workers, the union official who oversaw the plant, the GM managers sent to NUMMI, and researchers who studied the Toyota system. GM declined to be interviewed. The piece is reporting, not a study, and that shapes what it can show; the last section returns to this.

# Same workers, a rope that can stop the line

Before Toyota, the Fremont workforce "was considered the worst workforce in the automobile industry in the United States," says Bruce Lee, who ran the western region of the United Auto Workers (UAW) union and oversaw the plant. "Everything was a fight." Rick Madrid, who built Chevy trucks there, drank on the line: "I'd bring a thermos of screwdrivers with me." Some workers hated management so much they sabotaged cars, putting Coke bottles inside door panels so they would rattle. Absenteeism was so bad, Billy Haggerty recalls, that some mornings managers went across the street to the bar to grab enough people to start the line. Under the union contract, workers "practically had to commit fraud to get fired." GM closed the plant in 1982.

The natural conclusion is that Fremont had bad workers. Then, two years later, GM and Toyota reopened the factory "with—incredibly—most of the same workforce," after first sending some of them to Japan to learn the Toyota way.

The most visible thing Toyota changed was a rule. At old Fremont, the one cardinal rule everyone knew was that the assembly line could never stop. "I saw a guy fall in the pit and they didn't stop the line," Madrid says. Lee: "You saw a problem, you stopped that line: you were fired." So problems rode the line: Monte Carlos with Regal front ends, engines put in backwards, cars without steering wheels or brakes, all fixed later in a yard outside, sometimes with more damage. Toyota organized people into teams of just four or five, who switched jobs every few hours to relieve the monotony, with a team leader who stepped in whenever something went wrong. Along the line hung a thin nylon rope called the andon cord.

```text
 OLD GM FREMONT                          NUMMI
 worker spots a bad bolt                 worker spots a bad bolt
         |                                       |
 line must never stop                    pulls the andon cord
         |                                       |
 car moves on with the defect            team leader comes to the station
         |                                       |
 fixed later in the outside yard,        fixed on the line; if it takes
 sometimes with more damage              too long, the whole line stops
```

Follow Rick Madrid's bolt down both columns. When he trained in Japan, he saw workers stop the line over a single bolt. Under the andon system that is the second step, after the team leader has been called; what struck Madrid was that stopping was allowed at all. Under the old Fremont rule, that bolt would go down the left column: the car keeps moving, and the problem turns up in the yard, where fixing it means taking things apart. With the andon cord it goes down the right column: the first pull only summons the team leader, the team tries to fix it on the line, and the whole line stops only if the fix takes too long. "Fix it now so you don't have to go through all this stuff," Madrid thought. "That's when it dawned on me. We can do it. One bolt. One bolt changed my attitude." The man who had brought a thermos of screwdrivers to work was won over by a system that trusted him to stop production.

The result, according to Jeffrey Liker, author of *The Toyota Way*: on defects per 100 vehicles, "the best measure they use," NUMMI was "one of the best in America," and Toyota cars built in California matched the Corollas coming from Japan "right in the beginning."

This is the article's core evidence, and it works like a natural experiment: the same site and mostly the same people, before and after, with the management system changed. Because the people largely stayed while the defects fell, it moves the explanation for old Fremont's quality from "these workers" to "this system." It doesn't isolate which part of the system mattered most (the teams, the cord, the training in Japan, Toyota's managers), and "most of the same workforce" is not all of it. Something else changed too: the rehired workers had lost their jobs for two years, a difference the article itself raises when it gets to Van Nuys. And the "before" side rests on memories, not a defect count.

# Sixteen commandos and a company that didn't ask

General Motors sent 16 rising stars to help start the plant; two Wall Street reporters dubbed them the "NUMMI commandos." After the launch, they wanted to spread NUMMI's lessons across GM. "We were ready, we were fired (up) and we had the mental condition that said: 'we're going to change the world,'" says Steve Bera. They waited to be deployed, but the company didn't seem to know what to do with them. "Instead of coming back to the 16 of us and saying, 'There's some secret sauce here, what is it? How can we use it to our advantage?' No one ever asked us that question." Frustrated, Bera quit after two decades at GM.

GM did try to copy NUMMI at its Van Nuys plant, 400 miles south. Workers there were skeptical from the start, and Langfitt points to one difference from Fremont: unlike NUMMI's workers, they had never lost their jobs and didn't think they would. "The lack of receptiveness to change was so deep," says Larry Spiegel, one of the commandos who worked on Van Nuys. "There were too many people convinced they didn't need to change." GM had threatened to close the plant, but workers believed it would never happen and stuck with their old ways. Quality at Van Nuys never improved, and GM closed it in 1992. (The Keller chapter that this article supplements follows the Van Nuys attempt in much more detail.)

After Van Nuys, with GM's market share collapsing, executives did try to push the NUMMI system across the company, and the effort ran into GM's structure. Geoff Weller, whose job was to convert GM factory by factory, describes a sprawling, highly decentralized company where "plant managers were king" and ran their factories like fiefdoms. Some were responsive; one asked Weller to leave his factory after his presentation on the NUMMI system. Asked why the CEO wouldn't fire a plant manager who resisted a system producing better cars at lower cost, Weller said: "It's a big company ... and it doesn't work that way." In some plants the union also saw its traditions threatened by Toyota's team concept and refused to change.

Set Van Nuys and the company-wide push beside Fremont, and the first lesson gets a second half. At Fremont the system changed and the people changed with it. Elsewhere in GM the lesson was available, but what it took to adopt it was missing: workers who believed they had to change, plant managers without the power to refuse, and a company that asked its own NUMMI veterans what they had learned. Knowing what worked did not make GM do it. The article doesn't say what finally made the difference, only that over the years GM executives did learn from NUMMI and the lessons "finally caught on." By the early 2000s GM had developed a production model based on Japanese principles and made it standard at every plant. Its quality "eventually improved a lot," though it still lagged the Japanese.

# What fifteen years did and didn't cost

The article's strongest claim is about timing. James Womack, co-author of *The Machine That Changed the World*: "One of the ironies of GM was that at the moment it went bankrupt, it was probably a better company than it had ever been... But it was too late... If you take 30 years to figure it, chances are you're going to get run over. And they got run over." (The article doesn't explain Womack's "30 years"; its own count is fifteen years from NUMMI's opening.) Mark Hogan, a commando who rose to run GM's U.S. small-car division, goes further: if GM had moved in the late 1980s to implement the NUMMI system, "this ever increasing loss in market share would have been stopped," and GM might have avoided bankruptcy.

Langfitt sets his own limit on that claim: "Of course, quality and reliability weren't the reasons GM failed." GM had negotiated UAW contracts so generous that they crippled the company; some at GM first dismissed hybrids like the Prius as a publicity stunt; and the Great Recession destroyed the car market. GM's 2009 bankruptcy was the largest industrial bankruptcy in U.S. history and cost taxpayers more than $50 billion. So Hogan's counterfactual is one insider's judgment, not something the report shows. Langfitt adds a warning from the other side: by 2010 Toyota had problems of its own, which the article doesn't detail but which Toyota executives traced to one of GM's old mistakes, "stressing quantity over quality."

What the evidence supports, then: NUMMI's lessons were visible and on offer from the mid-1980s, GM's own people saw them, and GM took about fifteen years even to begin seriously implementing them, which plausibly slowed its quality improvement. What it doesn't establish: that faster adoption would have saved GM, or exactly which NUMMI practices produced the turnaround. Most of the voices are people who lived the NUMMI story and have reason to see it as vindication, and GM gave no side of its own.

When GM went bankrupt it pulled out of the joint venture, and Toyota didn't want to run the plant alone. NUMMI's last car was a Corolla. Over a quarter century it had turned out nearly 8 million cars and trucks, about 6,000 a week, and Haggerty still spots the Corollas he built running on the road: "It's still kicking. It feels good." Seen with the whole history in view, the closing plant is not a failure of Fremont's workers. It is the end of the clearest demonstration GM ever had that its worst plant's problems came from how the plant was run, a demonstration the company took fifteen years to act on.
