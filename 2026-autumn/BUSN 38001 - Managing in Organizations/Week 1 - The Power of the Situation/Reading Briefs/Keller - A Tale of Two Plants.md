# The plant where workers were not trusted

Keller begins with what American managers believed about the people on their payroll. American companies, she writes, "tend, fundamentally, to mistrust workers," salaried or blue-collar: "if you give them an inch, they'll take a mile." The idea that a worker could stop the line to fix a problem was heresy; wouldn't that mean line stoppages for every whim? Quality followed the same logic. At GM, quality meant *quality control*: adding more inspectors to find defects after the fact, on the assumption, taught to generations of business-school graduates, that high quality meant high cost. In Japanese companies, quality was part of the process, and the workers, not the inspectors, were primarily responsible for it.

GM's plant in Van Nuys, California, which built Firebirds and Camaros, shows what the American assumptions looked like from the floor. Brian Haun, an engineer from GM's central staff, took a job there as a production supervisor. His promised six months of training turned out to be on the job, taught by the very people he was supposed to supervise. They greeted him with hazing: "Here comes a young college kid. Let's get him." He won them over by taking their side. A defective welding robot kept missing welds, and a repair operator, whose contract limited him to repairs, was being made to do the missing welds "one hundred percent of the time." Management told Haun, "You make him do it, or else." He refused and told his workers to stand their ground; they won. "From then on, they broke their backs for me." Notice what he was defending: a narrow contract rule about who does which work, the kind of rule NUMMI would later sweep away. At Van Nuys that rule was the worker's only protection against being made to do someone else's job, and backing it was how Haun earned trust. Haun came away seeing workers who wanted to do their jobs well but were "fighting against unbeatable odds," in a plant where every problem became a confrontation.

The most telling story is about a small bracket welded to the back of the car to hold the sunshade. A man and a woman, one on each side of the line, kept missing it. The car would reach the end of the line and go to the "backyard," where a repairman pulled out the carpet and trim, welded the bracket, and put everything back, sometimes burning the carpet, "and, frankly, the car was never the same." Haun did something rarely done because it was so disruptive: he took the two workers off the line and showed them the repair. The woman said, "You mean to tell me that bracket holds the sunshade?" She had done the job for two years, and nobody had ever told her what she was welding. She turned out to be one of his best operators. Haun, meanwhile, got in trouble with his general supervisor, who was short on people and didn't want anyone pulled off the line.

The natural reading of a plant like this is that the workers are the problem: careless, unmotivated, protected by a union. Keller's reading is that the plant produces that behavior. The workers "operated in a vacuum." They didn't know who their immediate customers were (the repair crew, the next people down the line) or how much their piece mattered to the finished car. "In the plant, you're treated like a no-mind idiot," Haun says. Accountability came from intimidation ("Do your job and your supervisor won't yell at you"), which Keller calls a pretty thin incentive. "Why should they?" put in extra effort, she asks. "And Van Nuys wasn't unique. It was typical." GM's vice president of public relations, John McNulty, stated the philosophy behind it bluntly: the union's mission is "to get the most compensation for the least amount of labor," management's is "to get the most production for the least amount of compensation," and one way to do that is "with better technology." Keller says this philosophy, more than anything else, cost GM its competitive edge: "Control the hourly workers before they can control the company, and replace them with machines whenever possible."

# Same workers, a different system

A few hundred miles north, GM had already given up on a plant. It closed its Fremont, California, factory in 1982 and laid off five thousand people. Fremont was notorious as one of GM's most unmanageable plants: daily absenteeism regularly over 20 percent, beer bottles littering the parking lot, and even the slightest dispute going to the bargaining table. When GM and Toyota planned their joint venture there, NUMMI, Toyota resisted rehiring the old work force. GM insisted. Together they hired from the original worker pool, "avoiding those who were known to be troublemakers," and according to GM's vice president of labor relations, Al Warren, eventually took back all but a handful. "It demonstrated that a work force we thought was unmanageable could be managed."

For anyone who thought the Japanese edge was technology, Keller writes, NUMMI was a revelation. She gives three reasons for its success. First, a well-designed product, the Chevy Nova, a version of the Corolla that Toyota already built and sold in Japan. Second, the understanding that the level of technology was not the main driver of quality or cost; good design and the production system were. Third, the recognition that car-making is a people business, managed on the assumption that workers instinctively want to do a good job. Toyota deliberately avoided brand-new technology so it wasn't training a new work force and a new machine at the same time. When Haun later visited, he was amazed: "they basically used 1950s technology, and they did a heck of a job with it."

The hinge was the deal with the union. Toyota gave the UAW a strong job security clause and a greater say in plant operations. In return, the union agreed to work within Toyota's system, eliminating unproductive job classifications and setting up team assembly. The rest of NUMMI's system was built on that exchange:

- Job classifications went from as many as 183 at some GM plants to 4. Workers were put in teams led by fellow hourly workers trained in Japan, and "if a problem was spotted, the team had permission to stop the line." The team leader acted as mediator and instructor rather than boss, and every member was trained to do the leader's job.
- Hiring was a joint UAW and NUMMI decision, with about thirty-five hours of assessment for each candidate. The system's goals, above all quality, were spelled out in detail.
- Each team standardized its own work (body movements, parts locations, time per task) and then kept improving it, which Toyota calls *kaizen*, and eliminating waste, *muda*. Workers came to use those words as they had once used "line speedup" and "grievance."
- Just-in-time delivery meant keeping only a few hours' or days' supply of any part. That saved space, and it also worked as quality control: with no big stock to cushion defects, a bad part had to be dealt with at once. Old Fremont had devoted 12 percent of its floor space to reworking defective cars; NUMMI used 7 percent, and Toyota's Takaoka plant in Japan 1 percent.

Toyota's seven operating principles began with *kaizen* and *kanban* (the just-in-time system for cutting costs); the other five were about people: developing full human potential, building mutual trust, developing team performance, treating every employee as a manager, and providing a stable livelihood for all employees. Keller notes that nowhere did they mention a search for technological superiority. Behind them sat two premises that were new to GM: the average worker is motivated by a job that enhances his self-worth and earns the respect of other workers, and he is inspired by an employer who values his input.

Read the diagram from the top down. Each row on the left is something NUMMI management gave, and the arrow to the right is what that made safe or possible for workers. The downward arrows mean "depends on," not "causes" or "comes next": each lower row only works if the one above it holds. Just-in-time sits at the bottom because it only works when defects are already rare; with no stockpile, a plant turning out many bad parts would stall.

Run the sunshade bracket through it. At Van Nuys, the woman who missed the bracket had never been told what it held, had no permission to stop the line, and her miss traveled to the backyard. At NUMMI, the same miss would meet a team that had worked out its own standard job, was allowed to stop the line, and worked in a plant with little rework space to absorb a steady stream of mistakes. The top row is what holds the rest up. In a system where improvement means fewer jobs, a worker has every reason not to find the faster way; the job security clause, and the no-layoff practice that followed, removed that reason.

The results came quickly. Only three months after the first cars rolled out, NUMMI's cars were already "receiving 140 ratings—virtually no defects," and GM's surveys regularly ranked the Nova among the company's top three models for quality. One study estimated that the old Fremont plant would have needed about 50 percent more hourly and salaried workers to build the same car with comparable technology, and that better labor relations cut about $750 from the cost of each car.

The salaried side changed too. Stephen Bera, one of sixteen GM executives assigned to NUMMI, said, "I went through a personality change out there... No more politics. No more doing things just for my own personal gain. The issue was, 'What's good for the company?'" Before NUMMI, his motivations had been typical: "Do what is good for you as an individual... for promotions, for bonuses." He says all sixteen went through a similar transformation. On the floor, Rick Madrid, who by his own admission had once carried "a pen and a punch" as an inspector, became a team leader and was sent to Japan: "They put complete trust in me. Because of that, trust is now mutual." His co-worker Santos Martinez "learned a different meaning for the word respect—one that doesn't include fear."

So the first change in understanding: the old view was that Fremont had the worst workers in GM. After NUMMI, most of the same Fremont workforce, under a system that trusted them, gave them job security, and put quality in their hands, was building some of GM's best-rated cars. A large share of the old behavior had been produced by the old system. Keller herself lists the conditions that make this less than a clean experiment. Known troublemakers were screened out; MIT's John Krafcik noted the rehired workers had been laid off for a few years and were eager for jobs; the plant got a well-designed product; and Toyota gave it close attention. She treats these as parts of the equation, not as reasons to dismiss it.

# What GM expected to bring home

GM expected the lesson to be technological. Bera recalls the instruction: "Go to the joint venture and bring back this *magic* that exists in the Toyota production system." Corporate visitors asked the team how the future looked from Fremont. But when the NUMMI managers asked how they would bring their new knowledge back into the corporation, no one could tell them. GM set up a liaison office, produced videotapes and written materials, and ran plant tours. James Womack at MIT reported in 1986 that "plant tours, videotapes and manuals seem to be a poor substitute for hands-on experience," and that managers who had been exposed to the materials showed "a very partial understanding of NUMMI's procedures, and in many cases a defensive conviction that NUMMI techniques can never work in their plant." Toyota's top executives paid NUMMI close attention; GM's executives, one NUMMI manager said, did a "five-minute fly-by."

GM also did little with the people it had trained. Only one of the team, a finance man named Bob Hendry, went directly from NUMMI to run a plant, one of GM's most notorious in England, and he successfully changed its environment. Others left GM or were absorbed into jobs that didn't use what they'd learned; with managers rotating through, at most fifty could be NUMMI-trained by 1992. Keller's diagnosis: GM "wasn't prepared to learn organizational or people-oriented practices. It expected to learn mechanical or technological lessons." What NUMMI taught "touched the heart of what was culturally wrong with GM, and this could not be changed by one small experiment in a California town." She speculates that GM chairman Roger Smith may have wanted only a quick, cheap small car, or expected a lesson more tangible and less threatening to GM's culture.

# Team concept at Van Nuys

From headquarters, the logic of copying looked sound: teams were the most visible difference at NUMMI, and teams could be written into a plan and installed. So GM built its own "team concept" and tried it in other plants, without using its NUMMI-trained executives, in "an Americanized version that mimicked the Japanese wisdom only on the most superficial level." How it introduced the concept at Van Nuys is the chapter's second test. GM pitted Van Nuys against its Norwood plant near Cincinnati, which built the same cars: the more efficient plant would stay open and the other would close. Van Nuys started behind, with an estimated $400-per-car penalty for shipping parts in and cars east.

Plant manager Ernest Schaefer won the union representatives over and sent his people to observe Fremont. Haun watched NUMMI workers change over a press line in two hours ("If they wanted to rush, they could even change it in one hour"). But a field trip couldn't do "the most important thing—to instill mutual trust and respect between workers and management." Four NUMMI-based managers visited Van Nuys for months. One said later, "We tried to act as consultants and preach the gospel of NUMMI... Our approach was hard-sell, and that was the wrong way to convert these guys."

The union's plant chairman, Ray Ruiz, backed the concept as perhaps the plant's only way to survive. The workers' debate was about trust: was this a roundabout way to extract concessions? Union president Peter Beltran called it "the most dangerous scheme ever conjured up by GM to rob workers of their union," and said what he opposed was the plant-against-plant competition; a fair exchange for team concept would be a GM guarantee to keep Van Nuys open for at least ten years. One union member described the mood: "If GM says give us the team concept or we'll close the plant, why are we pretending we have a choice in the matter?" The vote passed narrowly. Then Ruiz was transferred to Detroit, Beltran ran for chairman, won, and set out to undermine the concept, and the joint union-management committees were dissolved. Even Haun thought Beltran was not "completely wrong to say, 'I don't trust those guys'."

What happened to the teams shows why trust was the missing piece. GM billed team concept only as a way to raise efficiency, and it hoped to cut head count at Van Nuys by as much as 25 percent, so workers reasonably read "efficiency" as "layoffs," and Beltran was determined the cuts would not happen. Under Beltran, teams of six were set up with extra leaders hired, while the relief workers who covered absences were kept; Haun went from supervising twenty-three people to twenty-six, and the body shop added at least ten to the payroll. Workers who now had permission to halt the line stopped it so often that quality actually fell and fewer cars went out the door; the chapter doesn't say whether that came from missing training, protest, or both. The same rule that worked at NUMMI, letting workers stop the line, produced the opposite result without the trust and training it depended on.

Both plants got a similar visible structure (teams, team leaders, permission to stop the line), so the table leaves it out. Read across each row for what surrounded that structure. The first three rows are choices GM made differently from NUMMI: the deal it offered, who led the change, and what the change was said to be for. Van Nuys still won the contest. In August 1987 GM closed the sixty-four-year-old Norwood plant and laid off four thousand workers, even though Van Nuys's cost per car was $400 higher. GM's president cited reasons including Norwood's age; Keller suggests the real reason may have been that Norwood's workers had rejected team concept outright. If so, GM rewarded the plant that accepted the form of the concept over the one that built cars more cheaply. In April 1988 Beltran was fired for absenteeism. Keller calls it winning the battle and losing the war: without a guarantee, Van Nuys had won only time, and she predicted it would "almost certainly be closed by the end of the decade."

Her explanation is the chapter's second change in understanding. The first said the Fremont workers weren't the problem; the second says a single piece of NUMMI's system doesn't transfer on its own. GM "once more fell victim to its habit of oversimplification. If putting workers into teams was the way to succeed, then put the workers into teams. But the team structure was only part of a much larger equation that included product, manufacturing system, plant layout, training, and company philosophy." Above all, GM ignored "the need to develop mutual trust between management and workers." Instead, "the message that filtered down to the workers was that team concept was a sneaky maneuver to cut back on workers and, ultimately, to close plants." Pitting plants against each other for survival may have produced a more militant work force, the reverse of what GM intended. And NUMMI's version had started by selecting people with leadership qualities who were open to teams, while most GM plants had work forces in place for twenty or twenty-five years and more resistant to change.

# What GM still had not learned

A NUMMI executive, a veteran GMer, ranked the three most important lessons. First, good people relationships, not advanced technology. Second, the value of a no-layoff policy: at NUMMI it produced loyalty and productivity, while in GM plants without one, workers were suspicious of management's motives. Third, attention to details. Another executive from the first group said, "You have to create the right environment before you can accomplish anything... Contrary to the suspicions of many GM people, the Japanese system wasn't mysterious at all. It was simple and basic." The UAW, which had feared NUMMI was like "putting the fox in the chicken coop," was impressed that when Nova sales were weak, no one got laid off.

At headquarters, though, long-time executives "still could not trust the evidence." GM ran several improvement programs at once with no central way to evaluate them. At one executive meeting, after twenty-five studies of NUMMI and a thousand GM people had gone through it, someone asked whether they could now say what NUMMI's secret was and introduce it to the rest of the company. The debate reached no conclusion, and no one on the Executive Committee seemed to have "the will to institutionalize change." Resistance came from the labor side too. Critics such as Mike Parker and Jane Slaughter called team concept "management by stress," though the UAW's Bruce Lee pointed out they had never set foot inside NUMMI. Keller reads the phrase as a real cultural clash rather than a smear: in the American ideal, a more efficient system should make work less demanding, while under continuous improvement, solving one problem just opens a new, harder one, so the striving never stops.

By the chapter's end, GM was slowly moving NUMMI-trained managers into plants where they could use what they knew, and one of them admitted the early hard sell had backfired: "We were like religious zealots... It's no wonder that people got hostile." The joint venture had a legal cutoff date of 1996 and no plan for its work force. Al Warren called that work force "a reservoir of a process that maybe we don't have anymore."

Back at Van Nuys, the woman who didn't know what her weld held was, in Keller's telling, typical of GM plants, not a special case of bad workers. The chapter's two tests say what she needed: Fremont showed that people like her do good work when they are told, trusted, and secure in their jobs, and the Van Nuys experiment showed that the rules alone, without that security and trust, made things worse.

# What Stays With You

Keller's point is that NUMMI's secret was no secret: give workers job security, responsibility for quality, and the power to stop the line, and most of the same Fremont workforce GM had written off builds some of its best cars with 1950s technology. GM couldn't take that in because it contradicted the mistrust of workers and the faith in technology that GM ran on, so it copied the team structure without the trust, imposed it by threat at Van Nuys, and got the opposite result.
