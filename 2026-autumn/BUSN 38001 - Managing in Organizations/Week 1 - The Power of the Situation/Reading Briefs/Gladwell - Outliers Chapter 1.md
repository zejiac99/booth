# A league that runs on merit

In May 2007 the Medicine Hat Tigers and the Vancouver Giants met for the Memorial Cup, the championship of the best junior hockey league in the world. The players were seventeen to nineteen years old and had been skating since they were barely more than toddlers. Vancouver won, and in the Giants' locker room afterward, under a hand-painted banner reading "Embrace the Struggle," coach Don Hay said, "There isn't one guy that didn't buy in wholeheartedly."

Gladwell starts by giving the system its due. Canadian hockey is a meritocracy. Boys are sorted from the "novice" level, before most of them are in kindergarten. At every age class they are evaluated, the more talented are separated out and groomed, and by their mid-teens the very best reach Major Junior A, the top of the pyramid. You can't buy your way in. It doesn't matter who your father is or how remote your corner of Canada is; if you have ability, the scouts will find you. "Success in hockey is based on *individual merit*—and both of those words are important." Players are judged on their own performance and ability, not on some arbitrary fact.

That is also how we usually explain anyone at the top, and Gladwell says so plainly. When we look at successful people we ask what they are *like*: their personality, their intelligence, their lifestyle, their special talents. The story line in success memoirs is always the self-made man. This chapter opens Outliers, and Gladwell states the book's claim against that story right away: "It's not enough to ask what successful people are like, in other words. It is only by asking where they are *from* that we can unravel the logic behind who succeeds and who doesn't." His image is the tallest oak in a forest. It grew from a hardy acorn, but it also got sunlight no other tree blocked, deep soil, no rabbit chewing its bark as a sapling, and no lumberjack. "This is not a book about tall trees. It's a book about forests."

So what forest produced the Medicine Hat Tigers? Gladwell prints the team's roster (name, position, height, weight, birth date, hometown) and asks the reader to spot what's strange about it.

# Seventeen of twenty-five

In the mid-1980s a Canadian psychologist, Roger Barnsley, was at a Lethbridge Broncos game with his family. His wife, Paula, was reading the program and asked whether he knew when these young men were born. He said they were sixteen to twenty, so the late sixties. "No, no," she said. "What *month*." There were an incredible number of January, February, and March birthdays.

Barnsley, his wife, and a colleague, A. H. Thompson, then gathered birth dates for every player in the Ontario Junior Hockey League. January was the most common birth month, by an overwhelming margin, then February, then March; there were nearly five and a half times as many players born in January as in November. The all-star teams of eleven- and thirteen-year-olds showed the same thing, and so did the National Hockey League. Barnsley's rule of thumb for any elite group of Canadian players: about 40 percent born January to March, 30 percent April to June, 20 percent July to September, and 10 percent October to December. "In all my years in psychology, I have never run into an effect this large," he says. "You don't even need to do any statistical analysis. You just look at it."

On the 2007 Medicine Hat roster, seventeen of twenty-five players were born in January, February, March, or April. Those four months are a third of the year, so if birthdays were spread evenly you would expect about eight. Gladwell retells the Memorial Cup goals with birthdays in place of names ("January 21 charges down the right side of the ice... slides a cross-crease pass back to January 21. He shoots!"), and the championship starts to sound like a ritual for teenage boys born under Capricorn, Aquarius, and Pisces.

At this point the merit story is under pressure but not yet refuted. Maybe something about being born in winter makes a better hockey player. Gladwell's explanation is more mundane than that. In Canada the eligibility cutoff for age-class hockey is January 1. A boy who turns ten on January 2 can be playing alongside one who doesn't turn ten until the end of the year, and before adolescence a twelve-month gap is an enormous difference in physical maturity. When coaches pick the traveling "rep" squad at nine or ten, they naturally see the bigger, more coordinated boys as the talented ones.

Then the selection starts to change the players themselves. The boy picked for a rep squad gets better coaching and better teammates. He plays fifty or seventy-five games a season instead of the twenty played by those left in the "house" league, and he practices two or three times as much. At first his advantage is only that he is a little older. "But by the age of thirteen or fourteen, with the benefit of better coaching and all that extra practice under his belt, he really *is* better," and he is the one who makes Major Junior A and, from there, the big leagues.


Read the diagram as the chain of causes Gladwell describes, starting at the top left and ending at the bottom left, where each pick earns the chance at the next one; because the chain repeats at every level, Barnsley and Gladwell describe it as a loop. The first box is a birth date measured against a rule, not a talent. The coaches at age nine or ten aren't wrong that the boy looks better; they are wrong about *why*. Everything after that is real: the extra games and coaching build real skill, so by the bottom row the scout watching a fourteen-year-old is correctly seeing a better player. That is why the system looks like pure merit from the inside. Barnsley names the three conditions that make the loop run: **selection** (deciding early who is good), **streaming** (separating the "talented" from the "untalented"), and **differentiated experience** (giving the "talented" a better experience). Wherever all three happen, a big advantage goes to the small group born closest to the cutoff.

That gives a test. If the cause is the cutoff and not something about winter babies, the peak should move whenever the cutoff moves, and the skew should weaken where there is less early sorting. Gladwell's cases line up that way:

| System                    | Cutoff | What the chapter reports               |
|---------------------------|--------|----------------------------------------|
| Canadian hockey           | Jan 1  | ~40% of elite Jan-Mar, ~10% Oct-Dec    |
| MLB, U.S.-born, 2005      | Jul 31 | 505 major leaguers born Aug vs 313 Jul |
| English soccer, 1990s     | Sep 1  | 288 born Sep-Nov vs 136 Jun-Aug        |
| Junior soccer, old rule   | Aug 1  | 135 born Aug-Oct vs 22 May-Jul         |
| U.S. football, basketball | n/a    | less early sorting, so less skew       |

Read each row on its own; the measures differ from row to row (shares, single months, three-month counts), so the rows aren't comparable with each other. Within each row with a number, the dominant birth months are the ones just after that system's own cutoff: August for a July 31 cutoff, autumn for September 1. Astrology can't explain a peak that follows an administrative date around the calendar. The junior soccer row uses the old August 1 cutoff; international junior soccer has since moved to January 1, and the 2007 Czech junior national soccer team shows the result: of twenty-one players, none born in July, October, November, or December, and one each in August and September. Under a January 1 cutoff, those empty months are the ones just before the cutoff, the youngest in each age group. The last row points the other way: American football and basketball don't select, stream, and differentiate as dramatically, so a child who is a bit behind physically can still play as much as older peers, and the pattern is weaker.

# The same loop in school

Hockey and soccer involve a select few, but Gladwell argues the same bias shows up where the stakes are higher. Parents of a child born late in the year may think about holding the child back from kindergarten, yet most assume the disadvantage eventually goes away. "*But it doesn't.*"

Economists Kelly Bedard and Elizabeth Dhuey compared scores on TIMSS, the international math and science tests, with month of birth. Among fourth graders, the oldest children scored between four and twelve percentile points higher than the youngest. Two intellectually equivalent fourth graders at opposite ends of the cutoff could land in the eightieth and the sixty-eighth percentile, which is the difference between qualifying for a gifted program and not. Dhuey describes the same loop as in hockey: in kindergarten and first grade "the teachers are confusing maturity with ability," put the older kids in advanced reading and math groups, where they learn better skills, and the next year they do even better, and the next year better again. When Bedard and Dhuey looked at four-year colleges in the United States, students in the relatively youngest group in their class were underrepresented by about 11.6 percent. "It's outlandish," Dhuey says, "that our arbitrary choice of cutoff dates is causing these long-lasting effects, and no one seems to care about them."

The one country where Dhuey doesn't see the pattern is Denmark, which has a national policy of no ability grouping until age ten, by which point the maturity differences have evened out. Denmark plays the same role as American football: it's the case where the early sorting step is missing, and the effect goes with it. That is what turns a curious birthday pattern into a claim about how the system works. The date only matters because the system acts on early, noisy differences and then feeds them.

It helps to be clear about what kind of evidence this is. The chapter's numbers are birth-month distributions across whole teams and leagues, plus test scores and college attendance. They show that the system tilts the odds for whole groups; they don't show that any particular player's career was decided by his birthday. The rep-squad loop is Barnsley's and Gladwell's explanation of those distributions, and the chapter presents it by describing how the system works rather than through a study that follows individual players over time.

# What the hockey story says about success

Now Gladwell states the revised view. Our notion that "it is the best and the brightest who effortlessly rise to the top is much too simplistic." He doesn't take away the players' ability: "Yes, the hockey players who make it to the professional level are more talented than you or me. But they also got a big head start, an opportunity that they neither deserved nor earned." The sociologist Robert Merton had a name for this loop, the "Matthew Effect," after the verse in Matthew's Gospel: "For unto everyone that hath shall be given." It's the best students who get the best teaching and the most attention, and the biggest nine- and ten-year-olds who get the most coaching and practice. Sociologists call the result "accumulative advantage": selection, streaming, and differentiated experience are how it accumulates. The professional player "didn't start out an outlier. He started out just a little bit better."

Put the change side by side. The merit view said: the players at the top are the most talented boys in Canada, and the system found them. The chapter's view says: the players at the top really are better now, but a large part of that superiority was built by the system, starting from an age difference that a calendar date created and that coaches took for ability.

Gladwell draws a second implication, about waste. We think starting all-star leagues and gifted programs early is the best way to make sure no talent slips through the cracks. Look again at the Czech soccer roster, with its empty late-year months: "*The talent of essentially half of the Czech athletic population has been squandered.*" Nor can an athletic Czech boy born late in the year escape to hockey. On the 2007 Czech junior hockey team that finished fifth at the world championships, only two of twenty-two players were born in the last quarter of the year. The waste argument rests on the premise the Denmark case suggests: that the late-born boys' early disadvantage was only maturity, which would have evened out if they had been kept in the game.

Because we "so profoundly personalize success," Gladwell writes, we miss chances to lift others onto the top rung, make rules that frustrate achievement, write people off as failures too early, and become passive about how large a role society plays in who makes it. His remedy is administrative and cheap. Hockey could run two or three leagues divided by birth month and pick all-star teams later; if late-born athletes had a fair chance, the Czech and Canadian national teams would have twice as many athletes to choose from. Schools could put January-to-April, May-to-August, and September-to-December births in separate classes, so children compete against others of the same maturity. That "would level the playing field for those who—through no fault of their own—have been dealt a big disadvantage." We don't do it, he says, "because we cling to the idea that success is a simple function of individual merit."

# Back at the Memorial Cup

Before the final, Gord Wasden, the father of Medicine Hat player Scott Wasden, stood by the ice and talked about his son. When Scott was four and five, he said, he would shove a hockey stick into his little brother's hand and they would play on the kitchen floor from morning till night. Scott always had a passion for the game, always made the top rep teams, and was "always a bigger kid for his age," strong, a scorer, a captain. "He's had to work very hard for whatever he's got."

Gladwell grants all of it: "Those were the ingredients of success at the highest level: passion, talent, and hard work. But there was another element." Scott Wasden was born on January 4. "If the eligibility date for Canadian hockey were later in the year, he might have been watching the Memorial Cup championship from the stands instead of playing on the ice."

The opening scene now reads differently. The Giants' banner, "Embrace the Struggle," and their coach's pride that every player bought in wholeheartedly were not false. Neither is Gord Wasden's pride in his son's work. What has changed is how much the word "merit" can be asked to carry. The father's own description, "a bigger kid for his age," is evidence for both stories at once: the one he tells about effort and passion, and the one the roster tells about January.

# What Stays With You

Gladwell wants you to hold two things at once: the young men on the Memorial Cup ice really are better than their peers, and an arbitrary rule, a January 1 cutoff, helped make them better by turning a few months of maturity into selection, coaching, and practice that compounded year after year. Because we credit the result to individual merit alone, we stop seeing the rules we wrote, so, on Gladwell's argument, we keep losing late-born talent and feel no reason to change the rules.
