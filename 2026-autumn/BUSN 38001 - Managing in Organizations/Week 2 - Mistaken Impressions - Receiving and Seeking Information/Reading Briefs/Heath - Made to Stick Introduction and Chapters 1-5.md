# Popcorn and stolen kidneys

The book opens with a story you have probably heard. A business traveler accepts a drink from an attractive stranger, blacks out, and wakes up in a hotel bathtub full of ice, with a note telling him to call 911. The operator asks him to feel for a tube in his lower back. One of his kidneys has been harvested. The Kidney Heist is false, yet it circulates in hundreds of versions that all keep the same three elements: the drugged drink, the ice-filled bathtub, the kidney-theft punch line. You could retell it an hour from now almost perfectly. Then the Heaths quote a nonprofit paper: "Comprehensive community building naturally lends itself to a return-on-investment rationale that can be modeled, drawing on existing practice." Try retelling that.

The comparison is rigged, and the authors admit it, but they ask which end of that spectrum your workplace communication sits nearer. The natural explanation is that some ideas are born interesting and others are born dull. The Heaths reject it in one line: "this is a nurture book." Their question is whether a *true, worthwhile* idea can be made to circulate as well as a false one. An idea "sticks" when it is understood, remembered, and changes what people think or do. The failures they have in mind are ordinary: a manager unveils a new strategy to staff who nod enthusiastically, and the next day the frontline employees are cheerfully carrying out the old one.

Their first case has none of the Kidney Heist's advantages. The Center for Science in the Public Interest (CSPI), a nutrition nonprofit, had movie popcorn analyzed and found that a typical medium bag held 37 grams of saturated fat, against a USDA recommendation of no more than 20 a day, because theaters popped it in coconut oil. Art Silverman, whose job was to tell the public, saw that almost nobody knows what "37 grams of saturated fat" means. Is it bad like a cookie or bad like a cigarette? "Saturated fat has zero appeal," he said. "It's dry, it's academic, who cares?" So at a press conference in September 1992, CSPI said that a medium "butter" popcorn "contains more artery-clogging fat than a bacon-and-eggs breakfast, a Big Mac and fries for lunch, and a steak dinner with all the trimmings—combined!" and laid that day's food out on a table for the cameras. The story ran on CBS, NBC, ABC, and CNN, sales plunged, and most of the biggest theater chains stopped using coconut oil.

The standard communication advice (make eye contact, tell 'em what you're going to tell 'em, know your audience, repeat yourself) wouldn't have helped Silverman. Most of it concerns delivery, structure, or knowing the audience, and none of it says how to design the message itself. Silverman knew his audience. What he needed was a better message.

Then the authors add a case that makes the question uncomfortable. In the 1960s and 1970s, rumors spread about sadists hiding razor blades in Halloween apples. By 1985 an ABC News poll found that 60 percent of parents worried. That year, sociologists who studied every reported Halloween incident since 1958 found no case of a stranger causing a child life-threatening harm by tampering with candy. California and New Jersey even passed special penalties for it. The Heaths call this scare the "evil twin" of the popcorn story. Both reveal an unexpected danger in a common activity, ask for a simple action, carry a vivid concrete image (a razor in an apple, a table of greasy food), and raise an emotion (fear, disgust). These traits preview four of the book's principles. The pairing also carries an implication the authors leave mostly implicit: the traits work for false ideas as well as true ones. The Heaths' own interest runs the other way. Some ideas get these traits by luck, but "we can genetically engineer our players."

They don't claim a formula. The traits are like a basketball player's height and speed: great players have some of them, and having all of them guarantees nothing, but if you're choosing strangers for a pickup game you bet on the seven-footer. The six traits are simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, and stories, compressed into the acronym SUCCESs. This week's reading covers the first five. Stories, the sixth, gets Chapter 6.

# The tune only the tapper can hear

If the principles sound like common sense, why is "our life filled with more process memos than proverbs?" The Heaths' answer is a villain, which they capitalize to give it drama: the **Curse of Knowledge**.

In 1990 Elizabeth Newton earned a Stanford Ph.D. by studying a simple game. "Tappers" picked a well-known song, such as "Happy Birthday to You," and knocked out its rhythm on a table; "listeners" had to name it. Of 120 songs tapped, listeners named 3, or 2.5 percent. The tappers had predicted they'd be understood half the time. They were getting through one time in forty and thought it was one time in two.

A tapper can't stop hearing the tune. The listener hears disconnected knocks, "like a kind of bizarre Morse Code," and the tapper can't believe the listener is struggling. "Once we know something, we find it hard to imagine what it was like not to know it." Effort and goodwill don't fix that. The tappers weren't careless; they had been handed the answer and couldn't imagine lacking it.

The Heaths carry the experiment into organizations, where the tappers are CEOs, teachers, and marketers and the listeners are frontline employees, students, and customers. "When a CEO discusses 'unlocking shareholder value,' there is a tune playing in her head that the employees can't hear." She may have thirty years in business, and she can't unlearn them. So there are only two reliable ways to beat the curse: "The first is not to learn anything. The second is to take your ideas and transform them." The principles are tools for the second.

To show what transforming means, the authors run two lines through their checklist: a CEO telling staff to "maximize shareholder value," and John F. Kennedy's 1961 goal to "put a man on the moon and return him safely by the end of the decade."

| Principle     | "Maximize shareholder value" | "Man on the moon"            |
|---------------|------------------------------|------------------------------|
| Simple        | Short, but not useful        | Yes                          |
| Unexpected    | No                           | Yes                          |
| Concrete      | "Not at all"                 | "Amazingly so"               |
| Credible      | Only via the CEO's position  | Sci-fi goal, credible source |
| Emotional     | "Um, no"                     | Yes                          |
| Story (Ch. 6) | No                           | "In miniature"               |

The quoted cells are the authors' own verdicts. Read down the CEO's column and nothing passes outright. On Simple the line is short but lacks "the useful simplicity of a proverb," a distinction the next chapter develops. On Credible, read carefully: both lines lean on the speaker. The CEO's is credible "only in the sense that it's coming from the mouth of the CEO," and Kennedy's goal "seemed like science fiction" but came from a credible source. The real difference lies in the other rows. The authors show what Kennedy avoided by translating his goal into corporate language: "Our mission is to become the international leader in the space industry through maximum team-centered innovation and strategically targeted aerospace initiatives." Both men knew far more than their listeners; the moon mission "was a classic case of a communicator's dodging the Curse of Knowledge." For now the checkmarks are only labels. The next five chapters work out what each row demands.

Does transforming an idea take a creative genius? A 1999 Israeli study suggests not. Researchers found that 89 percent of 200 award-winning and finalist ads fit six basic templates, most of them built on unexpectedness, while only 2 percent of 200 non-winners did. "Highly creative ads are more predictable than uncreative ones." Novices given two hours of template training then produced ads rated 50 percent more creative than an untrained group's, while novices trained in free-association brainstorming did no better than the untrained group. To the objection that a checklist is confining, the authors reply: "You want to invent new ideas, not new rules."

# One North Star: finding the core

The first principle starts with the Army. Every operation comes with detailed plans cascading down the ranks, yet, as Colonel Tom Kolditz of West Point says, "No plan survives contact with the enemy." So in the 1980s the Army added **Commander's Intent**: a crisp, plain-talk statement at the top of every order that names the plan's goal. For a battalion it might be to clear Hill 4305 "so we can protect the flank of Third Brigade as they pass through the lines." Kolditz: "You can lose the ability to execute the original plan, but you never lose the responsibility of executing the intent."

That is what the Heaths mean by simple. They don't mean dumbing down or sound bites; they mean finding the core of the idea. Cutting what's irrelevant is easy. "The hard part is weeding out ideas that may be really important but just aren't *the most important* idea." The value of the intent comes from its singularity: "You can't have five North Stars." The authors condense their own book the same way: "Step 1 is to find the core, and Step 2 is to translate the core using the SUCCESs checklist." The first half of this chapter is Step 1. From the point where the chapter turns to sharing the core, everything is Step 2.

Southwest Airlines shows a core at work. Herb Kelleher, its longest-serving CEO, said he could teach the secret of running the airline in thirty seconds: "We are THE low-fare airline." His example: Tracy from marketing reports that passengers on the Houston-to-Las Vegas flight might enjoy a chicken Caesar salad. "Tracy, will adding that chicken Caesar salad make us THE low-fare airline from Houston to Las Vegas? Because if it doesn't help us become the unchallenged low-fare airline, we're not serving any damn chicken salad." The core doesn't make the decision; it lets a manager make it as Kelleher would, without Kelleher in the room. Secondary values can sit in a ring around it. Southwest's is "Have fun at work," so joking about a flight attendant's birthday over the P.A. is fine, but throwing confetti isn't, because cleanup time means higher fares.

Choosing the core is painful for smart people, who see nuance and are tempted to linger in it. Journalists call the failure "burying the lead." The Heaths call the cure *forced prioritization*. In Bill Clinton's 1992 campaign, one of three phrases James Carville wrote on a whiteboard for the campaign workers was "It's the economy, stupid," the "stupid" aimed at them. When Clinton balked at dropping balanced budgets, an issue he'd talked about for two years and one Ross Perot was now getting attention for, his advisers told him: "There has to be message triage. If you say three things, you don't say anything."

A core matters to the *listener*, too. The economist L. J. Savage proposed a "sure-thing principle": if you'd choose the same thing whichever way an uncertain event turns out, you should choose it now. Amos Tversky and Eldar Shafir found people violating it. Students offered a cheap Hawaii vacation mostly took it whether they'd been told they passed an important exam (57 percent) or failed it (54 percent). But most of those who didn't yet know (61 percent) paid five dollars to wait two days. Irrelevant uncertainty paralyzed them. The people we address decide under exactly this kind of uncertainty, and a core helps by "reminding them of what's important," as "THE low-fare airline" did for the chicken salad.

Finding a core, though, "isn't synonymous with communicating the core." The *Daily Record* of Dunn, North Carolina, a town of 14,000, has a community penetration of 112 percent, the highest in the country. (Either commuters buy it or some households buy two.) Its founder, Hoover Adams, believed papers should be relentlessly local, which nearly every small-paper publisher would agree with and few papers practice. Adams made it stick with his staff. He said he would happily hire two more typesetters and add two more pages to every edition if reporters could find the names to fill them. Asked why the paper succeeded, he said, "Names, names, and names." No staffer can misunderstand that, and it carries a forced priority: names over costs. Adams couldn't be in the countless small decisions across close to 20,000 issues, but by finding the core and sharing it, the authors write, "*he has made himself everywhere.*"

"Names, names, and names" shows the second half of simplicity: simple messages are core *and compact*. Compactness alone is cheap ("Goats like sprouts" is compact and useless). Here the Curse of Knowledge returns. Experts, fascinated by nuance, forget what it's like not to know, so a compact version looks to them like dumbing down rather than what a newcomer needs. The authors' model is the proverb, which Cervantes called "short sentences drawn from long experience": compact, and core. Like "A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush," Adams's phrase guides individual decisions in a community with shared standards: a *Daily Record* photographer choosing between a boring committee meeting and a sunset over the park should shoot the committee meeting.

How do you pack a lot of meaning into a compact message? Use what's already in the listener's head. The letters "J FKFB INAT OUP SNA SAI RS" are hard to recall; regrouped as "JFK FBI NATO UPS NASA IRS," they're easy, because now each group is a flag on a concept you already have. The authors call such stored concepts **schemas**, a term they carry through the book. List a pomelo's attributes (largest citrus fruit, thick soft rind, light yellow to coral pink flesh), and the listener has to build something new. Say it's "basically a supersized grapefruit with a very thick and soft rind," and the listener enlarges the grapefruit already in their head and can even guess that pomelo juice would blend with orange juice.

Experts resist this because schemas cost accuracy. The solar-system model of the atom is wrong, and teaching it to a sixth grader feels like trading truth for accessibility, though the authors call it "a somewhat slower route to the 'real truth.'" In many cases the choice is false: "If a message can't be used to make predictions or decisions, it is without value, no matter how accurate or comprehensive it is." Here the CEO's line returns. Kelleher *could* have told a flight attendant her goal was to "maximize shareholder value," which is in some sense more accurate and complete than "THE low-fare airline." But it doesn't help her decide about the chicken salad. "Accuracy to the point of uselessness is a symptom of the Curse of Knowledge." To a CEO, maximizing shareholder value may be a useful rule; to a flight attendant, it isn't.

Analogies borrow schemas, and the best ones are *generative*: they let people work out what to do in situations nobody anticipated. Disney calls its employees "cast members": they audition for roles, they're onstage in the park, uniforms are costumes. From that alone you might guess that a cast member won't take a break in costume in public. Subway's "sandwich artists" is the evil twin. The defining trait of an artist is individual expression, the last thing Subway wants at the counter.

By the end of the chapter, "simple" means far more than short and easy. It means a ruthlessly chosen priority, expressed compactly through schemas the audience already has, so that people far from the speaker can make the decisions the speaker would make.

# Breaking the guessing machine on purpose

A core can be right and still be ignored. The second principle asks how you get attention and how you keep it.

The most basic way to get attention is to break a pattern, since we tune out anything constant. A flight attendant named Karen Wood got applause for a safety announcement by telling passengers that "there might be fifty ways to leave your lover, but there are only six ways to leave this aircraft." She could have broken the pattern just by stopping mid-sentence, but her jokes also held attention.

An Ad Council spot shows a pattern break that serves the message. A family drives through the suburbs in a new "Enclave" minivan while a voice-over lists its features, until a speeding car broadsides it. The screen reads "Didn't see that coming?" then "No one ever does," then "Buckle up. . .Always." The ad breaks two schemas: car commercials where nobody dies, and neighborhood trips that end safely. The Heaths describe schemas here as **guessing machines**. They predict what will happen so we know how to act, and surprise is what we feel when one fails. It stops us so that we notice the failure and repair the machine.

Surprise alone, though, is not enough. A 2000 Super Bowl ad showed wolves attacking a marching band, and the authors still have no idea what it advertised. Good surprise is "postdictable": you wouldn't have predicted it, but it makes sense afterward. The rule is to "break someone's guessing machine and then fix it," aiming at the part of the machine that relates to your core.

This is where the second principle depends on the first. To local reporters, "Names, names, and names" sounded like common sense; their schema was "Try to emphasize local angles when you can." Adams broke it with the extra typesetters and replaced it with "Names come before everything else, even my own profitability." Kelleher did the same with the chicken salad, turning "We want to please our customers in a low-cost way" into a low-cost airline "even if it means intentionally disregarding some of our customers' preferences." In both cases the surprise was the part of the core that common sense would otherwise smooth away. Hence the Heaths' process. Find the core. Ask what is counterintuitive about it, which is to say why it isn't already happening. Then break the audience's guessing machine along that dimension and help them fix it. "Common sense is the enemy of sticky messages," because messages that sound like common sense "float gently in one ear and out the other," and what sounds like common sense often isn't.

Nordstrom has to turn new hires from cost-minded retail jobs ("Get customers in and out the door as fast as possible, and try to smile") into service zealots. Telling them the goal is "the best customer service in the industry" wouldn't do it; any department store could say that. Instead, stories circulate about "Nordies" who gift-wrapped something a customer had bought at Macy's, warmed customers' cars in winter, and refunded money for tire chains, which Nordstrom doesn't sell. The same stories told at a 7-Eleven would be even more surprising and would be destructive. Their value comes from the fit between the surprise and the company's goals.

The cleanest example is Nora Ephron's first high school journalism class. The teacher read out facts: the whole faculty would travel to Sacramento next Thursday for a colloquium, with speakers including Margaret Mead and Governor Pat Brown. The students wrote leads that led with the famous names. The teacher said, "The lead to the story is '*There will be no school next Thursday*.'" Ephron: "In that instant I realized that journalism was not just about regurgitating the facts but about figuring out the point." The teacher knew how the students' schema was defective, had them commit to it publicly, then pulled the rug out with a surprise that fixed it.

Surprise is quick, though, and some messages take a semester. The psychologist Robert Cialdini found that the best science writing for lay readers opened with a mystery. One astronomer asked why three acclaimed teams had concluded that Saturn's rings were gas, dust, and ice crystals respectively; twenty pages later the answer was ice-covered dust, and Cialdini, who doesn't care about dust, had been turning pages "like a speed-reader." "The *Aha!* experience is much more satisfying when it is preceded by the *Huh?* experience." The economist George Loewenstein supplies the theory: curiosity is the discomfort of noticing a gap in our knowledge. So you have to *open* gaps before you close them. The natural habit is to lay out the facts in order, because the speaker knows them and that feels like the job. In one of the book's "Idea Clinics" (before-and-after rewrites of messages), a theater's fund-raiser stops reporting that a phone campaign got almost 20 percent response "which we consider a success." Instead she opens with a question: why do people under thirty-five, 40 percent of the audience, give only 10 percent of donations? She says a 10 percent response would have counted as success and holds back the result. The improvement, the authors say, "is driven by structure, not content." Stop asking "What information do I need to convey?" and ask "What questions do I want my audience to ask?"

Two things block a gap. One is overconfidence: people asked to solve their university's parking problem missed more than 70 percent of the best solutions but believed they'd found 75 percent. Making people commit to a prediction, as Ephron's teacher did, or letting them see disagreement, exposes the gap. The other is knowing too little. In the 1960s, when ABC needed viewers in Texas to care about Michigan versus Ohio State, the twenty-nine-year-old Roone Arledge proposed showing the campus, the crowd, and what the game meant to both schools before kickoff. He supplied enough context to turn an abyss into a gap.

The chapter ends with two goals that combine everything. After World War II, Sony's Masaru Ibuka set his engineers on a transistor radio Bell Labs thought impossible, and framed it as a "pocketable radio," when radios were furniture and radio factories employed cabinetmakers. The TR-55 shipped in 1957 and sold 1.5 million units. Kennedy's moon goal broke the same kind of guessing machine. Both surprised, both gave a sudden glimpse of how the world might unfold and why, and both opened a gap big enough to drive years of work but not so big it paralyzed: no "man on Mercury," no "implantable radio." Any engineer hearing the moon speech, the authors write, "must have begun brainstorming immediately." The "Unexpected? Yes" in the table now has content. The surprise sat in the core itself, and it left a gap people could start working on.

# The language everyone already speaks

The natural view of concreteness is that examples are for beginners. The Heaths half agree ("Abstraction is the luxury of the expert") and then spend the chapter showing that experts need concreteness too.

Aesop's fox, unable to reach the grapes, walks off saying, "I am sure they are sour," and "sour grapes" has lasted 2,500 years. The truth it carries survived because it was *encoded* in a fox and some grapes. As *Aesop's Helpful Suggestions* ("Don't be such a bitter jerk when you fail"), it would likely have had a shorter life. "Language is often abstract, but *life* is not abstract." Abstraction makes an idea harder to understand, harder to remember, and harder to coordinate around, because different people read it differently.

Concrete means something you can examine with your senses. A V8 engine is concrete; "high-performance" is abstract. Usually concreteness comes down to "specific people doing specific things": a Nordie warming a customer's car, not "world-class customer service." Concreteness is also what abstraction is built on. A 1993 study found that teachers in Japan and Taiwan often taught math through concrete situations ("You had 100 yen but then you bought a notebook for 70 yen"), in 61 percent of lessons against 31 percent in the United States. Teaching an abstract principle without such foundations "is like trying to start a house by building a roof in the air."

Concrete ideas are also easier to remember. The Heaths' model is Velcro: the more hooks an idea has (sights, sounds, feelings, places), the better it clings. At Georgia State, introductory accounting students spent a semester as the informal accountant for two imaginary sophomores launching a gadget. Fixed and variable costs were discovered rather than defined, and when the pair bounced a check despite record sales, the difference between profit and cash flow became a crisis to solve. About two years later, in the next accounting course, C-average students who had done the case scored about twelve points higher on the first exam. And after Martin Luther King, Jr., was assassinated in 1968, Jane Elliott, a third-grade teacher in all-white Riceville, Iowa, couldn't explain his death to her students. The next day she told them brown-eyed children were superior, made blue-eyed children wear collars, and reversed the groups the following day. Even their schoolwork changed: blue-eyed children took 5.5 minutes on a phonics drill when collared and 2.5 when on top. Studies ten and twenty years later found her students significantly less prejudiced than their peers. She had turned prejudice from a fact to learn into an experience with countless hooks.

If concreteness works so well, why do we drift away from it? Because what separates an expert from a novice is abstract thinking, and experts want to talk at the level where they see patterns. The researcher Beth Bechky studied a firm that built chip-making machinery. When manufacturing workers found a part that didn't fit, the engineers went back to their *drawings* and made them "increasingly elaborate," which made things worse. The workers were thinking, *Why don't you just come down to the factory floor and show me where the part should go?* The fix, the authors say, isn't to meet in the middle. The engineers should change, because the physical machine is the one language everyone speaks fluently. This isn't dumbing down, since the problems were complex and needed smart answers. It means finding a "universal language," and "inevitably, that universal language will be concrete."

The Nature Conservancy (TNC) shows the pull toward abstraction in an organization whose *strategy* had to become more abstract. For decades it protected land by buying it, "bucks and acres," which donors loved for its "results you could walk around on." In 2002, TNC California found 40 percent of the state worth protecting, far too much to buy, and shifted to easements, policy change, and marine protection. Donors can't walk around on a regulation, and acreage was the wrong unit anyway: two million acres is unimaginable, and a small plot can matter more than a huge one. So TNC set a goal of protecting fifty "landscapes" over ten years and gave them names. Unglamorous brown hills east of Silicon Valley became the Mount Hamilton Wilderness, and suddenly local groups and a major foundation grant were behind them. TNC staff laugh when they see other groups' documents citing the Mount Hamilton Wilderness: "You know we made that up." The context and the solutions had grown more ambiguous, but the *messages* could not be allowed to.

Experts need concreteness to coordinate with each other, too. When Boeing launched the 727 in the 1960s, the plane had to seat 131 passengers, fly nonstop from Miami to New York, and land on La Guardia's Runway 4-22, which was too short for any existing passenger jet. "Imagine how much harder it would have been to build a 727 whose goal was to be 'the best passenger plane in the world.'" Concreteness also brings expertise to bear. In 1987 Jerry Kaplan arrived at Kleiner Perkins with no business plan, slides, or prototype, described a pen-operated notebook computer, and tossed his plain leather portfolio onto the table: "Gentlemen, here is a model of the next step in the computer revolution." The partners began debating among themselves how much memory could fit in something that size. Their investment valued his nonexistent company at $4.5 million. The portfolio gave experts shared ground to think on.

One Clinic shows concreteness trading against the next two principles. A health nonprofit explains oral rehydration therapy in careful scientific prose. James Grant, the longtime head of UNICEF, instead carried a packet of salt and sugar to meetings with prime ministers and asked, "Do you know that this costs less than a cup of tea and it can save hundreds of thousands of children's lives in your country?" Grant gave up the statistics that lend the scientific version its credibility. He could afford to, the authors say, because nobody questioned the facts from UNICEF's director, so he fought the motivational battle instead. Belief and motivation are the next two chapters.

Concreteness helps the communicator decide, too. Hamburger Helper's brand manager, Melissa Studzinski, inherited three huge binders of data she called the "death binders." Visiting customers' homes taught her more. Seeing a mother cooking with a toddler on her hip made "convenience" real, and the visits showed that families valued predictability while the brand offered more than thirty flavors mothers struggled to find. Cutting the line saved money and, with other changes, helped lift sales 11 percent.

The Heaths call concreteness perhaps the easiest principle and possibly the most effective. The only barrier is forgetfulness: "We're the engineers who keep flipping back to our drawings." The chapter began with concreteness looking like a concession to beginners. It ends with concreteness as the ground where experts, too, understand, remember, and coordinate.

# When nobody takes your word for it

In the early 1980s, Barry Marshall and Robin Warren, two researchers in Perth, Australia, found that ulcers are caused by bacteria, later named *H. pylori*, which meant ulcers could be cured with antibiotics. Nobody believed them. Common sense said nothing could survive stomach acid. The source looked wrong: a hospital pathologist and a thirty-year-old internist in training. The place looked wrong: "a medical researcher in Perth is like a physicist from Mississippi." And skeptics had a fair point: the evidence showed correlation, not causation. In 1984 Marshall drank a glass containing about a billion *H. pylori*, developed gastritis within days, and cured himself. Critics still quibbled, but the demonstration "gave a second wind to supporters of the bacteria theory," and further research piled up. The National Institutes of Health endorsed antibiotics in 1994, and Marshall and Warren won the Nobel Prize in 2005. "So why did Marshall have to poison himself to get people to believe him?"

The deepest sources of belief, such as family, experience, and faith, are out of a communicator's reach. What's left falls into three wells.

| Well     | What it draws on                       | Example               |
|----------|----------------------------------------|-----------------------|
| External | Authorities; antiauthorities           | Pam Laffin            |
| Internal | Core details; statistics; Sinatra Test | 5,000 BBs in a bucket |
| Audience | Testable credentials                   | "Where's the beef?"   |

Read the rows from top to bottom as moving the source of belief closer to the listener: someone else vouches for the idea, then the idea vouches for itself, then the listener checks it. The chapter doesn't rank the wells strictly. It argues that the obvious sources, authority and hard numbers, "aren't always the best," and that the audience "may be the most powerful source of all."

The external well is the familiar one, and it's easy to fake: a 1999 e-mail warning about flesh-eating bacteria in bananas cited the "Manheim Research Institute." Most of us lack celebrity or expert endorsements, and the Heaths' alternative is the **antiauthority**. Massachusetts built antismoking ads around Pam Laffin, a twenty-nine-year-old mother of two who had started smoking at ten, had emphysema by twenty-four, and died at thirty-one. "I started smoking to look older and I'm sorry to say it worked." The lesson: "it can be the *honesty and trustworthiness* of our sources, not their *status*, that allows them to act as authorities."

Usually, though, a message has to vouch for itself, and here concreteness returns as a source of belief. In a 1986 mock trial, jurors weighed eight arguments for and eight against a mother's fitness. The only thing that varied was which side got vivid but irrelevant details. On one side her son brushes his teeth with "a *Star Wars* toothbrush that looks like Darth Vader"; on the other the school nurse spills Mercurochrome on her uniform. Jurors rated the mother 5.8 out of 10 when the favorable side got the details and 4.3 when the unfavorable side did. Because details work whether or not they matter, the authors ask that they be true and tied to the core. When the Liz Lerman Dance Exchange claimed diversity as a core value, a professor scoffed that the company was probably all tall, thin twenty-five-year-olds. The artistic director answered that its longest-serving member was Thomas Dwyer, seventy-three, who joined after a government career with no dance experience. The skepticism collapsed, and the authors note he could answer that fast because diversity really was core to the company.

Statistics are the second internal source, and the authors handle them warily. In the 1980s, the Beyond War movement's Geoff Ainscow would drop one BB into a metal bucket ("This is the Hiroshima bomb"), then pour in 5,000, one for every nuclear warhead in the world. "Afterward there was always dead silence." The authors argue nobody would remember the number a week later, and that it didn't matter. What stuck was the relationship, a danger scaled far beyond anything people had pictured. "Statistics are rarely meaningful in and of themselves." They should illustrate a relationship, at a scale people have intuitions for. Told that a measurement was as accurate as throwing a rock from New York to Los Angeles and landing within two thirds of an inch, 83 percent of people were very impressed. Only 58 percent were impressed by the identical accuracy expressed as a throw from the sun to the earth. The popcorn campaign fits here: "37 grams" was a number, a day's worth of fatty meals a relationship. A sleazier communicator could have compared the bag to 712,000 fat-free lollipops, and the authors admit the topic made them anxious. Their rule is to use statistics "as input, not output": let the numbers make up your mind, rather than deciding first and hunting for numbers.

The third internal source is a single example that passes the **Sinatra Test**: "If I can make it there, I'll make it anywhere." When a Bollywood studio scoffed at letting Safexpress, an Indian shipper, distribute its piracy-sensitive films, Rubal Jain of Safexpress said his company had delivered every copy of the fifth *Harry Potter* book to every bookstore in India by 8 a.m. on release day without a leak, and had carried the executive's brother's board exams. The deal was signed two months later.

The last well is the audience. Wendy's 1984 ads showed elderly women staring at a huge bun with a tiny patty until Clara Peller demanded, "Where's the beef?" Wendy's quoted no expert and no percentage. It made a claim any customer could check by looking at the burgers, and revenue rose 31 percent in the first full year. The Heaths call this a **testable credential**: "try before you buy" for ideas. It can backfire. A 1990s rumor told people to look at a Snapple bottle for proof that the company backed the Ku Klux Klan, and there really was a ship (the Boston Tea Party) and a circled *K* (for kosher). The see-for-yourself step was valid; the conclusion wasn't.

Done well, the test runs in the audience's own heads. A Clinic on the **availability bias**, our tendency to judge how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind, asks readers to guess which kills more people: homicide or suicide, floods or tuberculosis, tornadoes or asthma. With luck, readers botch at least one, since suicides outnumber homicides by 50 percent, tuberculosis kills nine times as many people as floods, and asthma eighty times as many as tornadoes. Botching the guess teaches more than reading that research subjects did. At one NBA rookie orientation, women who had flirted with players in the hotel bar the first night introduced themselves the next morning: "Hi, I'm Sheila and I'm HIV positive." The NFL's session, in which former groupies described targeting players, was powerful too. But "what's more likely to stick with someone: hearing about someone who fooled someone else, or being fooled yourself?"

The chapter closes on Marshall. His institution's backing (the external well) counted for little, and his careful data (the internal well) didn't clear the bar. With the glass of bacteria, the authors say, he "modeled" a testable credential, whose implicit challenge was: drink this and you'll get an ulcer, as I did. Their praise is for "knowing when it was time to draw on a different well." The chapter starts from the instinct to reach first for authority and hard numbers. It ends with a different set of options: an antiauthority might beat an authority, a few vivid details might persuade more than a barrage of statistics, and one story that passes the Sinatra Test might overcome a mountain of skepticism. Along the way it also shows darker versions of several levers: a fake institute, a circled *K*, and a stained uniform all moved belief too.

# Believing is not caring

The chapter opens with Mother Teresa: "If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will." In 2004, Carnegie Mellon researchers paid people five dollars for a survey and then asked them to donate to Save the Children. Readers of a letter full of statistics about hunger in Africa gave $1.14 on average. Readers of a letter about Rokia, one seven-year-old girl in Mali, gave $2.38. Readers given both gave $1.43: adding the evidence of massive suffering made people *less* generous. The researchers suspected the numbers put people in an analytical frame of mind. Sure enough, people primed with a math question before reading about Rokia gave $1.26, while people primed to think about feelings gave $2.34. "The mere *act of calculation* reduced people's charity."

This goes a step beyond the last chapter. "Belief counts for a lot, but belief isn't enough. For people to take action, they have to *care*." Everyone believes fatty food is unhealthy; belief alone doesn't change what they eat. (James Grant's packet of salt and sugar had already made this trade.) The goal, the authors stress, isn't tearjerking. It's to make people care, because "feelings inspire people to act."

The choice of feeling matters. Teenagers already believe smoking is dangerous. After the 1998 tobacco settlement, two campaigns ran side by side. The "Truth" ads showed teens piling 1,800 body bags, the daily death toll from tobacco, outside a tobacco company's headquarters. Philip Morris ran "Think. Don't Smoke." A 2002 survey found that 22 percent of teens spontaneously recalled Truth and 3 percent Think, although most had seen both. Teens exposed to Truth were 66 percent less likely to say they'd smoke in the next year; those exposed to Think were 36 percent *more* likely. The Heaths read "Think" as the analytical frame from the Rokia studies all over again. Truth tapped a classic teenage emotion, resentment of authority, by painting Big Tobacco as duplicitous. Teens once smoked to rebel; now they could rebel by not smoking.

The most basic way to make people care "is to form an association between something they don't yet care about and something they do care about." But associations wear out through overuse, which researchers call **semantic stretch**. Over twenty years in the top fifty U.S. newspapers, the share of articles calling something "unique" rose 73 percent while "unusual," which should cover everything unique, declined. Jim Thompson of the Positive Coaching Alliance found that "sportsmanship" had stretched to mean merely not misbehaving: "Sportsmanship trophies are seen as consolation prizes for losers." He moved to fresh ground, **Honoring the Game**, the idea that you owe your sport respect because it's bigger than any player. One Dallas league went from a technical foul every fifteen games to one every fifty-two after his workshops.

The most direct thing people care about is themselves. The copywriter John Caples, author of "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano. . .But When I Started to Play!", told writers to put self-interest into every headline and sell benefits, not features. As an old maxim puts it, people don't buy quarter-inch drill bits; they buy quarter-inch holes. The Heaths are uneasy about how shady many of Caples's ads were, but they keep the lesson. A 1982 study in Tempe, Arizona, sharpened it. Homeowners told about cable TV's benefits subscribed at 20 percent when cable arrived, the same rate as their neighbors; homeowners asked to *imagine* themselves enjoying the same modest benefits subscribed at 47 percent. "It may be the tangibility, rather than the magnitude, of the benefits that makes people care."

Here the chapter turns to how we misjudge others. The authors keep Abraham Maslow's list of needs, from physical and security up through esteem, learning, and transcendence, but drop the hierarchy, since people pursue them all at once. Asked how to frame a $1,000 bonus, most people say they personally would be moved most by recognition, "the company recognizes how important you are." They predict that *others* would be moved most by a down payment on a car. Pitching a job, people pick the chance to learn for themselves and predict that others want security or visibility. "We are motivated by self-esteem, but *others* are motivated by down payments," a gap the authors say "explains almost everything about the way incentives are structured in most large organizations." We picture ourselves in the penthouse and everyone else in **Maslow's basement**. The counterexample is Floyd Lee, a retired military cook who ran the Pegasus dining hall outside the Baghdad airport. Pegasus served the same Army menu from the same suppliers as every other mess hall, but there were legends of soldiers driving one of Iraq's most dangerous roads to eat there. "As I see it, I am not just in charge of food service; I am in charge of morale." That, the authors say, reaches for transcendence, beauty, and learning, needs managers forget. His cooks culled the fruit for the perfect tray and marinated the Sunday prime rib for two days, living out the identity the Heaths sum up as "*A Pegasus chef is in charge of morale, not food.*"

Self-interest can also backfire. Firefighters offered a free popcorn popper for reviewing a fire-safety film answered, "Do you think we'd use a fire safety program because of some #*$@%! popcorn popper?!" Politics deepens the puzzle: the political scientist Donald Kinder, surveying thirty years of research, found the effect of self-interest on political opinions "trifling" except when a policy's effects are significant, tangible, and immediate. James March offers a related idea, two models of deciding. One calculates consequences. The other asks, "Who am I? What kind of situation is this? And what do people like me do in this kind of situation?" To a firefighter who runs into burning buildings, a gadget for caring about safety is an insult.

Texas used identity against litter. Its typical litterer, in the researcher Dan Syrek's profile, was a young, pickup-driving man who disliked authority and wasn't moved by weeping Native Americans or cartoon owls. Syrek called him "Bubba." Self-interest offered Bubba nothing, and fear would backfire, so the message became that *people like him* don't litter: "Don't Mess with Texas," delivered by Dallas Cowboys and other unmistakably Texan celebrities. Within a year litter fell 29 percent, a planned enforcement program was dropped as unnecessary, and over five years visible roadside litter fell 72 percent. The authors warn against crediting the slogan. The idea was identity, and "Don't Disrespect Texas" would still have worked.

At the end of the chapter the Curse of Knowledge returns, now blocking caring. At a seminar for arts leaders, a duo piano foundation read its purpose: "We exist to protect, preserve, and promote the music of duo piano." The room was unmoved. After about ten minutes of questions, ending with why the world would be poorer without duo piano music, the group explained that two pianos responding to each other give "the sound of the orchestra but the intimacy of chamber music," and eyebrows went up around the room. They had known this all along. Years inside duo piano had made them forget that most of the world had never heard of it, and asking "Why?" three times moved them from what they did to why it mattered. The design firm IDEO got hospital staff to care about workflow with a video shot from a patient's gurney, and the staff's first reaction was always "Oh, I never *realized*. . ." The authors bring the chapter full circle to Mother Teresa: empathy comes from the one, not the mass.

By the end, the starting assumption that people act on what they believe and on what pays them has become something more specific. People act when they feel something, and they feel more for one person than for a mass. Feeling can be borrowed by association but wears thin. Self-interest works best when it's tangible enough to imagine, and identity, the question of what someone like me would do, often outweighs it. And we underestimate what moves other people.

# The CEO and the moon, again

Go back to the two lines from the introduction with the chapters behind you. The authors never run this second pass themselves; it is this brief's application of their chapters. "Maximize shareholder value" isn't wrong; it may be more accurate than "THE low-fare airline." It fails because it is useful only to someone who already hears the tune, and it can't settle a chicken salad. It sounds like common sense, so it is as forgettable as "the best customer service in the industry." It is the engineers' drawing, an abstraction an expert reads as a pattern and a frontline employee can't act on. Its credibility is borrowed from the speaker, which Kennedy's goal needed too, but borrowed credibility was all the CEO's line had. And it offers nothing to feel and no identity to live up to, unlike Floyd Lee's redefinition of his job as morale.

Kennedy's goal passes each row in the specific sense the chapters gave it. It named one goal for a decade. It broke a guessing machine about what was possible while leaving a gap engineers could start on. It was concrete enough that, the authors suspect, little time went to quibbling over "man," "moon," or "decade." And his speech asked a whole country to see itself in the effort: "it will not be one man going to the moon, it will be an entire nation." The difference between the two speakers was never how much they knew. Like Newton's tappers, both heard a tune their listeners couldn't. Kennedy transformed his.

Two misjudgments in this reading point in opposite directions. The Curse of Knowledge makes us assume listeners know what we know. Maslow's basement makes us assume they want less than we do. The Heaths treat them separately, but for a week on mistaken impressions the pairing matters. A manager can be wrong about what employees understand and, separately, about what moves them. The duo piano group and the $1,000 bonus are the same lesson from two sides: the listener's head is not the speaker's, either in what it holds or in what it wants.

# What Stays With You

The Heaths want readers to stop treating an idea's failure to stick as the fault of the idea or of the audience. The knowledge that makes someone worth listening to is the same knowledge that keeps them from hearing their own message as a listener does, which is why Newton's tappers expected to be understood half the time and were understood once in forty. Their principles turn a core into something the listener already has, but the same traits carried a false Halloween scare as easily as a true warning about popcorn, so choosing an honest core is still the communicator's job.
