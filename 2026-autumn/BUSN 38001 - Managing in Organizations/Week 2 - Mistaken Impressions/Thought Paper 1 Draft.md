# Thought Paper 1

## a. Connection to Lecture

When I joined my previous employer, I was the first Asian employee on a team that had always been homogeneous. The distance I felt was easy to explain in personal terms: my colleagues weren't interested in someone different, or I needed to push harder to fit in. Class 1 warned against that reading. The fundamental attribution error leads us to blame character when the situation is doing much of the work. Our situation offered no shared, low-stakes setting where a newcomer and an established team could connect, so conversations stayed formal and cautious.

Instead of asking colleagues to change their attitudes, I changed one factor in the environment: I brought traditional Chinese dishes to work and invited people to try them. Food gave everyone an easy reason to gather and ask questions without a lecture about respect. Gradually, colleagues grew curious about Chinese culture, and the meals became shared memories. That common context built trust, and it reached our work: communication became smoother, conversations more organic, and collaboration easier.

As at NUMMI, changing the setting was cheaper and more effective than changing the people. This is one case with no control group, and familiarity grows with time anyway. Still, I now read many "personality clashes" as missing context, which anyone on a team can supply.

## b. Response to Readings

The Heaths identify six principles of sticky ideas (SUCCESs):

- **Simple:** find the core and state it compactly, like a proverb.
- **Unexpected:** break expectations, then fill the gap with insight.
- **Concrete:** use sensory detail and human actions.
- **Credible:** carry its own credentials, through vivid details or testable claims.
- **Emotional:** make people feel for a person, not a statistic.
- **Stories:** a mental flight simulator for action.

The villain is the Curse of Knowledge: once we know something, we can't imagine not knowing it.

Questions:

1. Some work is inherently tedious, like fixing PowerPoint formatting. The Heaths say ideas are made interesting, not born that way. But making formatting sticky seems to require reframing it as more meaningful than it feels ("clients judge our numbers by how clean the pages look"). Is that finding the core, or a story we tell ourselves?
2. The Kidney Heist and the Halloween candy scare show the principles work as well for false, fearful ideas, and "semantic stretch" describes an arms race of intensity. In a polarized media environment, does optimizing for stickiness reward extremes?
3. The Heaths found these traits by studying ideas that stuck. Does that repeat the "study only the successes" error from *In Search of Excellence* in class 1, or does their comparison of award-winning and ordinary ads guard against it?

## c. Connection to Readings

"My mama always said life was like a box of chocolates. You never know what you're gonna get." Forrest Gump's line has outlived the movie because it uses nearly every principle. It is simple: one sentence carries a core truth about uncertainty, like a proverb. It is unexpected: life, a heavy philosophical subject, becomes something ordinary and childlike. It is concrete: you can picture your hand hovering over the box. It is credible because listeners can test it against their own lives. It is emotional through a mother's warmth. And it lives inside a story: Forrest says it on a bench while offering a stranger a chocolate.

It also answers my second question. Every chocolate is good, so the surprise comes without fear or exaggeration. A message can stick by being gentle and true; extremity is one route to stickiness, not a requirement.
