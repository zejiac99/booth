- **Concentration:** the AI trade is "almost half" of S&P 500 weight [JPM Midyear].

**Why it's happening: four forces**

1. **The winners have visible earnings.**
   - JPM attributes the post-March rebound to "strong earnings" plus AI compute demand that kept widening [JPM Midyear].
   - Korean memory CEOs quote three-year order books [BLK Jewell].
   - Micron guided next-quarter revenue to $61.5B vs. a $56.8B consensus, 8% above. 75% of its FY27 revenue is already locked in. It has 26 long-term supply agreements running to 2030–31, most with price floors and ceilings. Gross margin is about 86–87% [TALK 9/30].
2. **Rates and oil squeeze the losers.**
   - Kaplan says the rate-sensitive economy is "sluggish or weak": autos, housing, and companies selling to low- and moderate-income consumers. Small businesses that borrow at the Fed funds rate feel the hikes directly. Homebuilders are squeezed twice, by high mortgage rates and by short-term financing on their inventory [GS Kaplan].
   - Consumers face mediocre real income growth, a low saving rate and tax refunds that have run their course [GS Mericle].
   - Oil is near $100 [GS Kaplan]. Diesel crack spreads (the refining margin over crude) are near $100 a barrel, and they feed straight into airfares and freight [SJ 9/29].
   - Consumer-credit growth is only 2.6% [SJ 9/15].
3. **The economy itself is K-shaped.**
   - The share of GDP going to corporate profits is rising, AI is accelerating it, and the share going to labor is muted [GS Kaplan].
   - Kaplan calls it a "low-fire, low-hire" labor market, where low- and moderate-income workers are "struggling to make ends meet" [GS Kaplan].
   - People who own assets get a wealth effect worth about 0.3–0.4pp of consumer-spending growth [GS Mericle].
4. **Market structure amplifies the split.**
   - Retail is 20–25% of daily U.S. equity volume.
   - Zero-day options are about half of S&P 500 options volume, up from under 5% in 2020.
   - Leveraged-ETF, passive and quant flows keep growing. The result is a momentum market [BLK Jewell].

### Scene 3: The bear case, and why I think it's wrong about the Fed

**The bear case (steelman it first):**
- Inflation is sticky. Before the Sept 30 revision, core PCE had been above 3% y/y every month this year [JPM Feroli], and inflation has now been above target for six straight years [JPM Midyear].
- The Fed is hiking. In mid-September the market priced about 3.5–4 hikes through next year [SJ 9/15].
- Macro hedge funds are short [SJ 9/15], and value stocks are falling at near-bear-market speed [TALK 9/30].

**What I say:** "Hikes don't cause crashes. Hikes beyond what's priced do. This time the market priced more hikes than the Fed itself projects. And most of this inflation comes from supply shocks, which rate hikes can't fix."

**1. History: what hurts is the surprise, not the hike** [SJ 9/15]

| Cycle | What was priced | What the Fed did | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2015–16 | Fed dots: 4 hikes. Market: 2 | 1 hike | Market concludes rates stay low forever |
| 2018 | Complacent after a decade near zero | Hikes plus QT ran past expectations | S&P ≈ −19% (Q4 2018) |
| 2022 | ~6 quarter-point hikes (as of March 2022) | 425bp in 2022 (≈17 quarter-point moves); 525bp by July 2023 | S&P ≈ −25% |
| **Now** | ~3.5–4 hikes through next year (mid-Sept) | Sept hike + ~1 more in 2026, none in 2027 (dots) | **The market is priced ahead of the Fed** |

🚩 The source says the Fed did "16 or 17 hikes" in 2022. That counts in quarter-point steps: 425bp in 2022 and 525bp across the whole cycle. The exact drawdowns (−19.8% in 2018, −25.4% in 2022) and hike totals come from the historical record, not these sources, so double-check them.

**2. This is supply-shock inflation**
- Shanghao Jin puts the upward pressure on core PCE at about 1pp. Tariffs are about 0.6pp and fading as they drop out of the y/y comparison through year-end. Oil is about 0.2pp. AI-demand effects make up the rest, and GS says those are exaggerated by mismeasurement [SJ 9/29; GS Mericle].
- Excluding tariffs, the war and AI effects, inflation has been "reasonably close to 2%". Those effects add "a little bit more than a percentage point" to PCE [GS Mericle].
- A GS macro strategist put it as "a rate hike can't produce another barrel of oil." Shanghao Jin adds "…or another chip," and calls September "a credibility hike into a supply shock" [SJ 9/15].

**3. Demand isn't driving it**
- **Wages:** wage growth is 3.1%, below core inflation (3.3%) and below the policy rate. In 2015 and 2018, wage growth ran roughly 1–2pp above the policy rate. There's no wage-price spiral [SJ 9/15].
- **Credit and rents:** consumer-credit growth is 2.6%, vs. 6.9% in 2015 and 9.1% in 2022. Shelter inflation keeps slowing, with a lag [SJ 9/15; SJ 9/29].
- **AI and jobs:** AI is a disinflationary force on labor. Shanghao Jin estimates 10–15K AI-sensitive jobs are lost each month, partly offset by about 7K data-center construction jobs [SJ 9/29]. 🚩 This is one strategist's estimate.

**4. The trend is already improving**
- After a BEA methodology change, core PCE ran 0.13 / 0.13 / 0.25% m/m in June–August, about 2.1% annualized, vs. 0.26 / 0.28 / 0.31% in March–May, about 3.5% annualized. Core PCE y/y is 3.0% [TALK 9/30; annualization calculated from the source's figures].
- On that print, the odds of an October hike fell from about 50% to about 37–38% [TALK 9/30].

**5. The Fed's own signals are muted**
- **The dots:** the median shows one more hike in 2026 and none in 2027. The longer-run dot rose to 3.25% [JPM Feroli; GS Kaplan].
- **Kaplan (former Dallas Fed president):** skip October and maybe hike in December. Two moves get the rate to about 4–4.25%, "in the neighborhood of neutral," and then the Fed reassesses. In his view the market is "expecting more hikes than… is likely" [GS Kaplan].
- **Pace:** there's precedent for hiking once a quarter, as in 2017–18 [JPM Feroli].
- **Why they hiked at all:** Warsh had boxed himself in at Jackson Hole. Firm payrolls and CPI then pushed the hike odds from 69% to 86% to 94% [SJ 9/15]. The unanimous vote signals credibility and independence [JPM Feroli].

**What could make me wrong (say this before they ask):**
- **Spillover:** Kaplan's rationale for hiking is that a supply shock lasting six to eight months or more "begins to bleed into 30 or 40 items." Hikes can slow that spread [GS Kaplan].
- **Sticky core:** JPM expects core inflation to stay at 3% or higher and sees a "shallow but broad-based tightening" into 2027 [JPM Midyear]. Even the hawks say *shallow*.
- **Labor:** if the labor market tightens, with unemployment falling toward 4%, more than one hike is back on the table [JPM Feroli; JPM Midyear].
- **The forecasters' record:** the summer sell-side call was already wrong once. GS expected no move in 2026 and a cut in 2027; JPM expected no hike until 2027 [GS Mericle; JPM Midyear].

**Takeaway line:** "I expect one or two credibility hikes, not a cycle. Even the hawks call it shallow."

🚩 Fed pricing moves fast. Refresh CME FedWatch for the late-October and December meetings, especially after the mid-October CPI.

**Transition:** "And even if the Fed does one or two more, stocks aren't priced for perfection."

### Scene 4: Valuations and positioning. Anxiety, not euphoria

**What I say:** "If this were a bubble you'd see euphoria. Instead you see compressed multiples and light positioning. The S&P trades at about 19 times forward earnings, only about one turn above where it was during the 2025 tariff panic. A lot of the AI risk already looks priced in."

**Evidence**
- **S&P 500 forward P/E:** 19.3x on Sept 11, vs. 18.1x at the 2025 tariff low. That's about 1.2 turns, or ~7% higher; the source rounds it to "a bit over 5%." 🚩 Say "about one turn" or "~7%," not "5%." The multiple has been compressed for most of the past year while earnings grew into the price. As Shanghao Jin puts it, "7,000 today isn't 7,000 a year ago" [SJ 9/15].
- **Even the AI winners trade cheaply:**
  - In March 2026 the Mag 7 were the cheapest they'd been in 10 years [JPM Midyear].
  - Micron trades at 6.7x forward earnings despite gross margins of about 86% [TALK 9/30].
  - Stocks with high-return structural growth historically carried a 10–20% premium, and short-term investors have eroded it [BLK Jewell].
- **The worries are already in the price.** Rates, oil, hyperscaler borrowing (possibly negative FCF next year) and the midterms are all cited as reasons funds have sold [SJ 9/15].
- **Positioning is light:**
  - Fundamental funds have cut exposure and macro funds are short.
  - Hedge-fund gross and net leverage isn't heavy, and nobody is re-leveraging [SJ 9/15; SJ 9/29].
- **Sentiment is washed out:**
  - Breadth readings are depressed, and value ETFs are oversold on RSI (a momentum gauge).
  - HY spreads are at 3.09%, close to March's 3.30–3.35%.
  - The MOVE index of bond volatility is at 110.5, near its March level [TALK 9/30].
- **The difference from 1999:** today's concentration is backed by earnings at modest multiples, not by multiples alone.

**Caveats to keep in your pocket**
- **The froth is in market structure, not the multiple.** Momentum, retail, zero-day options and leveraged ETFs drive big swings; the Kospi has moved ±20% [BLK Jewell]. JPM warns that crowding could trigger "a flash crash or a correction" [JPM Midyear].
- 🚩 **Don't argue "not a bubble" from narrow breadth.** Leadership was narrow in 1998–99 too (general knowledge, not from the sources). Argue from earnings and multiples instead.
- 🚩 **Don't call 19.3x "low."** It's roughly the S&P's 10-year average (high-18s) and below its 5-year average (about 20x). Those averages are general knowledge of FactSet's data, not from the sources, so verify them. Say "reasonable relative to growth" or "compressed" instead.
- 🚩 **Biggest gap: no earnings growth number.** The sources say "strong earnings" over and over but never give an S&P 500 EPS growth figure. Pull the latest blended EPS growth, the CY26 and CY27 estimates, and Mag 7 vs. "S&P 493" growth (FactSet *Earnings Insight*). That last split would be perfect evidence for the K-shape.

**Anticipate the pushback:** "With the 10-year above 5%, the S&P's forward earnings yield (1/19.3 ≈ 5.2%) is barely above Treasuries. Isn't that expensive?"
- Concede that the premium over bonds is thin today.
- Then pivot: a bond coupon doesn't grow and earnings do. A thin premium means the market is paying for growth. That leads into Scene 5.

**Transition:** "So my base case is that the market keeps grinding higher on earnings, but in a different rate regime from the last decade."

### Scene 5: The new regime. Higher r, but potentially higher g

**What I say:** "I don't think we go back to zero rates. Something like 3–4% is the new normal. But higher rates don't automatically mean lower valuations. In a DCF, if AI raises long-term growth as much as rates have gone up, terminal values hold."

**Rates aren't going back to zero**
- **Your 3–4% range is backed by the Fed's own numbers.** The longer-run dot, the usual read on the Fed's estimate of neutral, is now 3.25%. Kaplan puts the nominal neutral rate at about 4–4.25% (about 1–1.25% real plus inflation) [JPM Feroli; GS Kaplan].
- **The bond market has changed.** Term premiums have normalized, and Treasury demand has shifted away from central banks, foreign officials and banks toward price-sensitive buyers. JPM says that justifies an upward-sloping yield curve [JPM Midyear].
- **The "risk-free" rate is less risk-free.** In JPM's words, "the risk-free asset is maybe slightly less risk-free than it has been in the past" [JPM Midyear, Dulake].
- **The economy is less Fed-sensitive than in the 2010s,** when growth leaned on cheap liquidity. AI-driven productivity is part of the reason [SJ 9/15].

**The DCF lens: TV = FCF₁ / (r − g)** (illustrative numbers)

| Scenario | r | g | r − g | TV ÷ FCF | vs. base |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base | 9.0% | 3.0% | 6.0% | 16.7x | — |
| Rates +100bp, growth unchanged | 10.0% | 3.0% | 7.0% | 14.3x | −14% |
| Rates +100bp, AI lifts g +100bp | 10.0% | 4.0% | 6.0% | 16.7x | 0% |
| Rates +100bp, AI lifts g +150bp | 10.0% | 4.5% | 5.5% | 18.2x | +9% |

- **The r that matters is the long end plus the equity risk premium, not the Fed funds rate.** BlackRock's Helen Jewell says 25-basis-point shifts "don't really have a massive change" on valuations [BLK Jewell]. The September hike barely moved the curve because it had already adjusted [GS Kaplan].
- **Signs that g could be rising:**
  - Corporate profits are taking a larger share of GDP as AI spreads [GS Kaplan].
  - JPM argues that expected AI productivity gains let the Fed hike slowly [JPM Midyear].
  - AI demand is spreading into power, cement and concrete for data centers [BLK Jewell], and into energy M&A [EVR '26].
  - Shanghao Jin cites roughly six-month paybacks on AI investment at cloud providers [SJ 9/15]. 🚩 Single anecdote, unverified.

**The caveat that sharpens your argument: growth isn't free**
- Growth only adds value when the return on invested capital (ROIC) beats the cost of capital: "not all growth is good growth" [BLK Jewell].
- The fuller formula is TV = NOPAT₁ × (1 − g/ROIC) / (r − g).
- Worked example: a company earning a 15% ROIC goes from 3% to 4% growth. It now has to reinvest about 27% of profits instead of 20%, so free cash flow falls about 8%. Its value falls about 8% even though r − g hasn't changed.
- So higher g offsets higher r only if the growth comes from productivity (more output from the same capital), not from pouring in more capital. That's the whole AI debate in one line:
  - The hyperscalers are buying growth with huge capex, and their FCF is down [BLK Jewell].
  - BlackRock's economists expect the growth in AI capex to start slowing from 2027 [BLK Jewell].

**Bonus link (not from the sources; economic theory):** in standard growth models, faster productivity growth also raises the neutral rate. So a higher-rate regime and an AI growth boom can be two sides of the same coin. The evidence for this link is debated, so use it as framing, not as fact.

🚩 **"AI lifts earnings across sectors" is still a prediction.** In the sources, AI profits are concentrated in infrastructure (semis, memory, power), alongside some job losses. Look for evidence outside tech, such as BLS productivity data and S&P 493 margin trends.

🚩 **Terminal g can't exceed long-run nominal GDP growth,** which is about 5% today (2%-plus real growth and 2.5–3% inflation) [GS Kaplan]. Keep illustrative g's in the 3–4.5% range.

**Transition:** "That's the base case. Here's what would break it."

### Scene 6: Risks. What would make me wrong

1. **Oil, Hormuz and diesel: the swing factor.**
   - Brent has gone from about $60 to about $120, back below $80, and is now near $100 [JPM Midyear; GS Kaplan].
   - The real pain point is refined products. Diesel cracks are about $90–100 a barrel in the worst regions.
   - China's export quotas for refined products are the swing factor. If China loosens them, GS sees cracks falling toward $40. If it doesn't, European diesel prices could rise 50% or more this winter [SJ 9/29].
   - The longer prices stay high, the more they spill into other prices [GS Kaplan].
   - Upside: JPM's mid-year forecast of $80 Brent in Q4 and $64 in 2027 would be a big relief for the bottom of the K [JPM Midyear].
2. **The long end of the curve and fiscal policy, not the Fed.**
   - The 10-year is above 5% after a weak auction and heavy September supply, with pension buyers holding back [SJ 9/29].
   - The CBO revised deficit estimates higher, and there's no fiscal plan [GS Kaplan]. The deficit is about 6% of GDP even at full employment. If the GOP keeps the House in the midterms, higher defense spending could push term premiums back up [JPM Midyear].
   - What shakes stocks is the volatility of long rates, not their level [SJ 9/29]. The MOVE index is at 110.5 [TALK 9/30].
   - Possible relief: Treasury shifts more of its issuance into short-term bills [TALK 9/30].
3. **The Fed's reaction function.**
   - Warsh gives less forward guidance [GS Mericle]. His press conference was cut to 30 minutes with no follow-up questions, and his messaging on the neutral rate was mixed [JPM Feroli].
   - Kaplan says the market is building in a "risk premium" because it doesn't yet understand how Warsh reacts to data [GS Kaplan].
4. **AI: returns, financing, crowding and regulation.**
   - Shareholders are starting to question the return on AI spending, with "a few cracks" showing [EVR '26]. The capex growth slows from 2027 [BLK Jewell].
   - A delayed Oracle project (presumably AI infrastructure), with debt held by Apollo, led to some redemptions in high yield [SJ 9/29].
   - Crowded positioning means a sudden drop is possible [JPM Midyear].
   - AI-regulation headlines caused the Sept 14 selloff [SJ 9/15]. 🚩 Keep this neutral in a chat; the source's framing is partisan.
5. **Credit and liquidity.**
   - HY spreads widened 48bp in September to 3.09%, and investment-grade spreads widened too. Part of that is heavy September issuance rather than panic [TALK 9/30; SJ 9/29].
   - Private credit faces retail redemptions and software loan maturities building toward 2028. JPM calls it manageable and "not systemic" [JPM Midyear].
6. **Geopolitics and global tightening.**
   - The U.S.–China summit only produced two-month extensions on tariffs and rare-earth exports. Taiwan and chip exports remain unresolved [SJ 9/29].
   - The ECB and BOJ are hiking, with the BoE expected to follow, into heavy debt loads [JPM Midyear; SJ 9/15].
   - Globally, the U.S. is the relative winner. It's a net energy exporter, the dollar is up, and "U.S. exceptionalism is coming back." Europe bears the energy cost [JPM Midyear; SJ 9/29].

**Upside risk worth naming:** if oil and long rates ease, the bottom of the K could rally hard. JPM called the consumer the "pain trade" because most portfolios are underweight it. JPM expected that rotation in the second half, but it hasn't happened yet because oil and rates went the wrong way in September [JPM Midyear; TALK 9/30].

**What would change my mind:**
- Core PCE at 0.3% m/m or higher for three straight months
- Unemployment falling toward 4% with wages accelerating
- HY spreads breaking above the March highs (about 330–335bp)
- Hyperscalers cutting their capex guidance
- Disorderly auctions pushing the 10-year meaningfully higher

### Scene 7: What it means for M&A

**What I say:** "The split is itself an M&A engine. Large caps have a valuable stock to pay with, open financing, and urgency around AI and scale. Undervalued mid-caps are increasingly willing sellers, or targets."

   - Hostile bids are at record highs. Rivals jump announced deals, and PE firms are teaming up with activists on joint bids [EVR '26].

2. **Strong stock prices bring currency and confidence.**
   - IPO proceeds roughly doubled, from about $67B to about $130B [EVR '26].
   - Convertible issuance is high, and consolidation deals between mega-cap strategic buyers lead activity [EVR '26].

   - 🚩 Those are 2025 figures, and the basis for the IPO comparison isn't clear.
3. **Financing is open despite the hike.**
   - CEOs care about the long end of the curve and credit spreads, not the Fed funds rate [GS Kaplan].
   - Public credit is holding up because all-in yields are attractive [JPM Midyear].
   - The LBO market is "strong" and "starting to pick up" [EVR '26].
   - 🚩 My inference, not stated in the sources: PE sponsors are more exposed to rates than strategic buyers because LBO debt is mostly floating-rate. September's HY widening and a 5% 10-year raise their hurdle. Verify with recent leveraged-finance data.
4. **AI is a catalyst.**

6. **Watch-outs.**
   - Rates volatility.
   - Quant and retail shareholders can punish an acquirer when an M&A deal stops its buybacks. Short interest in M&A deals has nearly doubled, and quant and retail ownership is up 3–4x over 10 years [EVR '26].

In September semis were up 11%, while banks, REITs and consumer discretionary were down 6 to 9%. Only about a fifth of S&P stocks are above their 50-day moving average.
>
> I don't think this turns into a 2022-style hiking cycle. Those selloffs came when the Fed hiked more than the market expected. Today the market is pricing more hikes than the Fed's own projections. The inflation is mostly supply-driven, from tariffs and oil, and wage growth is actually running below inflation. On the revised data, the last three months of core PCE annualize to about 2%.
>
> Valuations aren't euphoric either. The S&P is at about 19 times forward earnings, barely a turn above where it was at last year's tariff low, and institutional positioning is light. So my base case is continued, earnings-led upside, just in a world where rates settle around 3 to 4% instead of zero. I think about it in DCF terms: r is higher, but if AI productivity lifts g, valuations can hold.
>
> The risks I'm watching are oil, the long end of the curve and the deficit, and whether AI capex earns its return. From an M&A standpoint, this split is actually fueling deals. Large caps have the stock to pay with and the financing, and undervalued mid-caps are increasingly willing sellers."

## Likely follow-up questions

**"Isn't a 5% 10-year a problem for stock valuations?"**
Yes, the premium over bonds is thin: about a 5.2% forward earnings yield vs. a roughly 5% 10-year. But earnings grow and coupons don't, so a thin premium means the market is paying for growth. That's why it's so selective, paying up only where earnings are visible. Also, much of September's rise in long rates came from heavy supply and a weak auction, not from fears about growth [SJ 9/29].

**"Isn't AI just the 1999 telecom capex bubble?"**
- BlackRock says the market hasn't decided yet whether this looks like telecom in 1999 or cloud in the 2010s [BLK Jewell].
- The difference today is valuation. The Mag 7 were the cheapest in 10 years in March, Micron trades at 6.7x forward earnings, and memory is sold out three years ahead.
- The S&P's forward P/E peaked around 24–25x in 1999–2000. 🚩 That figure is from general knowledge; verify it.
- The real risk is an earnings air pocket from 2027 as capex growth slows, not today's multiple.

**"Inflation has been above target for six years. Why won't the Fed keep going?"**
Because hikes can't fix supply shocks like oil, tariffs or chip shortages. The demand-side drivers (wages, consumer credit, shelter) are all running below core inflation. The trend has already cooled to about 2% annualized, and the Fed's own dots show only one more hike. What would change my mind is a labor market that tightens again, or three straight hot core prints.

**"What are you watching over the next month?"**
- **Fed:** September CPI in mid-October, then the late-October FOMC (on Sept 30, the market gave an October hike about 37% odds).
- **Bonds:** 10-year auctions and the MOVE index.
- **Oil:** Brent, diesel cracks and any news on China's export quotas.
- **Earnings:** Q3 season starts mid-October. Watch hyperscaler capex guidance and the Mag 7 vs. S&P 493 earnings split.
- **Politics:** the Nov 3 midterms, for what they mean for fiscal policy and term premiums.
- 🚩 **Jobs:** the September jobs report (early October) came out after these sources. Check it.
