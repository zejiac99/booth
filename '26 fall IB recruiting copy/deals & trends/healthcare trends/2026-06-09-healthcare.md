### 1. [Weekly Health Tech Reads 5/31/26](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ksz9st62wyv9gctvmwmang8q) — *healthtechnerds.com* · *May 31, 2026*

**The AI platform land-grab and what a $100B healthcare winner might actually look like**

- Commure raised $70 million at a $7 billion valuation, and both CEO Tanay Tandon and GC’s Hemant Taneja are publicly anchoring a $100 billion ambition — built on acquiring point solutions and leveraging deep relationships with systems like HCA and Tenet. Tandon described Forward Deployed Engineers essentially doing efficiency consulting at GC-owned Summa Health to prove the AI-saves-a-failing-system thesis.
- Kevin O’Leary agrees that a $100 billion healthcare AI company is plausible over a decade or two, but argues that it won’t resemble the Palantir-for-healthcare model Commure is running. The winner, he thinks, will own more of the care-delivery and financing dollar — à la UHG — rather than staying a pure infrastructure layer. Health Catalyst’s $100 million market cap is a public-market warning that platform-only might not carry that kind of value.
- The Palantir comparison is useful because it sets up the real tension: selling AI into health systems as a services-plus-software layer versus embedding the economics of care delivery directly. Tandon’s interview makes clear that Commure often wins health-system deals as a point solution today, which suggests the platform narrative is still aspirational rather than proven.

**GLP-1s and the missing BMI shift**

- Epic Research published five-year BMI data showing essentially no movement: healthy-weight adults rose 0.5%, overweight/obese fell 0.5%. This despite ~$36 billion in GLP-1 revenue in 2025 and polling suggesting 12% of the adult population took the drugs.
- The sub-group on GLP-1s did show a 2% shift toward healthy BMI between 2024 and 2026, but the population-level signal remains tiny. O’Leary frames the puzzle clearly: if these drugs are solving obesity, the curve has to bend eventually — and it hasn’t yet, raising questions about adherence, access, and whether the current cash-pay and employer-coverage channels are reaching the right people.

**Utah’s AI pilot shows human-in-the-loop is still the default**

- Doctronic’s AI recommended approval for 72% of prescriptions, physicians approved 91% of those without additional information. When the AI escalated to a human, 31% of those escalations were deemed overly cautious by the physician.
- The striking part is how mundane the process looks: a human is still in every loop, and the real bottleneck appears to be patient adoption, not AI safety. For all the policy anxiety about replacing physicians, the pilot data suggests AI is acting more like a conservative triage assistant than a clinical decision-maker.

**Regulatory and market shifts across payer-provider economics**

- Clover Health won a Stars lawsuit that could re-rate its plan from 3.5 to 4 Stars, unlocking ~$120 million in 2027 bonus payments. The ruling, combined with the Chevron deference rollback, may open broader challenges to CMS’s Stars methodology — potentially repricing the quality-bonus machinery that MA plan economics rest on.
- Providence Health Plan and PacificSource are both exiting insurance markets. Providence is winding down its ACA, Medicaid, and employer lines after a $100 million loss in 2025; PacificSource is leaving the ACA market entirely across multiple states. The local reporting on Providence points to leadership distraction toward startup partnerships at the expense of health plan fundamentals. The regional health plan model — especially when provider-sponsored — is under structural pressure.
- The No Surprises Act IDR process continues to generate friction: Radiology Associates of North Texas says BCBS Texas has paid only 2% of binding awards, leading to $51 million in administrative waste, while a court dismissed BCBS Texas’s complaint against HaloMD. The law is holding, but the pricing gap between payer-side and provider-side vendors remains a live conflict.

**Funding highlights: navigation, nutrition, and the provider-as-distribution-channel thesis**

- Garner Health raised $100 million at a $2.74 billion valuation, claiming $200 million in ARR and 12% cost savings for employers — a healthcare navigation play that is starting to look like the consolidating category for employer-focused platforms.
- Nourish raised $100 million at a $1.75 billion valuation with Menlo Ventures praising its pharma-style field sales motion that turns providers into referral engines. This provider-as-distribution-channel strategy is showing up across mental health and now nutrition, suggesting a broader go-to-market pattern for services that sit alongside — rather than inside — traditional clinical workflows.
- Signos’s $20 million round brings Dexcom onto the cap table at a moment when Dexcom is under activist pressure and its own D2C CGM, Stelo, needs strategic direction. Kin raised $9 million for a patient-facing visit-recording app, with GoodRx co-founders involved, signaling a likely benefits-coordination monetization path.

**What Kevin O’Leary’s into lately:**
- The D2C full-body MRI brand Ezra, acquired by Function Health a year ago with promises of 1,000+ locations at $499, is now at 189 locations and $999 — a clear signal of slower-than-expected consumer demand.
- The WSJ piece calling NPs the “hottest job in healthcare” riled physicians, but a JAMA article on treating primary care as a public utility raises a deeper question: what actually counts as access — an AI chatbot, an NP, a MinuteClinic, a physician? The answers diverge sharply by audience.
- The NHS is finalizing a workforce plan that leans heavily on AI for care delivery, already being called a “dangerous gamble” by the British Medical Association.

### 2. [🏥 May Recap](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kt520c55bw85vg0dy5cbqsva) — *workweek.com* · *Jun 2, 2026*

Blake Madden’s monthly scan of hospital and health-tech moves is dense with signals. The through-line across most of them is a market that’s pricing two different realities at once.

**The AI price divergence in public vs. private markets**

- Doximity dropped 28% when the market noticed AI compute costs eating into its network margin; Health Catalyst fell 20% after admitting a significant portion of revenue is at risk during its AI pivot. In the same month, Forus (rebranded Tandem) raised $160 million at a $1 billion valuation from Thrive and General Catalyst, and OpenEvidence reported 650,000 doctors and 27 million searches at a $12 billion private-market valuation while Doximity’s enterprise value sits below $4 billion.
- Madden’s summary line sharpens the whole dynamic: “Public markets are pricing defensibility risk, private markets are pricing upside optionality, and one of those prices is wrong.” It’s not just a funding-environment observation — it’s a statement about what kind of evidence each market is using to underwrite the AI transition in healthcare, and how much those evidence sets diverge.

**Hospital M&A thaw — but regulatory friction remains**

- After the quietest year for hospital deals in 15 years, May saw a cascade: Sutter-Allina went definitive at $26 billion, Atrium “combined” with WakeMed, Sanford entered the Twin Cities via North Memorial, UPMC took CommonSpirit’s Trinity Health in Ohio, and Quorum Health shed its PE-backed for-profit identity to go nonprofit under Healthside Partners. Ascension’s $3 billion AmSurg deal sits in regulatory limbo.
- Vertical moves are equally telling: HCA acquired The College of Health Care Professions to build a labor pipeline, echoing its earlier Galen College of Nursing deal. When scaled operators are buying educational institutions to secure workforce, the labor shortage is being structuralized into M&A strategy.

**Litigation re-pricing MA’s core machinery**

- Clover’s Georgia Stars win — a judge tossed 20 measures and ordered a ~$120 million recalculation — and the Massachusetts AG suit alleging UHC upcoded duals for at least $100 million are two different attack vectors on the risk-adjustment and quality-bonus apparatus.
- The duals angle in the UHC suit is particularly consequential because dual-eligible populations are a much larger and thornier pool than standard MA risk-adjustment enforcement has previously targeted. Pair these with the OIG’s stroke-code audit finding $462 million in potential overpayments, and multiple branches of government are now contesting the actuarial foundation MA plans are built on.

**Medicare GLP-1 coverage is a stealth Part D expansion**

- CMS confirmed the $50/month price for Wegovy, Zepbound, and Foundayo starting July 1, with a net manufacturer price of $245. Even at 5% uptake across KFF’s estimated 14 million overweight/obese Medicare beneficiaries, that’s ~$1.6 billion annually. The bridge runs through December 2027 before handing off to the BALANCE program. Madden frames this correctly as the largest expansion of Medicare drug coverage since Part D in 2006.
- The simultaneous dynamics are worth tracking: Lilly posted $20 billion in quarterly revenue with Mounjaro/Zepbound at 65% of the total, while ~10% of employers are expected to drop GLP-1 coverage in 2027. Two payment rails are forming — cash-pay-via-Medicare for seniors, DTC-cash for the working-age population — and they may not converge smoothly.

**PE’s post-acute re-entry and exit window reopening**

- Enhabit closed its $1.1 billion Kinderhook take-private the same week CMS announced a six-month enrollment moratorium on new hospice and home health agencies — an immediate competitive advantage for scaled existing operators. General Atlantic closed its $3 billion Team Services deal, Select Medical went private, and KKR’s ambulance roll-up GMR Solutions cleared an IPO at $3.35 billion.
- The pattern: private, levered, integrated capital structures are betting on regulatory capture, M&A consolidation, and demographic tailwinds in post-acute care. The moratorium effectively raises the barrier to entry just as the largest players are going private, a timing coincidence that benefits the incumbents.

**Payviders and regional plans under pressure**

- Providence Health Plan is winding down most operations for 2027, Beckers reported systems broadly scaling back payvider ambitions, ACA deductibles spiked 37%, and Berkshire exited UnitedHealth entirely. The Milliman Medical Index hit $37,824 for a family of four — the largest non-COVID jump in a decade.
- The payvider thesis — that owning both insurance and delivery creates aligned incentives and better economics — is running into the reality that running a health plan well requires a different organizational muscle than running a delivery system, and the capital demands of both at once are punishing.

**Physician employment dynamics tightening on three fronts**

- A Health Affairs paper on PE-acquired primary care practices (2016–22) found services billed up 30% and patients seen up 11%, driven by APP scaling and ancillary-service mining. Nebraska banned healthcare staffing noncompetes, California’s AG is scrutinizing the Aspen Dental MSO model, and the PeaceHealth emergency staffing debacle is the first real test of Oregon’s stringent corporate practice of medicine law. The House E&C subcommittee is mulling Medicare physician pay reform.
- The regulatory perimeter around physician aggregation — PE roll-ups, MSO structures, staffing-company relationships — is tightening simultaneously at the state and federal level, which will reshape the economics of the physician-practice acquisition playbook.

### 4. [Weekly Health Tech Reads 6/7/26](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kthaejsvfdyzzyfrtamrzdjr) — *healthtechnerds.com* · *Jun 7, 2026*

**What’s the optimal amount of healthcare fraud?**

- Kevin O’Leary opens with a conversation from years ago: the optimal level of fraud in any large system may not be zero, because the marginal return of eliminating it eventually declines and begins catching legitimate actors in the net. The Minnesota Medicaid revalidation illustrates this in real time — 3,411 providers were disenrolled, but 2,491 of them were for incomplete or unsubmitted paperwork, and at least one visibly legitimate startup, YourPath, got caught in the sweep.
- The underlying idea, drawn partly from a Bits about Money post, is that fraud is an abuse of trust, and trust has immense social utility. In a low-trust environment — which is where healthcare policy operates now — the allowable level of fraud is pushed toward zero by political and social-media dynamics, and the collateral damage to good actors and to the investability of Medicaid becomes the price.
- This isn’t just a philosophical exercise. For startups and investors looking at Medicaid markets, a zero-tolerance fraud posture adds yet another layer of operational and reputational risk in an already difficult segment.

**The autism therapy market as a jobs program**

- A WSJ chart on the growth of the autism therapy workforce is the kind of graphic that makes the “healthcare as jobs program” argument vivid. When you juxtapose it with the fraud discussion, the question of how many of these jobs depend on a level of spending that wouldn’t survive a more stringent fraud-and-abuse environment becomes uncomfortable.

**The 850% rise in $3 million-plus claims**

- BCS, a reinsurer for Blues plans, reports an 850% increase over the past decade in claims exceeding $3 million, attributing it to gene therapies, specialty drugs, and complex pediatric, oncology, and cardiac care. The absolute number of claims isn’t disclosed, but the directional signal reinforces that the tail risk in health plan underwriting is thickening, which in turn pushes plans toward more aggressive risk-transfer and reinsurance strategies — including the kind of risk that venture-backed VBC platforms are now taking on.

**Wealthy consumers are buying human access, not just technology**

- Two WSJ real estate pieces capture a revealing pattern: wealthy seniors are purchasing “med-à-terres” — second homes near their preferred specialists at New York health systems — and luxury condos are marketing high-end longevity concierge services like Atria Health as an amenity. The technology is present, but the selling point is curated access to human expertise and experience. This is a useful corrective to narratives that consumer healthcare innovation is primarily about software or AI; the high-end buyer is voting for personalized human attention, tech-enabled but not tech-replaced.

**Out-of-network pricing litigation highlights a structural stalemate**

- Highmark Health sued HaloMD and a provider group for abusing the No Surprises Act IDR process, while MultiPlan and several payers face a lawsuit in Arizona alleging that MultiPlan’s pricing methodology amounts to a payer cartel that systematically underpays providers. The symmetry is the point: payers contract with vendors to pay low, providers contract with vendors to get paid high, and the IDR process sits in the middle as an imperfect arbiter. The underlying pricing gap isn’t going away.

**Another regional plan exit, and where the opportunity might be**

- Presbyterian is exiting Medicare Advantage after ~$60 million in losses, affecting roughly 30,000 members, but is staying in D-SNP where it serves 13,000 members. The decision to keep D-SNP while shedding broader MA suggests that the duals market — despite its complexity — is seen as a segment where a regional player can still make the economics work, fitting with the broader industry attention on duals as a growth and margin opportunity.

**Funding patterns: AI-ification roll-ups and primary care adjacency**

- Adaptive Innovations raised $60 million for an AI operating system that acquires mom-and-pop home health agencies and improves their margins. With 100,000+ home visits in Texas since launching in 2025, it’s deploying a classic roll-up-plus-tech playbook in a fragmented sector.
- Lassie raised $35 million from a16z for an AI platform targeting small practices, with particular traction in dental offices, and claims a path to $100 million-plus ARR within a year of launch. Five-figure annual contracts across 700 practices signal genuine appetite for AI-ification among small provider businesses — a market that health tech has historically struggled to serve profitably.
- Ilant Health’s $15 million round for virtual obesity care is positioned at the intersection of employer cost pressure and GLP-1 demand, straddling clinical outcomes and total-cost-of-care management.

**What Kevin O’Leary’s into lately:**
- Neil Shah on “hot peptide summer” and the wellness ecosystem filling gaps that primary care leaves open — a quick take on how the gray market and DTC channels are responding to what the traditional system won’t provide.
- North Carolina’s Healthy Opportunities Pilot, which used Medicaid dollars for food, housing, and transportation, reduced costs by $164 per person per month — and the state legislature opted not to fund it, suspending operations. The evidence was there; the political will wasn’t.

### 5. [Thyme Care: Initiation of Coverage](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ktm60qba37a27wkq212dm3za) — *healthtechnerds.com* · *Jun 8, 2026*

An initiation-of-coverage research note on Thyme Care, a value-based oncology platform that closed a $97 million Series D in September 2025 at a $1 billion post-money valuation. The piece is structured as a strategic assessment with scenario probabilities — the kind of analysis that bridges operating metrics and capital-market judgment.

**What Thyme does and why the growth curve demands scrutiny**

- Thyme is an asset-light, provider-agnostic oncology VBC platform. It wraps care-management services around existing oncology practices rather than employing physicians, taking risk on total cost of care. Medical spend under management went from ~$1 billion in 2024 to ~$5 billion in 2025; lives from 10,000 to 85,000, with guidance for 130,000 in 2026. That’s eight-and-a-half times the members and five times the dollars in twelve months.
- The cap table is a who’s-who of potential strategic partners and acquirers: Humana, Morgan Health, Memorial Hermann, Texas Oncology, CVS Health Ventures, alongside a16z, Town Hall, Frist Cressey, and others.
- The research note’s central concern is that this shape of growth has been a red flag in VBC before. Payers have, in recent memory, offloaded a lot of risk onto venture-backed startups in a short window, and venture capital pressure can amplify risk-taking rather than restrain it.

**The moat question: concurrent benchmarking**

- The most differentiated thing about Thyme is how it underwrites risk, using concurrent benchmarking — pricing a population’s expected cost trajectory against a contemporaneous control group rather than a historical baseline. In financial terms, this should reduce beta exposure (sensitivity to broad population-level cost trends) and increase alpha exposure (the value Thyme’s care model actually creates). If the methodology is durable and Thyme is persistently better at pricing risk than its payer counterparties, that’s a genuine moat in a sector where moats are rare.
- The open question is durability: can concurrent benchmarking hold up under adversarial contract negotiations, and does it survive when payers — particularly Humana, which sits on the cap table and has a history of internalizing vendor capabilities — decide to build their own version?

**Asset-light and provider-agnostic is a structural differentiator — with risks**

- Unlike the vertically integrated “for-profit Kaiser” playbook, Thyme doesn’t employ doctors at scale. It sits between payers and providers aligning incentives, which lets it scale faster without the operational drag of running clinics. If oncology turns out to be a category where the platform layer wins, Thyme is positioned for it.
- The flip side: the middleman position is vulnerable to disintermediation, particularly if payer partners insource the capability or if oncologists decide they’d rather take the risk themselves and keep the margin.

**Specialty pharma is the bull case and the hardest problem**

- Roughly 60% of oncology total cost of care is pharma, mostly buy-and-bill on injectables and infusables, and most oncology practices make their money on those margins. Thyme’s reported savings to date come from inpatient and ER avoidance and care management — the easier-to-capture savings. Solving the pharma problem means rearranging the buy-and-bill economics so that prescribing the cheaper biosimilar is financially neutral for the oncologist, with enough residual savings left for Thyme to capture. That math is possible but not yet proven at scale.

**Scenario range and the kidney-care parallel**

The note lays out five scenarios, summarized here with probabilities:

| Scenario | Prob. | Key condition | Implied outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monster Bull | 1% | Pharma spend solved, model extends to other specialties | IPO at $10B+ |
| Bull | 19% | Benchmarking proves durable; strategic acquirer sees it as the oncology platform layer | Exit at $2.0–3.0B |
| Base | 50% | Care-management savings hold but pharma moves slowly, margins compress | Exit at $1.2–1.5B |
| Bear | 20% | Oncology VBC rhymes with kidney care: competition, payer insourcing, growth outrunning risk capacity | Down round or acquihire at steep discount |
| Zero | 10% | Risk goes bad, losses exceed the balance sheet | Bankruptcy |

The probability-weighted midpoint lands at a modest premium to the $1 billion Series D mark, weighted toward strategic acquisition rather than IPO. The combined bear-plus-zero tail at 30% is heavier than typical for a company at this stage because the speed and scale of risk taken on creates a real bankruptcy path if a contract year goes badly. The bull and monster bull cases both route through strategic acquisition, not public listing — a read that this isn’t a public-company shape in its current form.

**What to watch and what to ask management**

The research team flags five watchpoints: payer-insourcing signals (track Humana job postings), multi-homing and bake-off dynamics among payers, specialty-pharma execution milestones, provider-relationship friction, and regulatory attention from state insurance regulators to the risk-transfer arrangements. The management questions center on total-cost-of-care reductions across the full book, the buy-and-bill economics, the underwriting edge over insurers, the regulatory visibility into reserves, and the company’s explanation for why its growth curve won’t end like the kidney VBC wave did.

## Notable Perspectives

- **“Public markets are pricing defensibility risk, private markets are pricing upside optionality, and one of those prices is wrong.”** Blake Madden’s diagnosis of the AI valuation gap between Doximity/Health Catalyst and OpenEvidence/Forus. It’s a lens that applies well beyond this month’s headlines — anytime a sector transitions from legacy economics to an AI-native model, the two markets will disagree on whether the incumbents or the new entrants capture the value, and the resolution usually involves some of both being wrong.

- **The $100 billion healthcare AI platform probably won’t look like Palantir-for-healthcare; it will own more of the care delivery and financing dollar, like UHG or HCA today.** Kevin O’Leary isn’t dismissing the ambition — he’s challenging the architectural assumption behind Commure’s model. If the history of large-scale healthcare platforms is a guide, the ones that reached escape velocity did so by integrating vertically into risk-bearing and care delivery, not by staying a pure technology layer. That’s a structural argument worth holding onto when evaluating any of the current crop of AI-infrastructure plays.

- **Concurrent benchmarking as a VBC moat — reducing beta exposure to isolate alpha.** The Thyme Care research note explains that by benchmarking against a contemporaneous control group rather than historical averages, Thyme insulates its performance from population-wide cost trends and ties its economics more directly to the value of its care model. If durable, this underwriting advantage is a rarer form of defensibility in VBC than network effects or scale. The question is whether it survives adversarial contract cycles and payer insourcing.

- **The optimal level of fraud may not be zero, because fraud is a trust problem.** Kevin O’Leary’s musing — informed by a Bits about Money post on the social utility of trust — reframes the fraud crackdown as a trade-off between eliminating bad actors and preserving the trust necessary for a functioning market. When Minnesota disenrolls 3,411 providers mostly for paperwork gaps, including a legitimate startup like YourPath, the policy has crossed into destroying value that the system needs. For investors, this makes Medicaid markets harder to underwrite; for policymakers, it’s a genuine dilemma that the current political discourse refuses to acknowledge.

- **Wealthy healthcare consumers are buying access to humans, not just technology — and that’s the leading edge of how innovation diffuses.** The WSJ pieces on med-à-terres and longevity concierge services reveal that the premium experience is human-mediated, tech-enabled care, not a pure software or AI replacement. If innovation follows the iPhone-like path from high cost to mass adoption, the early signal is that personalized human attention is the scarce resource, and the technology that wins will be the kind that makes that attention more scalable, not the kind that tries to eliminate it.
