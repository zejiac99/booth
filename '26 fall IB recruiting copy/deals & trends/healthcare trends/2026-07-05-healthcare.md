### 1. [🏥 Regulations with unintended consequences for care delivery](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ktq1fbvhn9swh10pcdj2pmjr) — *Hospitalogy* · *Jun 9, 2026*

A Hospitalogy community discussion on two questions: which regulation produced the most unintended consequences for care delivery, and which care model the system can’t support. A few standout answers anchor the piece, then the back half covers an AI-in-primary-care event with MaineHealth and Lumeris.

**Regulation candidates:**
- **ESRD entitlement (1972):** Hardcoded a reimbursement architecture around end-stage rescue that still organizes capital and care delivery around the back end of disease rather than prevention.
- **EMTALA (1986):** Solved patient dumping but made the ED the default primary-care entry point for the uninsured, funneling non-emergent demand into the most expensive setting without a companion gatekeeping structure. Every downstream cost shift, copay hike, and federal adjustment was an attempt to rebalance what EMTALA rerouted.
- **DRGs:** Accelerated the business orientation of hospitals — forced them to think about revenue and expense by service line, but 40 years later many still don’t do cost accounting well.
- **ACA:** Expanded coverage but drove deductibles high enough that patients default to prevention-only, then get hit with surprise bills when screening leads to diagnostics, eroding trust in the system.
- **ICD-10:** Technological burden, reimbursement chasing, coding errors — volume over quality.
- **Broader pattern:** Well-intended policy collides with how care actually happens. Policy gets written from theory; the tradeoffs hit care delivery.

**Care models the system can’t support:**
- **Primary and preventive care:** Payment structures reward procedures, not time, continuity, or coordination. Primary care influences up to 70% of spend but gets about 5% of it.
- **Longitudinal, relationship-centered care:** Most systems remain built for transactional, episodic interactions, not trusted clinical relationships over time.

**Scaling primary care with agentic AI (MaineHealth / Lumeris event):**
- MaineHealth serves the state with the oldest population in the U.S.; primary care remains a loss leader in a FFS structure even when leadership believes in it.
- The capacity math is brutal: if every PCP did everything evidence-based guidelines require, it would take 26 hours per day per panel.
- The “infinite intern” framing: an AI agent that takes histories, summarizes charts, manages in-basket messages, and handles interstitial care (medication adherence, chronic disease check-ins, preventive outreach) so the physician arrives ready for the meaningful conversation.
- Trust mechanics: the AI must be anchored to the existing physician-patient relationship, not a standalone product. Standing orders are the right mental model for oversight — validate up front, then let it run.
- Consumerism threat: patients are already asking ChatGPT health questions; if health systems don’t fill the AI-primary-care gap, tech platforms will.
- Payment unlock: CPT codes measure visits, not panel management. Sub-capitation and subscription-based DPC (MaineHealth’s Trellis Health) are interim workarounds while payers catch up. The CFO case: an AI-enabled care team member that lets a 1,500-patient panel grow to 1,700–2,000 flows through to 340B capture, pay-for-performance, and shared savings attribution.

**On Blake’s radar:** Michael Greeley’s piece on Florida’s healthcare innovation ecosystem; Navvis named a leader in IDC MarketScape for value-based care consulting; June Hospitalogy Plus roundtable registration.

### 2. [🏥 The year of the clinical intelligence layer](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ktw5dbyb91ywyjcmat7r2c25) — *Hospitalogy* · *Jun 11, 2026*

Blake frames 2026 as the year ambient AI stopped being the product and became the front door for enterprise-wide clinical intelligence platforms. The anchor is Abridge’s keynote, then he works through physician compensation data and a pair of hard-hitting reports on MA economics and AI-enabled upcoding.

**Abridge’s move beyond the scribe:**
- After winning the ambient documentation category (300+ health systems, 100M+ conversations annually), Abridge announced a clinician intelligence platform that spans before, during, and after the visit.
- New moves: enterprise-wide rollout with Northwestern Medicine; CDS content collaborations with ADA, AAFP, AAN, ASCO on top of existing UpToDate/NEJM/JAMA integrations; coding-accuracy partnership with AHIMA; VBC alignment with Aetna; real-time claims pilots with Cigna; point-of-care clinical trial screening.
- The thesis: the scribe was the wedge. The destination is a layer of intelligence that routes documentation, reimbursement, prior auth, and evidence through increasingly automated workflows. Whether one company owns it or several share it is still open.

**Physician compensation trends (MGMA):**
- wRVUs fell in 16 of 23 specialties, total encounters fell in all 23 — yet median comp rose everywhere. Groups are paying for scarcity, not production.
- Real pay is roughly flat: against 16.4% cumulative CPI since 2021, surgical specialists broke even, primary care trailed slightly, nonsurgical lost ground.
- A chunk of the visit-intensity increase (wRVUs-per-encounter up 16.7% in primary care) is mechanical: the 2021 E/M revaluation plus G2211 inflated the ratio independent of clinical effort.
- Recruiting cooled at the edges; early-career surgical hires saw a 17.6% guaranteed-comp drop in one year. Over 80% of orgs now attach payback clauses to signing bonuses.
- The 2026 CMS efficiency adjustment — a 2.5% wRVU haircut on ~7,700 non-time-based codes — means procedural specialists will record fewer wRVUs for identical work starting January 1, mechanically shifting comp-per-wRVU benchmarks.
- AI’s report card: only 46% of practice leaders say AI tools made providers more productive. Ambient scribes are the one clear winner; everything else hasn’t translated into more visits or revenue.

**MA is now a capital allocation decision (A&M MA Pulse Survey):**
- 60% of health system execs see declining MA yields, 74% report rising denial rates, 52% are dealing with delayed payments and higher-than-expected utilization in risk-based arrangements. 100% expect moderate to significant financial pressure in 2026.
- The diagnostic flip: this isn’t a payer-relations problem, it’s a portfolio management problem. Systems running the “participate with everyone, manage it better” playbook are subsidizing payer margins while burning capacity on denial management.
- The response data shows bifurcation: 67% are pulling back from risk-sharing while 57% are leaning deeper into VBC. Some are exiting plan relationships entirely; others are concentrating on one or two payer partners they actually trust.
- A&M’s 4-pillar blueprint includes “evaluate every MA contract on total return.” The core provocation: treat the payer book like a portfolio, cut the losers without sentimentality, and only stay where reimbursement adequacy, authorization burden, denial risk, and payment timeliness clear a real bar.

**AI-enabled upcoding (BCBSA / Blue Health Intelligence):**
- At hospitals with high AI coding adoption, diagnoses of acute posthemorrhagic anemia in maternity admissions spiked 3–15%, while blood transfusions — the treatment that normally accompanies that diagnosis — rose only 0.7–0.9%. That single diagnosis shift added $22M to maternity admission costs in one year.
- BCBSA estimates roughly $663M in inpatient spending and $1.67B in outpatient spending may be tied to AI-powered coding tools. They’re building data infrastructure to detect these patterns. The audit and compliance environment is about to intensify.

### 6. [🏥 Weighing in on VBC and the impact of truly site-neutral care](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kv950gd2r4fbebf8d09k7082) — *Hospitalogy* · *Jun 16, 2026*

Another community-sourced discussion, this time on where organizations actually sit on value-based care and what true site-neutral care would require operationally. The answers reveal a gap between stated commitment and organizational readiness that the public conversation rarely captures.

**Where orgs sit on VBC:**
- **Difficult transition (Jason Hager):** Most organizations aren’t against VBC; they’re held back by the difficulty of navigating between models while FFS still funds the system. The operating model is starting to clarify at the primary care layer, which could accelerate the move from dabbling to commitment.
- **Fully committed with structural logic (Keith Pinter, AristaMD):** VBC aligns incentives around long-term outcomes for seniors, shifts documentation toward clinical impact rather than coding intensity, and PCPs are the right lever because they have deeper, trusted relationships. Rather than forcing FFS specialists into VBC constructs, enable PCPs to make better referral decisions — 30% of specialist consults prove unnecessary.
- **Eyes open with CEO cover (anonymous):** Genuinely committed in ACO REACH and DTE, but only works because the CEO came from a payer background and the board has patience. Most counterparts are one bad quarter away from the board re-prioritizing volume.
- **Frustrated payer-side view (anonymous medical director):** Providers keep treating VBC as a philosophical debate when the actuarial case is simple. But the real problem is that most provider organizations don’t have the data infrastructure, care management bandwidth, or coding discipline to succeed in two-sided risk — and then they blame the model. The gap between “VBC-aligned” and “VBC-capable” is enormous, and nobody talks about it honestly.

**What true site-neutral care would actually require:**
- **The disaster scenario (Nathan Kaufman):** Site neutrality applied to the current nonprofit hospital cost structure would crash margins — Medicare margins are already -13%, and 30–40% of hospitals are operating at a loss. The only response would be service reductions and layoffs.
- **The operational redesign (Jason Hager):** Kaufman is right that site neutrality as a rate cut alone is disaster. But the real work is the operating-model change underneath: stripping out hospital-level overhead from services that don’t need it and standardizing delivery across settings. That hasn’t been done consistently. So we’re debating a reimbursement model without the redesign that would make it viable.

### 8. [Why does your body make cholesterol if it kills us?](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kvb7cpqy848fycp49m0bewpk) — *The Next (Substack)* · *Jun 17, 2026*

A guest post by Justin Eaton, a non-physician with a family history of heart disease, who spent two years reading the primary literature on both sides of the cholesterol debate. He doesn’t land on a simple answer. He argues that the fight between the “LDL causes heart disease” and “LDL is blameless” camps is largely an artifact of using an outdated metric, LDL-C, and that the real driver is upstream metabolic dysfunction. The piece is worth reading in full for the specificity of the evidence weighed and the clarity of the mechanistic explanations.

**What both camps agree on: the heart attack event.**
- Atherosclerosis is slow plaque buildup, but the fatal event is vulnerable plaque rupture triggering a fibrin-and-platelet clot that blocks the artery. The disagreement is entirely upstream about how the plaque got there.
- Orthodox model: LDL particles cross the endothelium, get retained and oxidized, macrophages turn into foam cells, inflammation drives growth.
- Thrombogenic model (Kendrick): LDL cannot cross a healthy endothelial lining — damage from high blood pressure, smoking, hyperglycemia, or other factors must come first, and the body responds with tiny clots that get covered over, building plaque layer by layer over decades.

**Why the body makes cholesterol — and why “lower is always better” deserves scrutiny.**
- Roughly 75% of blood cholesterol is produced by the liver, not from diet. Cholesterol is structural for every cell membrane, the precursor for all steroid hormones, and constitutes ~25% of the brain by weight. The brain makes its own locally, not trusting systemic supply.
- Very low cholesterol shows association with depression and suicide attempts in meta-analyses (32 studies, 7,000+ patients). Placebo-controlled trials confirm statins lower testosterone.
- Eaton is careful not to say high LDL is good. He says the “lower is always better” framing needs to be interrogated.

**The metric problem: LDL-C is an archaic proxy.**
- LDL-C measures the volume of cholesterol inside LDL particles. It tells you nothing about particle count (ApoB), size, oxidation status, or Lp(a) — a separate genetic risk factor affecting ~20% of people that does not respond to statins.
- Small dense LDL particles enter artery walls more easily, oxidize more readily, and stay in the bloodstream longer. Oxidized LDL is what drives plaque formation; native LDL is largely benign.
- A 2009 AHA study found roughly half of 136,000 hospitalized CAD patients had LDL-C below 100 mg/dL. That alone makes the case that LDL-C alone is a crude tool.

**Metabolic dysfunction as the upstream master variable:**
- When cells become insulin resistant, the liver converts excess sugar and energy into fat, producing an overabundance of VLDL. CETP swaps triglycerides into LDL and HDL; hepatic lipase strips triglycerides away, collapsing LDL into small, dense, atherogenic particles. The same process breaks down HDL. High blood sugar glycates LDL, making it more oxidizable and harder to clear.
- A single upstream problem — insulin resistance — produces the classic high-risk lipid profile (high triglycerides, low HDL, small dense LDL) and is upstream of type 2 diabetes, obesity, Alzheimer’s, certain cancers, fatty liver disease, and depression.

**The statin evidence, framed both ways:**
- Pro-statin: CTT Collaboration meta-analysis shows 22% relative risk reduction per 1 mmol/L LDL drop. Mendelian randomization (Ference, 2012) shows that genetically lower lifetime LDL correlates with fewer events.
- Skeptic reframing using absolute risk: in secondary prevention, NNT = 39 over five years to prevent one non-fatal event, 83 to prevent one death. In primary prevention, NNT = 104 with no statistically significant mortality benefit. A meta-analysis calculated median postponement of death within trial duration at 3.2 days for primary prevention.
- Statins increase new-onset diabetes risk 9–13% and drop plasma CoQ10 25–50%, providing a biochemical mechanism for muscle pain. Most major trials used a run-in period that selected for tolerators; patient-level data is held by the CTT Collaboration at Oxford, which sits inside a unit that has received hundreds of millions in pharma funding.
- Eaton is not anti-statin. He’s anti-pretending they solve the problem they don’t really solve, and points to PCSK9 inhibitors, ezetimibe, and bempedoic acid as more logical targeted approaches.

**His personal decision: nattokinase.**
- Because both camps agree on the clot as the terminal event, he focused on fibrinolysis. Nattokinase is a fibrin-dissolving enzyme derived from fermented soy. A 12-month study of 1,062 participants showed a 36% reduction in carotid plaque size, plus improvements in triglycerides (-16.5%), LDL-C (-17.9%), and HDL-C (+17%).
- His own six-month nattokinase results (10,800 FU/day): triglycerides down 32%, LDL-C down 38%, ApoB down 20%, systolic blood pressure down ~10 points.
- He acknowledges no decades-long RCT exists and that one is unlikely because natural compounds lack the financial incentive. His closing thought: the research is genuinely unsettled, neither side should be as confident as it appears, and better testing and earlier engagement will let this generation reverse the heart disease trend.

### 11. [🏥 There is no labor shortage](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kvedp9546wy39424kq5zdpex) — *Hospitalogy* · *Jun 18, 2026*

Blake reports from ShiftMed’s Transformation Summit in Tampa with the core contrarian claim of the week: there is no nursing shortage. There’s a coordination problem and a packaging mismatch dressed up as one. The rest of the piece pulls through a series of provocative takes on labor, AI, consumerism, and the financial headwinds hospitals are about to walk into.

**The labor market is a misdiagnosis:**
- RN supply is increasing; nearly 1 in 4 working Americans is employed in a health system or hospital; hospital unemployment is 1.6%. Many states project an RN surplus. The real issue is fit: ~77% of clinician searches are for part-time or fractional work, while ~80% of postings are full-time. We’re advertising the opposite of what the workforce is looking for.
- Nurses over 55 have dropped from ~43% of the workforce to ~16% in five years — the most seasoned clinicians are exiting while the incoming Gen Z majority has completely different expectations. Flexibility is the new currency; pay ranks around #4 or #5.
- One system banked ~$130M in labor savings across 1M+ hours by leaning into flexible models. The operational version — going from 22 spreadsheets and a two-hour phone tree to filling a shift in 58 seconds — is plumbing, not moonshot. It’s the lowest-glamour, highest-ROI work in the building.

**Peak hospital mode and the margin headwind:**
- 2026 is the calm before the 2027 storm. Government payer mix has crept from ~40% to ~45%, operating margins slid from ~3.5% to ~2.5%. Over 42% of hospitals are losing money. Medicare service lines run at roughly -13% margin. The demographic freight train of 10,000 boomers per day turning 65 adds a structural mix-shift headwind of roughly a full margin point over five years.
- Layer on OBBB (~$1T out of the system), uninsured care climbing toward $85B, Medicaid work requirements, ACA subsidy lapses, and automatic re-enrollment going away — and you get a sector bracing for 2027.
- Blake’s valuation-guy observation: margin accretion in healthcare is almost always a short-term loan from the government. CMS claws it back through DRG reweighting, MA rebasement, and VBC benchmark moves. When a vendor tells you AI will permanently expand your margin, expect most of it to get recaptured by payers, government, and the vendor ecosystem.

**AI’s self-driving car problem:**
- Healthcare AI is judged against perfection, not against the deeply flawed humans it replaces. 40,000 annual road deaths are background noise; a Waymo gets stuck and it’s national news. Nearly every clinical AI study measures against an idealized ground truth, not against relative human performance (the tired resident at 3am).
- The bot wars: providers got to AI first (ambient scribes → more complete documentation → higher net revenue per adjusted admission). Payers are now firing back with billions in AI investment, more denials, and research exposing coding variation. The risk is automating and entrenching the worst parts of the system — prior auth becoming bot-versus-bot, with the same human friction running at machine speed.
- Over-reliance is a real failure mode. But the convergence point where it’s cheaper and lower-risk to deploy AI than not is coming, even if liability and reimbursement questions are unsettled.

**The workforce and the tool:**
- The most resonant take from the summit: “The clinician you want operating the AI is the one who learned to do it on pen and paper first.” Tools without underlying skill produce confident, sterile, wrong.
- On automating RCM: nobody had a straight answer on what happens when you can fire 2,000 people. “We’ll reskill them” is a cop-out. The honest version is that nobody knows, and that’s the story.

**Consumerism and the two-tier system:**
- ~30% of people are using AI for health questions; OpenAI alone fields ~40M health queries a day. That’s an existential rerouting of the front door. The patient without a PCP isn’t calling your access line — they’re asking a chatbot that will eventually be wired to a payer’s network that steers them somewhere that isn’t you.
- Primary care is stratifying into cash-pay longevity clinics for the affluent, APP-heavy MA models for everyone else, and an AI-enabled opportunity on the Medicaid/rural side. GLP-1s are torching elective surgical lines. The personal health record is getting another flood of venture and frontier-lab money. Systems that aren’t building their own consumer-facing front door are ceding it.

**What can’t be automated:**
- The best innovation story was a government agency renting Uber’s logistics rather than trying to invent rideshare from scratch. The organizations that win won’t be the ones with the most AI; they’ll be the ones flexible enough to absorb it while keeping the humans — clinicians and patients alike — believing the loop works.

- PwC’s mid-year 2026 outlook; GAO report finding consolidated MA plans deny the most requests and overturn most of them; McKinsey’s latest on the health system CEO imperative for AI; last call for the Context Healthcare Autonomy Conference.

## Themes

**The AI trust gap enters the clinic**
Three pieces document the same pattern from different angles: the technology works, but the deployment and trust layer is where things break. O’Leary’s ambient-scribe field report shows a skeptical late-adopter doctor pausing the recording because the tool medicalizes casual remarks and can’t be trusted as a system of record. Blake’s summit takeaway is that we judge AI against perfection when the real bar should be the flawed human it replaces, and that the risk is automating bot-versus-bot denials at machine speed. The eviCore lament — where a surgeon now misses the human prior auth reviewers because AI can’t handle ambiguity — completes the arc. The shared signal: the bottleneck isn’t model accuracy, it’s organizational design and clinician trust.  
*Articles: 2, 5, 9, 11, 14*

**VBC faces a capability crisis, not a philosophical one**
Across the Hospitalogy community thread, the Hawaii 3PC case study, and the A&M MA report, the same diagnosis emerges: the actuarial case for shifting risk is settled, but most provider organizations don’t have the data infrastructure, care management bandwidth, or coding discipline to survive in a two-sided model. The gap between “VBC-aligned” and “VBC-capable” gets named explicitly, and the Hawaii case shows what happens when you push a capitation model without the underlying redesign — utilization rises, access falls, and the payer retreats to FFS. The bifurcation in the A&M data (67% pulling back from risk while 57% lean in) reflects a market splitting between those who can execute and those who quietly exit.  
*Articles: 1, 5, 6*

**Hospitals are entering 2027 with a margin structure built on a short-term loan**
Blake’s observation — that margin accretion in healthcare is almost always a short-term loan from the government — cuts across the MGMA comp data, the Blues profitability report, the site-neutral care debate, and the ShiftMed summit consensus that 2026 is peak hospital mode. Medicare margins run at -13%, the government payer mix is creeping up by a full point of margin every five years, and CMS consistently claws back efficiency gains through reweighting and rebasement. The “coordination problem, not labor shortage” reframe doesn’t let anyone off the hook: it says the money you’re saving through flexible staffing and AI will get recaptured, so take advantage of the windfall while it lasts but don’t plan around it.  
*Articles: 2, 6, 11, 14*

**The employer market cracks open — and so does the political flank**
The OhioHealth anti-steering settlement, the White House report projecting 18% price reductions, the HCSC copay-only plan launch, the Marathon/Lantern partnership, and the ICHRA bull case all point in the same direction: the commercial market’s legal and competitive structure is shifting toward narrow networks and alternative plan designs. But the NPR piece on employer GLP-1 restrictions and the Oregon ED contracting drama signal that these moves carry real PR and regulatory risk. The same forces opening the market are also arming the critics with stories about access denied.  
*Articles: 5, 9, 12, 14*

## Notable Perspectives

- **“There is no nursing shortage. It’s a coordination problem.”** Blake’s Summit reframe: the RNs are there, but the shift from full-time postings to fractional demand, the generational shift, and the scheduling infrastructure haven’t caught up. This changes the solution set from “pay more” to “redesign the institution around the professional.”

- **“Healthcare AI has a self-driving car problem.”** We judge the machine against perfection and the status quo against nothing — 40,000 road deaths are background noise but a Waymo that hesitates is a scandal. The same thing happens with clinical AI: studies benchmark against an idealized ground truth, not against the tired resident at 3am. Until the bar shifts, tools that are already safer than what they replace will get slow-walked.
