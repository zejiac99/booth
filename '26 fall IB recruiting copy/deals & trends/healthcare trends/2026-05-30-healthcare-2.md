### 1. [How Resident Physicians Will Use AI Differently as Attendings](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ksdhhggwh09m5wbvx5yqyzeb) — *Healthcare Huddle* · *May 24, 2026*

- **Current AI use splits into two buckets.** As a resident, Dashevsky uses AI to automate clerical tasks — discharge summaries, transfer notes, inbox messages, prior auths — and as a clinical learning partner that pokes holes in his assessments, generates board-style questions, or walks him through PFT interpretation step by step.
- **The shift with attending status is about responsibility, not just knowledge.** Once the buck stops with him, AI must make his thinking sharper rather than doing the thinking for him. Clinical intuition born from owning outcomes can’t be replicated; the pattern library AI offers is useful, but judgment in a hypotensive RV-dysfunction crisis comes from feel and accountability, not retrieval. He expects his questions to become fewer and higher-order — not “teach me pneumonia” but “what’s the guideline footnote that matters for this exact patient.”
- **Signal-to-noise becomes the litmus test.** Early in training, more context feels helpful. Later, the seasoned clinician wants the answer, the caveat, and the one thing that changes the plan — with zero patience for fluffy, nice-to-know responses. The core want: AI that strips away low-value work and amplifies high-value work, freeing enough attention to be present, decisive, and human at the bedside.

### 2. [🏥 The Gen AI Juggernaut, Year 2](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ksk6kmn75wm790kztvc05g9z) — *workweek.com · Hospitalogy* · *May 26, 2026*

**Eric Larsen’s provocations on healthcare AI**

- **A deflation thesis:** Larsen argues healthcare will shed up to 700 basis points from its share of GDP as labor automation kicks in, with payers scrambling to catch up in the “bot wars.” The velocity of AI deployment — not just model quality — will separate winners and losers. Sutter Health is explicitly called out as a test case, with a prediction of more cross-market mergers to capture scale advantages in AI diffusion.
- **Job displacement is the conversation nobody’s having.** Larsen notes a vacuum around reskilling and asks what an AI-abundant future actually looks like under capitalism — a question the industry hasn’t begun to answer.
- **Liability is the clinical AI battlefield.** The winner in clinical AI, in Larsen’s view, will be whoever puts capital behind accepting clinical liability for AI-driven decisions, moving beyond the current state where everyone talks about augmentation but no one owns outcomes.

**Hospital Q1 2026 macro themes**

- **ACA subsidy erosion is progressing as modeled.** Same-facility exchange admissions fell 15% at HCA, 10% at Tenet, 5% at UHS (with reserving implying ~10–12%), and 3.9% at CHS. All operators expect the attrition to steepen as 2025 grace periods expire and silver-to-bronze migration compresses revenue per encounter.
- **Supplemental Medicaid payments delivered surprise upside.** Georgia’s grandfathered approval and Texas ATLAS reinstatement gave HCA $120M in unexpected Q1 benefit and CHS $25M. HCA raised full-year supplemental payment guidance by $200M. Florida approvals remain pending across the group, offering additional upside if granted.
- **Capital is pivoting hard toward ambulatory and technology.** Outpatient assets — urgent care, ASCs, freestanding EDs, virtual sitting, behavioral tech (Talkspace) — are the universal capital destination. Hospital-centric M&A is effectively over outside strategic infill; ambulatory is where growth and multiple expansion live.
- **AI moved from slideware to quiet operating discipline.** Tenet nearly doubled Conifer analytics productivity and built governance for pilot go/no-go decisions. UHS scaled 8 acute revenue cycle use cases. Ardent deployed virtual sitting across 2,000+ rooms. CHS and HCA are earlier in disclosed deployment, creating a compounding gap likely to surface in 2027 margins. Most investments remain in the unambiguous-ROI zone of revenue cycle management.
- **A weak flu season and January storms** cost the group $200M+ in EBITDA. Respiratory admissions fell 40–42% YoY across HCA, Tenet, UHS, and Ardent. All operators framed it as temporal, with March volumes returning to plan and full-year guidance reaffirmed.

### 3. [🏥 An infinite workforce for primary care?](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ksmzacdyz1q2yd938qagdqws) — *workweek.com · Hospitalogy* · *May 27, 2026*

*Sponsored deep dive on Lumeris’ Tom platform. Madden makes the case that Tom represents a genuine paradigm shift — the first credible attempt to build primary care’s operating system on frontier AI.*

- **Reactive vs. proactive is the fork in the road.** Most healthcare AI — scribes, copilots, search tools — is reactive: the human brings the agenda, the AI serves. Tom flips this. Tom wakes up with an objective (close a care gap, complete a follow-up, navigate symptoms) and works on the patient to accomplish it. The patient is the one being moved. This proactive-agent flavor is what actually changes the economics of care delivery because it scales the care team itself, not just the efficiency of the existing team. It is also dramatically harder to engineer — every conversation must navigate clinical guardrails, trust, and escalation judgment.
- **Pace is the moat.** The bar patients now use to judge healthcare’s digital experience is set by their banking app, Instacart, and daily ChatGPT use — not by the health system across town. Lumeris shipped Tom Native Audio roughly a month after Google released the underlying capability. If you’re a health system building internally or relying on a vendor that ships annually, you are already behind and accelerating away from the frontier. The competitive dynamic has shifted from health-system-vs-health-system to hyperscaler-cadence-vs-procurement-cycle.
- **Tom as an operating system, not another app.** Lumeris ships a coordinated stack of agentic capabilities on a shared substrate (the Tom persona, clinical reasoning layer, Hawkeye monitoring, data connectivity) with new capabilities every 2–3 weeks. The explicit decision not to build yet another healthcare app — and instead use communication modalities patients already trust — is a discipline most vendors lack. The deployment with the Collaborative for Healthy Rural America is the stress test worth watching: rural primary care is where the labor constraint has already broken.
- **The Google partnership is the underrated strategic asset.** It goes far beyond GCP credits. Lumeris gets pre-release access to frontier models (enabling the lockstep Native Audio launch), direct line-of-sight into Google’s healthcare research teams, and co-location in Kendall Square. The healthcare AI market will bifurcate sharply between vendors that absorb every model upgrade as a tailwind and those that picked a model in 2024 and are now stuck. Lumeris has placed a capital allocation bet on Google’s full-stack position from TPUs to data — a bet Madden considers one of the safer ones in tech.
- **Hawkeye watches everything; nobody watches your nurses.** Every Tom interaction — every word, reasoning step, pause, patient response — is logged and monitored across ~85 dimensions: clinical guideline adherence, conversational delays, patient intonation, guardrail compliance. The actual operational baseline of human-delivered care, by contrast, is a black box. Nobody reviews every nurse triage call. Nobody audits front-desk tone. Madden channels Zak Kohane’s “Compared to What?” argument: the safety bar we’re setting for AI is not being benchmarked against actual human care quality, which is far worse than the industry admits. It’s being benchmarked against an idealized clinician who doesn’t exist.
- **The infinite-workforce question reframes the entire strategy.** A health system CEO asked, “What would we do with an infinite workforce?” and almost nobody is asking it. The industry default is OpEx framing: Bob does five steps, AI does three, we need fewer Bobs. That produces modestly cheaper versions of the same business model. The real question — what changes when the binding constraint of human labor becomes unconstrained — touches panel sizes, follow-up cadence, preventive care economics, serviceable geographies, and the lines of business that become viable. This is the most consequential strategic split Madden sees this decade: cost-takeout shops vs. capability-unlock shops. He urges executives to look at the budget, not the strategy deck, to see which side they’re actually building toward.

### 4. [🏥 How Aetna is Redefining Itself](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ksraa619by8hxgrf3ht2mk1v) — *workweek.com · Hospitalogy* · *May 28, 2026*

*Madden interviews Dr. Ali Khan, CMO of Aetna Medicare — a rare payor executive who has been a hospitalist, startup operator, and division leader who scaled a clinic network from 23 to 75 sites during a pandemic. The conversation pressure-tests CVS’s vertical integration thesis through an operationally grounded lens.*

- **The VBC reckoning is a fork in the road.** Khan traces the arc: first-generation VBC (CareMore, Iora, ChenMed) was obsessively focused on total cost of care. The ACA changed the incentives — Stars money and risk adjustment became easier levers than the grinding clinical work of reducing total cost of care by 10%. The industry rationally chose the easier path. The result is millions of lives in “accountable care relationships” with PCPs still seeing 28 patients a day and doing 40 HCC queries in their pajamas after hours — a billing overlay on a broken workflow. Khan frames the current moment as a binary choice: the industry either responds to MA financial pressure by doubling down on genuine clinical transformation, or it doesn’t. No middle ground remains.
- **The analog bet.** Khan explicitly leads with care models, not AI. Aetna organizes around condition-based, cohort-based, and categorical frameworks — asking “what’s the right place of first contact for longitudinal coordination?” rather than defaulting to the PCP. The most concrete example is Signify Direct Connect: Signify Health clinicians doing in-home assessments now push alerts during the visit (this member is attributable to a VBC provider but has never engaged), dial the partner on the patient’s phone, and the partner commits to a 15-second SLA for answering. Khan claims 10–15x engagement lifts over traditional outreach — a brute-force analog fix to a problem the industry has spent a decade trying to solve with apps and portals.
- **Behavioral economics and the consumerism gap.** CVS has a behavioral economics team running A/B tests on text-based nudges across its retail pharmacy, MinuteClinic, and app surface area — not just flu shot reminders, but prompts like “talk to your doctor about cardiac rehab — bring them this message.” The cardiac rehab example reveals the coordination challenge: the nudge only works if paired with benefit design changes that make the program affordable, network design that makes it accessible within 7–10 days, and operational governance to track it. That’s four functions — behavioral science, product design, network ops, clinical governance — operating on 2–3 year return horizons with agile sprints. It’s a more sophisticated framework than most large payors run, though Khan acknowledges healthcare consumerism has “often fallen flat” because empowering people in asymmetric-information environments remains unsolved. The CVS thesis is that walking with people — digitally, analog, or with shoe leather — closes the gap. Madden calls it a show-me story.
- **Rebuilding trust at the bedside.** The payor-provider relationship is “decades of scar tissue.” Aetna’s Clinical Collaborative puts care management nurses back at the hospital bedside for collaborative discharge planning — a practice that was common in the 90s per diem era but disappeared under DRG-based payments. The nurse makes sure home health authorizations go through immediately, processes DME like shower chairs in parallel, and aims to be the “easy button” for hospital care managers. In oncology, Aetna is piloting prior auth bundling: all imaging, medications (including anti-nausea drugs), and related services for a cancer patient authorized upfront as a bundle. One Midwest health system saved dozens of days over six months that staff had been spending on appeals and peer-to-peers. Khan uses a surgical metaphor: rebuilding trust sometimes needs fine dissection, sometimes blunt force. It’s a long game.
- **M&A integration fails on time horizon and trust, not thesis quality.** Khan has lived through Anthem/CareMore, One Medical/Iora (and Amazon/One Medical), and CVS/Oak Street. His read: most deal theses are directionally correct. The synergies aren’t that far of an operational leap. But clinical operations need 18–24 months to percolate and show results, and acquirers either don’t give that runway or cycle leadership before the thesis can validate. CVS uses the term “enterprise athletes” for people who can connect dots across business lines — and Khan’s role is essentially being that connective tissue for the Oak Street, Signify, and broader CVS integration.

**What Blake’s recommending:**
- **Commonwealth Fund annual report:** The U.S. spends more than any OECD nation and still has the worst health outcomes among high-earning countries — now over 40 years of the same pattern.
- **His own article on credentialing and enrollment delays:** 90-to-120-day “provider readiness” delays are a strategic margin problem, not just an admin one.
- **Becker’s rundown of 20 large health systems getting bigger** through mergers, acquisitions, and partnerships announced or planned this year.

### 5. [Why Zocdoc Exists (and Why It’s So Hard to Replace)](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ksqcdwykr0sycq44yfrapj1h) — *Healthcare Huddle* · *May 28, 2026*

- **Zocdoc is a scheduling marketplace that bundles four broken layers.** Provider discovery and reputation. Directory and insurance normalization (solving the ghost-network problem — insurers don’t reliably update rosters, so the platform often becomes the de facto source of truth for who is actually in-network and accepting patients). Appointment inventory access (the hard part: integrating with EHR calendars or building a sync layer that reads availability and writes back confirmed appointments across messy scheduling tools). Conversion tooling (reminders, rescheduling, cancellation backfill, waitlists).
- **Demand aggregation is the real product.** Zocdoc captures high-intent traffic — patients searching by specialty, location, insurance, availability, ratings — and routes it to bookable supply. Once a platform owns discovery, ranking becomes an ad product. At that point, the business is selling demand with attribution: performance marketing for physicians, with pricing drifting toward whatever a specialty can afford to pay per acquired patient.
- **The business model follows the incentives.** Revenue comes from per-new-patient booking fees, sponsored placements, API/infrastructure products, and enterprise partnerships with health systems. The platform optimizes for completed bookings, not for clinical appropriateness or steering patients to the right level of care at the right time. Patients get speed and transparency; physicians and health systems get acquisition and backfill but risk dependency and commoditization as they sit directly alongside competitors.
- **The middleman survives until the system earns the right to be self-serve.** Dashevsky’s core argument: Zocdoc exists because directories are inaccurate and appointment inventory isn’t interoperable. Until payers, health systems, and EHRs fix those two structural problems — meaningfully addressing ghost networks and inadequate physician supply — the scheduling marketplace remains valuable and expensive.

## Themes

**The shift from reactive assistance to proactive, agentic care delivery**
Dashevsky’s resident AI use is entirely reactive: a tool that responds when summoned. Madden’s Lumeris piece draws the explicit contrast — Tom as an agent that wakes up with an objective and works the patient to close a gap — and argues this is the fork in the road that actually changes care-delivery economics. Khan’s Aetna strategy is analog but shares the proactive instinct: Signify clinicians initiating real-time handoffs, bedside nurses doing collaborative discharge planning, behavioral nudges prompting specific actions rather than waiting for the patient to come. Together, they suggest the industry is moving from AI as a background efficiency layer to AI (and process design) as an active participant that initiates and drives patient engagement. *Articles: 1, 3, 4*

**The infinite workforce question and the redefinition of labor constraints**
Lumeris’s framing — what would you do with an infinite workforce? — is the most distilled version, but the same question echoes across the batch. Dashevsky’s piece imagines AI freeing attending attention for the human, nuance-dependent work. Larsen’s deflation thesis predicts 700bps of GDP reduction through labor automation. Hospital Q1 results show capital fleeing inpatient toward ambulatory and technology, responding to a wage inflation problem that has no end. Khan’s Aetna doesn’t lead with AI but effectively addresses the same constraint: Signify as a force multiplier for engagement, care models that route patients to the right labor pool, prior auth bundling that removes hours of administrator time. The combined view: labor is the structural bottleneck, and the players that treat it as something to redesign around — rather than just optimize within — will reconfigure their business models, not just their cost structures. *Articles: 1, 2, 3, 4*

**The gap between consumer-grade expectations and healthcare’s delivery infrastructure**
Zocdoc exists because the system can’t do what an airline or restaurant booking app does: accurate availability, insurance confirmation, and one-click booking. Madden’s Lumeris piece names the competitive dynamic explicitly: patients now judge healthcare’s digital experience against their banking app and ChatGPT, not the health system across town. The Aetna conversation reveals the same tension — Signify Direct Connect is a brute-force analog hack for engagement because digital portals have failed to reach the populations they’re meant to serve, and behavioral economics nudge programs require coordination across product, network, and ops that most health systems can’t execute. The implication across all three pieces is that the “translation layer” — whether a marketplace, an OS-like care orchestration platform, or an embedded nurse-at-bedside program — is where value accrues until the underlying infrastructure becomes interoperable. *Articles: 3, 4, 5*

**The double standard in safety and monitoring standards for AI vs. humans**
Madden’s Hawkeye section makes the point directly: Tom is surveilled across ~85 dimensions in real time; no human clinical workforce is monitored at anything approaching that resolution. The “Compared to What?” framework (Zak Kohane) argues that the safety bar being set for AI is not the actual, error-laden baseline of human care but an idealized version that doesn’t exist. Dashevsky’s piece adds texture to the same logic: AI can’t own outcomes, and the seasoned physician’s judgment is what navigates the room with a crashing patient — but if AI is being rejected for its imperfections while human care is never audited with the same rigor, the policy conversation is distorted. Madden calls this a policy failure dressed as caution, with a body count attached to it in the form of patients who didn’t get care because the labor wasn’t there to deliver it. *Articles: 1, 3*

**Fragmentation as the enduring business opportunity**
Zocdoc is a toll on the fact that directories are wrong and scheduling is locked behind phone calls. Lumeris’s Tom is an operating system designed to orchestrate across fragmented care delivery — coordinating data, care plans, communication modalities, and clinician workflows. Aetna’s Clinical Collaborative inserts a payor-employed nurse at the bedside to bridge the information and authorization gap between hospital and plan. Every piece in this batch is, at some level, about a party stepping into the white space between silos and charging (or building moats) for the privilege of translating across them. The meta-pattern: fragmentation is the industry’s most durable value pool, and the entities that can aggregate demand, normalize data, or broker trust between adversarial parties keep getting built — and keep getting paid. *Articles: 3, 4, 5*

## Notable Perspectives

- **Eric Larsen’s 700bps healthcare GDP deflation call:** A provocative macro thesis that labor automation will shrink healthcare’s share of GDP, with deployment velocity as the separating mechanism. Worth pairing with Q1 hospital capital allocation data — if ambulatory and tech are where the money is flowing, the reallocation may already be underway. *(Article 2)*
- **“Compared to What?” as a regulatory and cultural wedge:** Zak Kohane’s framing, revived by JC Saghbini and amplified by Madden, argues that AI safety standards must be benchmarked against real human clinical performance, not an idealized clinician. This is the argument health systems and AI companies cannot make credibly on their own, which is why Madden positions himself as an independent voice willing to carry it. It will resurface every time an AI deployment generates a public safety incident. *(Article 3)*
- **Pace as moat:** The idea that matching hyperscaler release cadences matters more than model quality in the medium term. If your AI ships annually and your competitor ships weeks after every Gemini or GPT upgrade, you get lapped. This reframes the build-vs-buy decision for health systems and the partner selection logic for AI vendors. *(Article 3)*
- **The VBC industry’s original sin:** Khan’s insight that the ACA made Stars and risk adjustment the easier path — reducing total cost of care by 10% is a fundamentally harder, multi-actor problem than raising a Stars score by 10% — explains years of disappointing VBC results. It’s the most succinct diagnosis I’ve seen for why “accountable care” relationships often amount to a billing overlay. *(Article 4)*
- **Scheduling marketplaces as de facto directory truth:** Zocdoc solves the ghost-network problem by aggregating actual booking data, making it the real source of truth for in-network status and new-patient availability. That dynamic creates a defensible position — until insurers and health systems make their own directories reliable and interoperable, which nobody has done at scale. *(Article 5)*
