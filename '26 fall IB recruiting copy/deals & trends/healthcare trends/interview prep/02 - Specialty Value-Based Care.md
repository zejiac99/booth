# 2. Specialty value-based care — Interwell / Thyme Care

Prepared October 6, 2026. This is a separate path from primary-care ownership. The chain is **specialists managing costly patient journeys → strategic ownership and growth capital → follow a specialty care platform**.

## The specific thesis

Payers and specialist partners are moving selected oncology and kidney populations into arrangements that reward management of total care costs. The capability being funded is disease-specific care management, clinical engagement, data access, and credible risk underwriting. It may sit around existing physician practices rather than require owning those practices.

The proposition is more specific than “VBC is growing”: a primary-care team may coordinate a patient's care, but often cannot manage every specialist treatment decision or complication. Specialty platforms can intervene at those points. This complements primary care; it does not show that specialty VBC is replacing it.

## Mechanism and why now

A kidney or oncology patient can generate high spending beyond the specialist's office. Preventing an avoidable hospitalization, coordinating a transition, or changing an appropriate site of care can affect the whole patient journey. An ordinary management-services organization earns fees for supporting a practice; a specialty VBC contract adds financial accountability for defined cost and quality outcomes. Not every specialty MSO takes risk, and not every “value-based” agreement includes downside exposure.

**Why now:** cost pressure creates demand, while a larger share of specialist care remains outside risk contracts. More mature claims data and specialist networks make underwriting and coordinated interventions possible. They do not make the risk easy.

McKinsey's July 2025 analysis of 2020–2023 claims found six specialties accounted for 38% of direct medical spending in 2023. It estimated risk-bearing VBC penetration of 5% or less in oncology, cardiology, behavioral health, and women's health; nephrology was further along at about 28%. These are historical estimates with defined datasets, not a 2026 census. The article's projected savings are potential, not achieved results. [Original industry analysis](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/healthcare/our-insights/specialty-risk-the-next-frontier-of-value-based-care).

PwC's 2027 outlook projects a 9% **Group** medical cost trend from its health-plan research. This corroborates broad cost pressure but should not be presented as Humana's MA cost trend or a realized increase. [PwC's original report](https://www.pwc.com/us/en/industries/health-industries/library/behind-the-numbers.html).

### Rationale, with the structural nuance

**Buyer:** increase its economic participation in specialty VBC and strengthen coordination with its existing dialysis-services and products businesses. The parent already controlled Interwell; this was a deeper commitment, not the first creation of its VBC strategy.

**Sellers:** receive liquidity through accelerated settlement of previously granted put rights. The available evidence does not justify describing this as a fresh auction or saying investors exited because the business was failing.

**The technical follow-up worth knowing:** because Interwell was already consolidated, buying minority interests does not create 17 percentage points of new consolidated revenue. It changes ownership economics and the portion of profit attributable to minority holders. The 20-F records the cash flow in financing activities and the transaction in equity. Do not confuse this 2025 event with the 2022 Cricket–Interwell–Fresenius Health Partners merger.

**Price opinion:** the strategic commitment is defensible, but there is insufficient public information here for a firm “overpaid” judgment. Dividing €312 million by the approximate percentage purchased would produce only a rough pro-rata equity indication, not verified EV, and can mislead because the settlement arose from put rights. Do not use it as a comparable M&A multiple.

**Important limitation:** this is a real recent ownership transaction, but a less conventional interview M&A example than a public-target merger. Its structure is interesting; its public valuation and advisor disclosure are thin.

### What makes Thyme interesting

The local HTN analysis describes an asset-light model around existing oncologists, rather than a large physician-employment platform. It questions how quickly medical-spend risk expanded and whether benchmarking remains durable as payer partners renegotiate or build internally. Those are useful diligence questions, not independent confirmation of the newsletter's scenario probabilities.

The difficult part of oncology is drug economics. The digest estimates pharmaceuticals represent roughly 60% of oncology total cost of care and highlights buy-and-bill incentives. If most savings come from fewer emergency visits, they may be meaningful but limited. A platform cannot simply assume it will change drug economics without physician alignment.

## Hard follow-ups

**Why is this different from MaxHealth?** MaxHealth adds owned primary-care infrastructure and patient panels. Specialty VBC adds disease-specific decision support and risk management; physician ownership is optional.

**How does the platform make money?** Depending on the contract: care-management fees, shared savings, or a payment with defined cost-risk exposure. Net economics require subtracting care-team costs, technology, and any risk losses. Medical spend under management is not revenue.

**What is a credible savings benchmark?** An appropriate comparison population, acuity adjustment, clear attribution, adequate claims runout, and consistent treatment of trend and unusual cases. Otherwise selection or timing can look like care-model improvement.

**Strategic or PE?** These examples are strategic-led. Venture and PE investors can fund independent platforms, but payer/provider partners bring distribution and patient access. Independence may support multi-payer reach; strategic ownership may threaten that neutrality.

**Strongest counterargument?** Specialty drugs and severe disease may leave less controllable spending than advertised. Catastrophic cases, attribution overlap with PCP contracts, weak clinician engagement, and payer insourcing can erode economics.

**Your forward view?** Expect selective partnerships, minority investments, and eventual acquisitions of models with credible savings and durable contracts. Do not forecast a universal specialty-MSO rollup.
