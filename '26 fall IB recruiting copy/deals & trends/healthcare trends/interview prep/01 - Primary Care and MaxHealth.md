## Valuation points you can defend

**Oak Street:** $10.6 billion announced EV divided by approximately $2.16 billion of FY2022 revenue equals **about 4.9× FY2022 revenue**. This is our retrospective calculation: the full-year results were released February 28, 2023, after the acquisition announcement. FY2022 adjusted EBITDA was **negative $286.3 million**, so an actual trailing EV/EBITDA multiple is not meaningful. Revenue includes capitated medical spending; this is not comparable to a software revenue multiple. [FY2022 results](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1564406/000156440623000016/osh-2023x02x28x8kex991.htm).

The useful interpretation is that investors were underwriting future mature-clinic profitability and growth rather than positive current EBITDA. A high revenue multiple alone does not establish overpayment, and a goodwill write-down is not a transaction multiple.

**MaxHealth:** approximately $941 million of consideration is verified, but target earnings and a clean EV bridge are not established in these public materials. No defensible public EBITDA multiple or claim of a distressed purchase follows from the price alone.

**The Villages:** $68 million was the winning bankruptcy-auction bid, not $64 million and not a clean whole-company EV. Describe a bankruptcy asset purchase that continued the care business, rather than saying the clinics were simply liquidated. The detailed sources and deal facts appear below.

## The specific thesis

Insurer-owned senior-care platforms are selectively acquiring established primary-care practices to obtain existing patients, clinicians, and local care-delivery infrastructure. The attraction is faster access to a functioning care model and greater density in a market, with the potential to manage patients' total medical costs. This is a more defensible MaxHealth thesis than “healthcare services valuations are low, so buyers are purchasing distressed providers.”

The acquisition is verified. Local density, faster scaling, and potential medical-cost benefits are our strategic interpretation; the reviewed disclosures do not quantify those synergies or establish that the seller was distressed.

## Mechanism and why now

1. A primary-care platform can influence care outside its own clinics through prevention, chronic-disease management, referrals, and follow-up after discharge. Under a risk contract, lower total medical spending can improve the platform's economics, subject to contract terms and quality performance.
2. Building a clinic requires recruiting clinicians and filling patient panels before its fixed costs are well absorbed. Acquiring an established practice brings those relationships at closing, although retention and integration remain uncertain.
3. A strategic owner can spread technology, care-management staff, contracting, and administrative infrastructure across more local patients. The purchase creates value only if these benefits exceed the acquisition price and integration costs.

**Why this is current:** reimbursement-model changes and continued medical-cost pressure make operating performance more important. CMS completed the three-year phase-in of the 2024 CMS-HCC model in 2026. That is a change in risk adjustment, not evidence that aggregate MA payments fell: CMS projected a 5.06% average payment increase for 2026. [CMS rate announcement](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/2026-medicare-advantage-part-d-rate-announcement).

**Independent evidence:** a June 2025 Health Affairs Scholar study found that payer-operated practices' share of Medicare primary-care service volume grew from 0.78% in 2016 to 4.2% in 2023. The definition includes some joint ventures and operating partnerships, and the data cover both traditional Medicare and MA. This establishes expansion of payer-operated care, not that ownership itself reduces costs. [Original research](https://academic.oup.com/healthaffairsscholar/article/3/7/qxaf120/8160115).

**Current deal-market context:** KPMG's September 2026 report describes selective healthcare capability acquisitions and network expansion rather than indiscriminate consolidation. This supports the framing, but is broader than senior primary care. [H1 2026 healthcare M&A report](https://kpmg.com/us/en/articles/mergers-acquisitions-trends-healthcare-life-sciences.html).

### Buyer rationale and seller rationale

**Buyer:** obtain a functioning Florida platform, established patient and clinician relationships, and an opportunity to extend CenterWell's operating infrastructure. The closing announcement explicitly supports footprint expansion. Better referral coordination, shared infrastructure, and medical-cost improvement are potential benefits to underwrite, not disclosed realized synergies.

**Seller:** Arsenal realizes an investment in a platform it helped assemble, while founders and management gain access to CenterWell's resources. Arsenal identifies its investment year as 2020. The available announcements emphasize growth and strategic fit; they do not establish liquidity distress or a forced exit. [Arsenal portfolio history](https://www.arsenalcapital.com/portfolio/MaxHealth).

### Your opinion on price

A good answer is: **“I like the strategic fit, but I cannot defend a cheap or expensive multiple without standalone earnings and an EV bridge.”**

A purchase price is not enough to judge value. Normalize contract economics for risk-model changes, patient mix, claims development, and mature versus growing panels. Then value sustainable cash flow and add only achievable synergies. Do not divide the purchase price by Humana's or CenterWell's EBITDA: neither is the target denominator.

If asked what matters most, prioritize patient/physician retention, total medical cost per patient, the quality of payer contracts, and the ability to integrate the acquired platform without disrupting care.

## Hard follow-ups

**Why buy rather than build?** Acquisition brings existing relationships immediately, but at an upfront premium and with integration risk. De novo clinics offer greater design control but require a panel-building period. A balanced strategy can use both.

**Strategic or PE?** Strategic-led at exit: CenterWell is the buyer. PE participated in building the seller's platform. A strategic can capture benefits across care delivery and insurance; PE typically relies more directly on provider cash flow, growth, and a later exit.

**Does owning primary care guarantee lower costs?** No. A practice must change utilization while preserving quality. Risk adjustment, patient selection, local market power, and contract structure can distort apparent savings.

**What could break the thesis?** Loss of patients or clinicians, unfavorable contracts, higher acuity, integration disruption, or cost savings that are weaker than assumed.

**What would you buy next?** An adjacent established practice with durable clinicians and strong risk-contract performance, or a specialty partner that closes a specific care gap. Start with the capability and economics before naming a target.
