### 1. [Fixing Baby Formula - The Next #67](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgrqpn0d1ghahz5dpshxaw2) — *Justin Mares*

Justin Mares opens with a meditation on building things from scratch — Stripe at 40 people, Kettle & Fire’s ten-year journey to a $100M+ brand — before pivoting to his new target: American baby formula. The regulatory hook is the Infant Formula Act of 1980, which mandated linoleic acid from seed oils in all formula, effectively illegalizing alternatives even as evidence mounts linking formula-fed infants to rapid weight gain, higher obesity, and lower IQ. He cites a Consumer Reports finding that half of tested formulas contained harmful levels of lead, arsenic, or PFAS, and contrasts lax U.S. standards against EU regulations that keep cleaner European formulas out through FDA labeling and import barriers. Mares is now backing Julia Lipton’s OpenLabel Research project to test popular formulas for heavy metals, glyphosate, BPA, phthalates, mycotoxins, and actual nutrient content, with results to be published freely at fixtheformula.org.

The business section is a ten-year Kettle & Fire retrospective: started as a side hustle targeting $10k/month, self-funded with a $20k loan from parents, a cold email to Mark Cuban that led to their manufacturing partner, an initial mistake of refusing to spend on retail marketing, expansion into every Whole Foods in the U.S. 18 months later, fundraising, building their own production facility (which nearly killed the company), and eventually reaching 20k+ stores and 50+ employees. The early emails and investor updates are being opened up later, a move that could make the post a resource for consumer health founders.

### 2. [Why are the Amish so healthy? The Next #68](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgrqpjm9hpnfrcgy3bp6hn0) — *Justin Mares*

Two separate threads: first, a dissection of how the FDA’s 1997 guidance on consumer-directed broadcast advertisements unlocked the direct-to-consumer pharma advertising boom — spending doubled from $310M to $664M in one year, now exceeding $6B annually — and how this helped create a populace where newborns today are expected to spend 37–48 years of their lives on prescription drugs. Only the U.S. and New Zealand allow DTC pharma ads. Mares frames this as pharma effectively owning the media (27% of primetime news ad spend), which he argues explains why negative coverage of vaccine injuries or Ozempic’s downsides has been suppressed.

The second segment treats the Amish as a natural experiment in avoiding modernity. Cancer rates are 40–56% lower than national averages. Childhood obesity: 4% versus 36.5%. Asthma: 1–2% versus 8–10%. Type 2 diabetes at half the rate despite a high-calorie, animal-product-rich diet. Amish men average 18,425 steps per day. They’re not trying to be healthy — no Amish CrossFit, no Huberman — yet their environment free of electricity, estrogenic plastics, polyester, and the standard array of industrial toxins produces radically better outcomes. Mares treats this not as a model to emulate but as evidence that population-level health is a function of environment, not genetics — and that the “there’s nothing we can do about chronic disease” narrative is false.

### 3. [Product recommendations for a healthier life - The Next #69](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgrqpgdrpb1sez468s3b8r2) — *Justin Mares*

The supplement section flags that 4 of 6 creatine gummy brands tested contained no creatine at all, a reminder of how much fraud sits inside the wellness market.

### 4. [A New Funding Round, and is Bread Healthy? The Next #70](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgrqpe55f61mm3c522myzgv) — *Justin Mares*

The lead item is Truemed’s $34M Series A led by a16z, with Bessemer, Long Journey, Box Group, and Trust participating. Mares frames the company’s mission — letting HSA/FSA dollars flow to exercise, dietary interventions, supplements, and other root-cause lifestyle interventions — as a structural fix to a system where ~90% of U.S. healthcare spend goes to managing, not curing, chronic disease. The bet: lifestyle interventions are often more effective than pills and surgeries for chronic conditions, but the infrastructure to fund them through the existing healthcare system doesn’t exist. Truemed is building that infrastructure.

The health segment rehabilitates bread, but only if it’s freshly milled, long-fermented sourdough made from organic whole grains. Industrial milling strips 80% of the fiber and 76% of vitamins; modern flour goes rancid within weeks as polyunsaturated fats oxidize. Traditional sourdough fermentation reduces phytic acid by 62–95%, making minerals bioavailable. The real point is a macro critique: the food system has been so degraded that “bread is bad” is the wrong conclusion — the right one is to demand better sourcing and preparation.

The business section maps an investment thesis: roughly 80% of U.S. food is controlled by 12 companies, whose scaled, centralized supply chains are becoming liabilities as consumers shift toward well-sourced, healthy products. Only a handful of challenger brands — Vital Farms (IPO), Chobani (rumored IPO), GT’s Kombucha — have grown large without selling to Big Food. The opportunity Mares sees is a holding-company-style competitor to Big Food that turns scale advantages into disadvantages, building a localized, nutrient-dense, low-pesticide alternative. He explicitly invites pitches from anyone building this.

### 5. [Inside TrumpRx: GoodRx Coupons, Who Profits, and Real Drug Costs](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kq50xesbkkbvke592spsjegm) — *Healthcare Huddle*

Jared Dashevsky dissects TrumpRx — the administration’s new “most-favored-nation” drug pricing portal covering 43 drugs from Pfizer, Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk, AstraZeneca, and EMD Serono — and finds it to be a branded wrapper on existing infrastructure, not a new model. Roughly 80% of the program runs on GoodRx’s PBM-powered coupon infrastructure: when a patient uses a TrumpRx coupon, the claim still runs through a PBM, the pharmacy pays a transaction fee, the PBM shares a cut with GoodRx, and presumably the government now gets a split. The remaining 20% simply redirects patients to pharma D2C platforms like LillyDirect or AstraZeneca’s portal.

The analysis is useful less for the political branding than for what it reveals about drug pricing architecture. Farxiga costs $180 through TrumpRx (routed to AstraZeneca’s D2C) versus $288 on GoodRx. Protonix brand is $200 via the TrumpRx coupon; the generic costs $6 at Cost Plus Drugs. The real winners are PBMs and coupon platforms collecting transaction fees; independent pharmacies continue to absorb the financial hit. Medicare and Medicaid patients cannot use the platform. Dashevsky concludes that without addressing the underlying PBM rebate and spread-pricing incentive structure, this is a consolidation play, not a systemic fix.

### 6. [The GLP-1 Compounding Crackdown](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kq50xepf8gjttve69r36n9jn) — *Healthcare Huddle*

Hims & Hers announced a compounded semaglutide pill at $49/month (first month) and $99/month thereafter — $100 less than Novo’s Wegovy pill — and within 72 hours, the HHS general counsel referred them to the DOJ for FDCA violations, the FDA threatened to restrict GLP-1 active ingredients for compounding, Hims & Hers retreated, and Novo filed a patent infringement lawsuit. Dashevsky’s frame: this is not an isolated event but a preview for the entire D2C weight-loss industry now that semaglutide and tirzepatide are off the FDA drug shortage list and compounding pharmacies can no longer mass-produce them.

The business stakes are visible in the numbers: Hims & Hers stock dropped from $63 in October to $15 in mid-February as the FDA sent violation letters to dozens of compounding pharmacies. Dashevsky outlines three survival paths — partner directly with pharma, build comprehensive care models like Virta Health (medication plus nutrition, coaching, behavioral support), or pivot to another high-margin category — and argues the companies that survive will be those that built real clinical infrastructure, not regulatory arbitrage. The clinical reality is stark: 84% of patients without diabetes stop GLP-1s within two years, and the compounding crackdown will accelerate that cliff while eroding trust in both digital health companies and the regulatory apparatus.

### 7. [The Peptide Wave - The Next #72](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kjwstt1vd8cyh71n91gc1q5h) — *Justin Mares*

Mares lays out a structural critique of the pharmaceutical industry’s business model: because companies can only patent novel molecules — not compounds the human body already produces — the system systematically ignores peptides, which are naturally-occurring signaling molecules. Research shows peptide-based drugs are ~2x more likely to be approved in clinical trials than novel small-molecule approaches, not because of better science but because they work with evolutionary biology rather than against it. Yet the $2.6 billion average drug development cost, justified by 20-year patent monopolies, makes unpatentable peptides a non-starter for pharma. The exception that proves the rule: semaglutide, a slightly modified GLP-1 peptide that became one of the most successful drugs in history.

The forward-looking argument hinges on what Mares calls “the monitoring unlock”: while peptides lack long-term human trial data, the explosion of consumer-accessible diagnostics — CGMs, Function/Superpower biomarkers, Prenuvo scans — means individuals can monitor for side effects with far greater precision than the traditional trial infrastructure allows. This shifts the risk/reward calculus for compounds like BPC-157, TB-500, and the “Wolverine Stack.” He’s careful to flag the risks (gray-market Chinese peptides, zero quality control, unstudied stacking interactions, potential cancer links) but sees the combination of better detection, cheaper diagnostics, and a popular revolt against the chronic-disease industrial complex as the conditions for a new biotech paradigm that moves from “don’t die” molecules to “more life” optimization. The investment implication is that the companies building the monitoring, legalization, and quality-control infrastructure around peptides will capture value that pharma’s patent moat has historically blocked.

### 8. [Where Americans Get Care in 2026](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kq50xfpx33ac5r5b5kyjd5ry) — *Healthcare Huddle*

CDC data for 2024 shows 90% of adults have a usual source of care, but the composition is shifting: 12.2% of 18–34 year olds use urgent care or a retail clinic as their usual source, versus 4% for those 65+. Urgent care centers have nearly tripled since 2010 while primary care physician density dropped 22%. Dashevsky maps the converging trends: a projected shortfall of up to 70,610 PCPs by 2038, rural areas averaging 5 PCPs per 10,000 versus 8 in urban, a 7% decline in primary care visits among commercially insured patients from 2018–2024, and behavioral health visits surpassing primary care visits for the first time (66.4M vs. 62.8M).

The near-term solution he endorses is agentic AI that continuously monitors a physician’s entire patient panel, identifies high-impact actions, and autonomously handles outreach, care-gap closure, and chronic disease management — specifically Lumeris’s “Tom” platform. The structural argument is that PCP supply cannot ramp fast enough, so the only realistic lever is making existing panels more productive. This sits at the intersection of two of his recurring themes: the primary care access crisis and AI as an operational force multiplier.

### 10. [Pearl Health: The VBC Math That Should Change Your Mind](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kqd7d0yrg94y32vhx9ftx74r) — *Hospitalogy*

Blake Madden’s sponsored deep dive with Pearl Health’s CEO Michael Kopko distills six operational lessons for health systems entering value-based care, but the piece’s real contribution is an economic reframing that should shift how CFOs evaluate risk-bearing contracts. The core argument: the utilization that VBC is designed to reduce — avoidable readmissions, ED visits for ambulatory-sensitive conditions, poorly managed chronic disease — is “overwhelmingly terrible-margin care” for health systems. A 93-year-old who missed a pneumococcal vaccine and ends up in the ICU for three weeks generates a low-rent DRG on a Medicare fee schedule that doesn’t cover the stay’s cost. A CHF readmission within 30 days tells the same story. Dennis Hillen (ex-Humana, ex-Oscar Health) makes the point explicit: clinicians don’t treat patients differently based on who’s paying, so the complexity is structural — and the structural economics, when run honestly, often favor the value-based approach. This moves VBC out of the “mission vs. margin” trap and into a pure margin-improvement argument.

The six lessons trace the operational path: (1) risk without real-time claims visibility is a bet, not a strategy; (2) benchmark design — especially MSSP’s methodology that rewards historical inefficiency — determines financial outcomes more than care delivery; (3) physicians act on clarity, not on undifferentiated data; (4) the 5% of patients who drive 50%+ of cost are the hardest to manage and require clinical resources most primary care environments lack; (5) downside risk creates alignment that upside-only shared savings never does; (6) the first year’s objective is not maximum margin but an “undeniable win” that builds organizational confidence.

Pearl’s three-bucket framework makes the technology argument concrete: get into the right arrangement (program-agnostic actuarial matching), drive performance (peer-reviewed data showing 2–4% better MLR than Medicare benchmark in year one), and — most distinctively — cut the cost of doing VBC. Traditional enablers (Agilon, Privia, Aledade, Wellvana) deploy human labor — care managers, coders, disease management nurses — that can consume 60–80% of shared savings. Pearl’s bet is on agentic AI to handle patient prioritization and administrative workflows, claiming a 20–40% reduction in operational labor costs. If that holds at scale, it’s a structural unlock that makes VBC pencil for systems that have been running the numbers and coming up short.

The AI section, without hype, breaks into three layers: insight generation (table stakes), agentic task allocation (scheduling, follow-ups), and data portability — the idea that AI agents communicating across EHR walls could become the new interoperability layer, directly threatening EHR incumbents’ moats. This is the most speculative but potentially transformative claim in the piece.

### 12. [Photon Just Killed the “E” in E-Prescribing](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kqg92et47mw9h8a108r6ycgf) — *Hospitalogy*

Blake Madden’s second sponsored deep dive makes the case that health systems are leaving $50M–$250M in annual gross margin on the table by treating ambulatory pharmacy as a fulfillment cost center rather than the highest-frequency consumer touchpoint in the enterprise. Photon, which just raised a $16M Series A led by Healthier Capital (Amir Dan Rubin’s firm), built its consumer-facing prescription marketplace by first serving DTC virtual-care brands during the cash-pay GLP-1 wave — which means it has consumer-product DNA, not enterprise-vendor DNA. When health systems began inbounding, pharmacy leaders realized the interface Photon built for a startup weight-loss brand was the thing they had been failing to ship inside their EHR for a decade.

The unit economics: an average retail Rx generates $6–$14 in fully loaded margin; the typical ambulatory patient fills 12–15 scripts per year. Multiply by a mid-sized IDN’s 500,000-patient panel, and leakage rates of 60–80% to retail chains and mail-order translate into a margin loss equivalent to the EBITDA of an entire mid-sized service line. Post-discharge fill rates compound the argument: a patient who fills their post-MI regimen within seven days is dramatically less likely to be readmitted, and avoiding one preventable readmission is worth $15K–$25K under VBC contracts or penalties. Photon’s engagement platform — which surfaces price, stock availability, and fulfillment options across pharmacies — posted 60–70% engagement on routed prescriptions and sell-through above 90% when consumers picked a pharmacy through it.

The strategic framing that Madden amplifies is that ambulatory pharmacy is the consumer engagement engine, not specialty. A patient filling a statin at the system pharmacy in 2026 is materially more likely to fill a specialty Rx at the same system in 2034. The CPO of a prominent academic medical center framed this as the “Catholic Church” strategy: own baptism, first communion, confirmation, marriage, and last rites — the full longitudinal relationship — not just maximize margin on weekly collection plates.

The venture lens: Healthier Capital’s oversubscribed $220M Fund 1 closed in January 2026, 47% above its $150M target, in a year when global venture fundraising hit an eight-year low. The bet is that the next generation of consumer-front-door healthcare assets gets built around high-frequency Rx engagement rather than annual primary care visits. Madden flags the enterprise sales complexity — the deal requires buy-in from pharmacy, CIO, marketing, CX, and potentially the payor side — and the open question of whether the model scales to the 150-bed community hospital with one IT person. But the strategic asymmetry is clear: health systems, unlike every other pharmacy competitor, are clinically aligned with the patient’s outcomes, which gives them a structural LTV ceiling that CVS, Walgreens, or Amazon cannot match.

### 15. [A Physician’s Guide to Drug Pricing & the Supply Chain](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kqt21qbdhp7c1favtbqqvnmc) — *Healthcare Huddle*

This is a teaser for a paid guide, but the free portion provides a tight summary of the drug supply chain’s six key players and the core incentive distortion: PBMs control 80% of U.S. prescriptions and profit from rebates tied to list prices, creating a structural incentive to favor expensive drugs over cheaper alternatives. Rebates reduce the insurer’s net cost but almost never reduce patient copays at the point of sale, leaving patients exposed to list-price-based cost sharing. The guide explicitly highlights Cost Plus Drugs as proof that the system can work differently — transparent pricing, no rebates, no spread pricing — and positions market-driven solutions as moving faster than policy (Inflation Reduction Act, Medicare negotiation). The promotional framing is clinician-empowerment: knowing when to check cash prices, how to write effective prior authorization appeals, and where to direct patients for assistance.

## Themes

**The PBM/Rebate Architecture as the Persistent Core of Drug Pricing Dysfunction**
Dashevsky’s TrumpRx analysis, the physician’s guide, and Kevin O’Leary’s earnings commentary all locate the PBM rebate-and-spread model as the structural obstacle that makes incremental reforms hollow. TrumpRx is a GoodRx wrapper — the same PBM transaction fees and opaque adjudication — while Mark Cuban’s simultaneous partnership with Humana and anti-vertical-integration rhetoric reveals that even disruptors currently need PBM-scale distribution. The takeaway across the batch is that the rebate system is not being dismantled; it is being repackaged, branded, and occasionally partnered around, but no single policy or startup move has yet changed the underlying flow of money.

**The Economics of VBC Are Better Than the Skeptics Think — If You Can Cut the Cost of Doing It**
Pearl Health’s margin reframe — that the avoidable utilization VBC targets is already terrible-margin care under FFS — converges with the earnings signals: risk-bearing payors (United, Elevance, Molina) outperformed in Q1 while providers (HCA) saw volume-driven misses. Pearl’s claim that technology-first enablement can reduce operational labor costs by 20–40% directly addresses the trap where VBC enablers consume 60–80% of shared savings in human infrastructure. If agentic AI can shrink the delivery cost of population health management, the CFO math flips for the “Camp One” skeptics Madden describes — and the recent policy pressure (LEAD ACO, involuntary bundles) makes waiting costlier.

**Consumer-Prescription Interfaces Are Becoming the New Front Door — and an LTV Battleground**
Photon’s thesis, the TrumpRx portal (however thin), and the compounding crackdown that is forcing D2C weight-loss companies to either partner with pharma or pivot all converge on the same insight: the prescription moment is being reengineered as a consumer engagement event with downstream LTV implications. Photon argues ambulatory pharmacy is the highest-frequency trust-building touchpoint; TrumpRx attempts to capture that trust under a government brand; the GLP-1 market showed that patients will route scripts to whoever offers the best combination of price, speed, and experience. The strategic question for health systems and pharma is who owns that interface — and whether the infrastructure to monetize it can be built before Amazon or a VC-backed marketplace locks in the relationship.

**The “Health Optimization” Paradigm Is Building Its Own Parallel Supply Chain — Outside the FDA-Pharma-PBM Triangle**
Mares’s peptide thesis, Truemed’s HSA/FSA routing, the compounding crackdown’s exposure of regulatory arbitrage, and the consumer monitoring infrastructure he describes (CGMs, Function/Superpower biomarkers, Prenuvo scans) all point to a health economy forming at the edges of the traditional reimbursement system. Peptides are unpatentable and therefore ignored by pharma, but consumer demand and self-pay models are building a market anyway. The compounding crackdown shows that this parallel system runs into hard regulatory limits when it gets large enough — but the monitoring tools that enable risk-taking without traditional clinical trials change the argument for how this market can grow legally over time. The investment question is which companies will build the quality control, legal pathway, and distribution infrastructure for this parallel supply chain, and whether incumbents will attempt to absorb it (as Novo is doing with litigation) or ignore it until it is too large to suppress.
