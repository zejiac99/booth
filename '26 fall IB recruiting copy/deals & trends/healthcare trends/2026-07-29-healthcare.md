### 1. [🏥 Healthcare mid-year public market update](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kvtqk43rs8edp9vncbmqf517) — *Workweek (Hospitalogy)* · *Jun 23, 2026*

Blake Madden rolls out a custom “Hospitalogy Index” of ~75 healthcare services, distribution, and tech names and benchmarks it against the S&P 500. The sector is underperforming the broader market, but the dispersion underneath is what matters.

- **The index underperformed the S&P 500 by a notable margin.** Managed care and value-based care names were the bright spots. agilon, P3 Health Partners, Clover, and Oscar all posted strong returns. Clover benefited from its Stars lawsuit win; Oscar outperformed even amid a shrinking ACA market.

- **Hospital valuations have fallen sharply from the October 2024 peak.** Forward EV/EBITDA multiples for the publicly traded operators declined across the board, with a broad selloff in Q1 2026. The forward-looking nature of equity markets is pricing in uncertainty around AI investment execution, cost restructuring, and the 2027 headwind stack (site-neutral, OBBBA, Medicaid redeterminations). Madden notes multiples are near 5-year lows and asks whether things are really *that* bad.
- **Sub-sector breakout shows the market is rewarding the rebounding payers and VBC plays, not the providers.** The performance gap between managed care and hospital names that widens later in the month (see articles 11–15) is already visible here.

### 2. [🏥 ACA exits and ambulatory utilization trends](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kw07c5r1xfw832n1tvfczfpp) — *Workweek (Hospitalogy)* · *Jun 25, 2026*

Two tight data stories: shrinking insurer participation on the ACA Marketplace, and persistent MA ambulatory utilization that refuses to revert to pre-pandemic norms.

**ACA Marketplace participation turns down for the first time since 2021.**
- Average carriers per state fell from 9.6 to 9.0. Counties with only a single insurer option nearly doubled, from 93 to 165.
- The primary driver was Aetna CVS’s full exit from 17 states. UnitedHealth pulled back sharply in Kansas and South Carolina while expanding in Oklahoma. Centene exited North Carolina entirely through its WellCare subsidiary — surgical footprint recalibrations, not panic.
- The expiration of enhanced subsidies shrank the risk pool. KFF estimates effectuated enrollment could drop ~5 million, and healthier enrollees leave first, pushing adverse selection pressure onto the remaining carriers. Madden flags the downstream threat for health systems whose payor mix depends on ACA coverage.

**S&P Capital IQ data shows MA utilization still elevated.**
- Title XVIII Medicare encounters as a share of total health enrollments hit 8.1% in Q1 2026, up from 5.4% in 2016 and still above the pre-2025 range. On a last-12-months basis, encounters reached 30.2% vs. 21.8% in 2016.
- Insurers are leaning on AI to tighten adjudication and payment integrity, but the ambulatory-driven intensity — downstream testing, specialist follow-on, risk-adjustment economics — hasn’t shown clean reversion. Medicare remains the margin risk layer for managed care.

### 3. [Weekly Health Tech Reads 6/28/2026](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kw7dxqf9vjg4mrw66ktfqw7y) — *Health Tech Nerds* · *Jun 28, 2026*

Kevin O’Leary leads with the agentic voice AI land grab, then moves through a set of threads that collectively mark the summer’s tightening tension between healthcare’s innovation promises and its structural frictions.

**Agentic voice AI: Prosper and Assort land big rounds, but the durability question is open.**
- Prosper AI raised $30M from a16z; Assort Health raised $120M at a $1.2B valuation from Menlo Ventures. Both report dramatic growth (Prosper 5x revenue in six months; Assort 20x in 15 months, implying ~$60M ARR).
- Customer quotes in both press releases claim each is the only platform to meet their needs after a comprehensive market evaluation. O’Leary is skeptical: the platforms’ listed capabilities (scheduling, eligibility, billing) look identical, and a real-world test of Assort’s agent after its prior raise didn’t inspire confidence.
- The underlying dynamic is real — PE-backed specialty practices are desperate for admin support, and the short-term ROI is tangible ($100k+ EBITDA improvements, $1M+ labor savings). The question is whether this is a genuine platform market that supports multiple multi-billion-dollar outcomes, or a temporary land grab where differentiation collapses as automated answering becomes table stakes. O’Leary leans toward the latter; Assort’s big raise suggests a roll-up strategy aimed at FOMO-driven acquisition.

**Chart of the Week: physician supply vs. wait times is counterintuitive.** Joel Selanikio shows Boston has the longest average physician wait times among large metros, even though Massachusetts has the most physicians per capita. More supply doesn’t automatically mean better access — allocation, payer mix, and measurement matter.

**Other notable items:**
- **Cityblock acquiring Homeward Health.** Homeward launched in 2022 with $20M from General Catalyst, raised another $50M at a $345M valuation, and is now being absorbed by a fellow GC portfolio company. The narrative about rethinking rural care delivery gave way to a consolidation move. Cityblock gets in-network MA contracts in Michigan.
- **UpDoc receives FDA clearance for its LLM** as a medical device (510(k) for insulin dose titration), alongside $18M in seed funding. Cleveland Clinic will run a 6–12 month pilot. O’Leary notes the mismatch: FDA clearance was supposed to be the proof point, yet a pilot is still needed. The Utah regulatory model — treating clinical AI like the practice of medicine rather than a device — looks more coherent.
- **Early ACA 2027 rate filings show payers requesting 10%+ increases.** The market is bracing for another year of premium escalation.
- **Elevance Health paid CMS $342M to resolve risk-adjustment sanctions** that would have suspended new MA enrollments. No challenge, just a wire transfer.
- **National health expenditure projected to hit $9 trillion by 2034**, representing 20.6% of GDP.

**What Kevin’s reading / watching:**
- **“Lasering” in the stop-loss market:** carriers carving out high-risk individuals by applying a significantly higher deductible to a single employee. Marsh McLennan saved a 600-person company ~$1M by recommending a laser for one high-cost-procedure employee. The downstream implications if this practice spreads are worth tracking.
- **Freestanding ED growth**, particularly in Texas (25% of ED visits), led by HCA, Atrium Health, and Nutex Health. Nutex’s out-of-network billing strategy and its reliance on the No Surprises Act are under scrutiny.
- **No Surprises Act IDR payments are wildly inflated:** Elevance data shows median IDR awards at 67.4x FFS Medicare, mean at 144.8x. A system designed as a last resort became a business model.

### 4. [🏥 1 patient, 100 front doors](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kwa0dwrt0zewshk1xkpwadcd) — *Workweek (Hospitalogy)* · *Jun 29, 2026*

A sponsored deep-dive on Nabla, but the strategic frame is worth the real estate: ambient documentation is moving beyond the enterprise health system into the fragmented, multi-modality reality of 2026 primary care. Madden argues that adaptability across care settings is becoming the differentiator.

- **The patient now assembles care across a PCP, a DTC telehealth brand, a virtual menopause clinic, and more.** Each touchpoint generates documentation that doesn’t talk to the rest. Most ambient AI went after the enterprise first, selling into Epic and Cerner environments, while virtual-first and rural providers — who don’t run Epic — were left uncovered.
- **Nabla’s bet is adaptability across modalities.** Echo captures video and phone visits and keeps documentation consistent with in-person notes. Connect is an API that lets rural providers and others build ambient into their own stacks. The FQHC example: a community health center that rolled out Nabla saw revenue cycle compress from 40 days to 7, surfacing roughly $250K in previously leaked revenue.
- **CMS’s ACCESS Model goes live July 1** and pulls ~150 tech-enabled organizations into Medicare. Many have never documented in a way anyone outside their own walls would read. ACCESS pays referring clinicians a co-management fee to review updates from these new entrants — so a note generated inside a wearables company has to land, legibly, inside a primary care workflow. The ambient layer that doesn’t exist yet for many participants is exactly what ACCESS’s economics assume.
- **The longer play:** whoever owns documentation that travels consistently across in-person and virtual, enterprise and startup, becomes the wedge into the clinical intelligence layer. Madden frames 2026 as the year that layer gets real.

### 5. [🏥 When Chick Fil A Enters Healthcare](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kwd0wzmm86yx9tyzaaezmj3x) — *Workweek (Hospitalogy)* · *Jun 30, 2026*

A long, vivid piece on Matic, an AI-native primary care platform co-developing with Catalyst Health Group in Dallas. Madden went on-site, talked to physicians, and came back with a thesis: consumer-grade design philosophy, applied to full-risk advanced primary care, might be what the physician burnout problem actually needs.

**Founding story**
- **Calvin Carter** spent 30 years building consumer apps (Chick-fil-A, NPR, Southwest, LEGO) through his firm Bottle Rocket. His design principle: consumer apps don’t get the luxury of being hard to use; you have zero leeway for complexity.
- **Alex Sheppert** has a PhD in AI, built algorithmic options-trading systems for hedge funds, and then — seeing AI would hit healthcare hardest — went to medical school on purpose so he’d hold both badges when the wave arrived. He built the first version of Matic as a scribe to survive his own residency.
- They met through a blockchain rabbit hole. Calvin tried to schedule time with Alex, who kept saying he had class. Calvin assumed MBA or PhD. Alex was in med school.

**Design philosophy**
- Matic labels itself a “physician engagement platform,” not an “AI workflow.” Carter’s line: if you have an AI workflow that’s not engaging, you have an invoice, not a product.
- The “new PX” is a triangle: physician, practice, patient — in that order. Serve the physician first, and the freed-up time and lowered cognitive load cascade into everything else.
- No training manuals, no ROI calculators you have to squint to believe. If a primary care doc has to be convinced to open it, the team already failed.

**Technical differentiators**
- **Multi-LLM orchestration (“Thunderdome”):** a single doctor’s question gets broken into components, routed to different models, with deterministic and non-deterministic steps stitched together through an audit pipeline. This isn’t a wrapper around one model call.
- **Compute discipline:** Matic figures out which sub-tasks need the expensive model and which can go to something fast and cheap. The goal is radically efficient, not cheap.
- **Adversarial training:** Alex’s PhD thesis area. When a physician flags an error, Matic doesn’t patch a prompt — it turns the correction into a permanent training example (bad transcript, wrong output, right output, rationale) and runs it through a recursive LLM-as-judge loop. Over time, thousands of edge cases get solved permanently. Example: an early note wrote “start empagliflozin” without the dose; 50mg vs 100mg is not the same drug to a patient. That becomes a baked-in adversarial case.
- **Start at the summit and ski down:** Matic embedded inside Catalyst, which runs full-risk Medicare capitation, value-based contracts, FFS, and direct primary care simultaneously. By building for the hardest operating environment in primary care, walking into a simpler one feels like a downgrade in difficulty.

**Physician impact (10-week pilot, early data)**
- Physicians report ~90 minutes saved per day, near-perfect note accuracy, and dramatically lower cognitive load.
- Dr. Doug Fullington, internal medicine, used to wake at 5 a.m. to prep charts. Now sleeps until 6:30. “It thinks like my brain thinks.”
- Dr. Keith Eppich, 24-year family medicine physician and self-described “C-minus tech guy,” converted from cynic to evangelist after a Sunday where 23 notes took 20 minutes instead of two hours. His verdict: “Well, shit. It’s pretty good.”
- Madden is careful to note this is still a pilot; clinical-outcome claims need quarters, not ten weeks.

**Feedback infrastructure**
- A Microsoft Teams channel called “The Matic Prototype Cyborgs” where physicians fire off what’s working and what they need next. An AI ingests, dedupes, and routes it into the sprint backlog.
- A design that lived on a whiteboard on June 24th was a near-production MVP five days later. Carter’s head of delivery called this “the golden age of the deep generalist”: AI removed esoteric development knowledge, leaving taste, judgment, and three decades of pattern recognition as the scarce inputs.

**Roadmap**
- Inbox management and smart referral management are next. The referral play is built on the premise that the PCP is the true quarterback and refers out more than needed; Matic wants to flag when a referral is even warranted and route to high-value, in-network specialists using the practice’s own outcomes data.

### 7. [🏥 Mercy Health: the playbook others will copy](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kwz55p9skesj282k0ha0xawa) — *Workweek (Hospitalogy)* · *Jul 7, 2026*

Madden sits down with Mercy CEO Steve Mackin and extracts the most coherent health system strategy of the month. Mercy is running a deliberate playbook that concedes on price and wins on care model efficiency, and the early numbers are working.

- **Scarcity as a strategic advantage.** Mackin frames the nonprofit constraint — can’t turn anyone away, thin margins, tough markets — as something that forces better innovation. “Scarcity’s your friend. It forces you to think differently around the care model.” He hires for “Mercy fit”: change-oriented, other-centric, comfortable challenging his thinking.
- **The contrarian pricing bet:** Mercy has deliberately operated at a lower price point. Mackin’s position: “We can’t honestly continue to push 10, 12, 15% price increases to employers.” They’ll accept the constraint on price and win through efficiency and consumer-friendly volume growth. Revenue CAGR of 11.9% over three years; EBITDA climbed from 4.5% to ~5.7%.
- **The CHF program as proof of concept.** Mercy redesigned the entire congestive heart failure pathway: frequent proactive outreach, 24/7 virtual access, and same-day outpatient clinics for fluid management. ED admissions for CHF patients were cut in half over the last year. They’ve deployed 12 clinics and plan to double that in six months. The flywheel: better chronic management frees acute capacity for the patients who actually need it, funding more growth.
- **Killed 1,300 point solutions.** Migrated all data to the cloud, standardized on one of the largest single Epic instances in the country, and can now deploy new solutions enterprise-wide at pace. Aidoc’s AI imaging platform was rolled out across all hospitals and clinics, covering nearly 4 million lives and 4,400 providers, in ~90 days — something most systems would take a year to do.
- **Mayo partnership as a data moat.** The combined data asset spans 15 million patients, 12 billion images, 3 billion lab results, 10 million pathology reports, and 1.6 billion clinical notes. It functions as a validation environment for AI models and positions Mercy/Mayo as gatekeepers for clinical credibility.
- **On AI and labor:** Mackin doesn’t buy the displacement narrative for healthcare. The industry is structurally short on bedside labor; AI pulls bureaucracy out of the workflow and pushes people toward the bedside at the top of their license. “Brings back the joy of practice” rather than empties the building.
- **Consumerism and the “third rail”:** Standardizing schedules and templates across 4,500 employed clinicians is politically fraught, but it’s table stakes for building a digital front door that doesn’t train patients to route around the system. Mercy is doing the unsexy work.
- **What’s next:** Mackin expects the 5.7% EBITDA to be a floor. They’re co-developing a 3-million-square-foot destination medical campus in Northwest Arkansas with Alice Walton, working with Wellvana on CIN expansion, and partnering with the PillPack founders at General Medicine on access.

### 8. [🏥 A State of the Union on the $37,824 Problem](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kx43kzr8g5hh4nt0ym2ne7mz) — *Workweek (Hospitalogy)* · *Jul 9, 2026*

Madden synthesizes months of interviews into a thesis piece on employer-sponsored healthcare’s breaking point and the structural response it’s forcing. This is the single best framing of the direct contracting movement that appears in the batch.

**The cost math has crossed from expensive to emergency.**
- Milliman 2026 MMI: $37,824 for a family of four, up 7.9% — the steepest non-COVID increase in over a decade.
- Mercer pegs the employer increase at 6.7% after plan design changes; the raw number before offsets was closer to 9%.
- John Zutter (Lantern) reports his own self-funded plan took a 20%+ increase; across ~60 large accounts, average trend landed in the mid-teens.
- Small fully insured groups under 200 lives saw renewals of 30%, 40%, in some cases 80% as carriers repriced commercial books to cover their MA wounds.
- ACA individual market: enhanced subsidies expired, net premiums jumped 58%, deductibles spiked 37% to a record $3,786, and ~3M people dropped coverage.

**Ali Diab’s MLR critique: the toll booth economics.**
- Collective Health’s CEO frames the ACA medical loss ratio as functionally a cost-plus contract: if an insurer must spend 85 cents of every premium dollar on care, the only way to grow the 15 cents is to grow the dollar. Every stakeholder in the chain profits when unit costs and utilization rise.
- Layer on “discount off the Neiman Marcus price” — networks and PBMs negotiate rebates off an inflated MSRP and market the markdown as value — and nobody in the payment chain wins by making the underlying thing cheaper.
- His personal example: a $340 coinsurance charge for a knee brace that retails for $125 at Dick’s, non-negotiable because it was baked into an insurance bundle he couldn’t unbundle. The CEO of a health benefits platform couldn’t unbundle it.

**The waning power of managed care — and where it’s still fine.**
- Dan Mendelson at Morgan Health: when Jamie Dimon announced Morgan Health’s mandate, a major health plan CEO called and asked, “Are you friend or foe?” Mendelson’s reply: “That’s up to you.”
- Plans deliver fine on the everyday 95% — open access, OB-GYN relationships, behavioral health, the ER on vacation. They fail at the high-acuity layer, where 1% of members drive a third of spend, 5% drive ~60%, and 10% drive 85%.
- This is where the disaggregated layer is getting built: Lantern on specialty steerage (12M lives, ~70% compounded growth for a decade), Thyme Care on oncology, Centivo running narrow-network plans anchored to Baylor. The smart plans are now bringing Lantern to their own clients; CVS Aetna co-invested with Morgan Health in Thyme Care. Irrelevance is optional.

**How direct contracting gets deployed: the Northwell Direct mechanics.**
- 170K union members migrated onto a high-performance network, closed in 75 days, bringing Northwell Direct past 310K covered lives and $100M+ in proposed savings over three years.
- The pricing stack: a near-term rate concession (~20% cut), a contractual 5% YOY trend cap, benefit design that steers with lower copays rather than forced switching, and clinical management on the back end.
- An independent third party ran regression on historical claims against proposed network rates before signature — pre-deal, third-party re-adjudication is becoming table stakes.
- Roughly 20% of Northwell Direct’s capacity is non-Northwell providers; a walled garden doesn’t work when your members are scattered across Staten Island to Connecticut.

**Unbundling the whole enchilada.**
- Employer stack 2.0 is modular: a chassis (Collective Health as the Stripe of health benefits), then direct contracts, high-performance networks, specialty navigators, digital-first providers, and direct pharmacy relationships.
- Collective Health’s book is running a 3.9% annualized trend over the trailing 24 months vs. a ~9% self-insured average. A near-6-point delta, sustained, reprices expectations.
- Mercer: 35% of large employers now offer at least one high-performance network plan; 67% offer three or more medical plan choices, up from 60% in 2023.
- ICHRA keeps stubbing its toe on narrow exchange networks; group captives are emerging as the more viable SMB escape hatch.

**AI completes what the HMO started.**
- The HMO movement failed because the consumer experience was adversarial — restricted choice with no intelligence attached. AI-enabled navigation that routes members before they ask, nudges the overdue colonoscopy, and personalizes around individual circumstances makes steerage feel like service.
- AI cuts both ways: Milliman flagged AI as a healthcare cost factor for the first time in the MMI’s history. Providers use it to optimize billing capture; payers deploy it on adjudication. An arms race is underway.

**Madden’s bottom line: this is a paradigm shift, not a cycle.** The simultaneous arrival of brutal math (5% of members driving 60% of spend at 20% trend), the waning popularity of the BUCA vertically integrated model, AI tooling that makes steerage feel consumer-oriented, and committed capital (Morgan Health’s $280M) represents a convergence of forces that prior “revolutions” never had. Health systems that build a direct-to-employer arm now own a durable revenue line and a hedge against commercial rate compression regardless of which direction the market tilts.

### 9. [Weekly Health Tech Reads 7/12/26](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kxbgsmam0k05y3rfwbtzaex5) — *Health Tech Nerds* · *Jul 12, 2026*

### 10. [🏥 Who’s absorbing the liability for AI?](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kxh6yjw7pbgsp3hr7qe0xj4q) — *Workweek (Hospitalogy)* · *Jul 14, 2026*

Madden brings Teira Gunlock (CEO, First Stop Health) and Dr. Eric Bricker (healthcare finance commentator and former CMO) onto a joint podcast episode. The conversation ranges across AI liability, the EMR as walled garden, radiology economics, emotional labor, and the employer escape hatches. Bricker delivers several of the most quotable lines of the month.

**AI regulation and liability: the mismatch is structural.**
- Gunlock: “The regulatory structure is iterative at best, and we have this revolutionary technology growing by leaps and bounds. Those two things are just not going to meet very well.” The deeper problem is that most debate treats AI as a tool, but it’s increasingly behaving like a member of the care team — with its own scope, workflow, and failure modes. That maps poorly onto FDA device frameworks and state provider licensing.
- Bricker: right now, physicians absorb the liability through malpractice coverage. The plaintiff’s bar goes after everyone in the chain. But liability is a distraction from the bigger prize: agentic AI inside clinical workflow software.

**The EMR is a walled garden.**
- Bricker: “Electronic medical record is actually a bad name for it. What it is, is the clinical workflow software. The record-keeping is only a small part of what it does.” Orders, documentation, routing, communication — that’s the mouse-click economy. Agentic AI could take a huge bite out of it, but Epic and other large vendors haven’t operated as open architectures. They’ve operated like Steve Jobs’ original instinct with the iPhone — wanting Apple to build all the apps for control — except nobody talked them out of it. It may require regulation to force the garden open.
- Madden pushes: ambient AI companies that capture 100% of clinical documentation at the point of care are arguably becoming the system of intelligence, and plausibly the system of record, even while the EMR keeps the workflow.
- Gunlock adds: EMRs are clinical workflow *and billing* software. The billing side is where the revenue lives for the vendor, the health system, and the practice. You don’t casually reform that.

**Radiology AI as a canary for subsidy compression.**
- Bricker is unambiguous: letting AI read routine scans in rural and critical access settings is “100% good.” Radiology groups are using AI-assisted reads to push more volume through fewer FTEs and are starting to underbid competitors on hospital stipends. The groups that can shrink the subsidy win the contract.
- This dynamic hasn’t shown up in hospital earnings calls yet; Bricker and Madden both expect it to surface in the back half of 2026 and into 2027. If any specialty sees the subsidy line item compress first because of AI-driven labor arbitrage, radiology is it.

**Who captures the AI dividend?**
- Gunlock frames the open question: is it providers (lower burden, more time with patients), patients (lower cost, better access), or the institutions in the middle (margin compression into the P&L)?
- Bricker says the quiet part: publicly traded hospital systems are going to do layoffs first because their boards will demand it, or because margin compression forces it. Nonprofit systems will talk about reskilling and redeployment. Gunlock is more nuanced: yes, it’s partially a cop-out, but also partially sincere — health systems are the largest employer in many communities and have a moral imperative to invest in reskilling more than other industries. The tension is real.

**Bricker’s emotional labor thesis.**
- “We moved from a world where physical labor was taken over by machines so that we could do intellectual labor. And we’re moving to a world where intellectual labor is being taken over so that we can do emotional labor. The next frontier of work is actually emotional work.”
- He demystifies the physician: a doctor compares two datasets — the patient in front of them and the compendium of medical knowledge — and produces a diagnosis. “There is no mystique in what we do.” The bottleneck is dataset 1, the patient interaction. APPs are often better at it. Arm them with an LLM-based compendium and you’ve addressed the shortage from a direction medical societies won’t love.
- 30 years from now, the physician isn’t the smartest person in the room — they’re the most trusted. Madden draws a parallel to media: AI creates a sterilization effect (LinkedIn comments, newsletters, spam), and the way to stay relevant is to lean harder into authenticity and intimacy. The clinicians who thrive will double down on the parts of the job that were always supposed to be human.

**ICHRA, captives, and what’s actually available to small employers.**
- Two escape hatches from brutal fully insured renewals: ICHRA (defined contribution to individual market) and group captives (collective bargaining for stop-loss coverage).
- ICHRA adoption is countercyclical but limited by exchange network adequacy — a top salesperson whose kid’s specialist is out of network on every available plan will quit, and the business can’t afford that. So ICHRA gets deferred.
- Group captives pool small self-funded employers so a reinsurer will underwrite the aggregate risk without “lasering” a single dialysis patient. This is getting more airtime in 2026.
- Neither escape hatch solves the underlying cost curve. A sleeper catalyst: if carriers ever segmented ICHRA pricing from the broader individual pool (since ICHRA members lack the adverse selection of people who shop because they have a specific expensive need), ICHRA economics improve materially.

**The biggest-picture take: you can’t fix the politics, so decentralize.**
- Bricker quotes Donna Shalala: “The path to healthcare reform is campaign finance reform.” Since that’s not happening, the realistic path is employer-driven unbundling — direct contracting, COEs, captives, ICHRA-adjacent structures, and a smarter broker ecosystem. Messy, local, slow, but it’s the door that’s actually open.

- Provider-sponsored health plans are also retreating: Baylor (100K enrollees), Providence Health Plan unwound its entire operation, Memorial Hermann exited commercial.

**A raft exists: the Rural Health Transformation Program.** $50B over five years, roughly $200M per state annually, with fund allocations coming in the back half of 2026. Use it or lose it.
