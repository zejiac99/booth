### 1. [The Grand Roundup: 05/04/2026](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kqt1yqp9hcawgcz031cg48t4) — *healthtechnerds.com* · *May 4, 2026*

- The live show preview is secondary; the core of this piece is a crisp analysis of why MA PPOs are so hard to run profitably.
- The basic MA value prop trades away broad network and low utilization management for lower premiums and extra benefits. PPOs try to give some of that freedom back — wider provider access, less friction — which members love but margins hate.
- The difficulty isn’t mysterious: it’s insurance math plus competitive dynamics shaped like a prisoner’s dilemma.  
  - If you price cautiously and a rival prices aggressively, you lose market share in a good year. If you match their aggression and the year turns bad medically, you blow up margins.  
  - Stock-based executive comp biases toward growth and risk-taking, amplifying the temptation to lean in.
- Humana CEO Jim Rechtin’s comment is the thesis: PPOs aren’t inherently broken; the industry simply used them as growth engines at the expense of margin. After two years of repricing, HMO/PPO growth is rebalancing — any product works if priced correctly.
- The real constraint is that you don’t just need your own underwriting right; you need competitors to price responsibly too. That’s a collective-action problem, not a technical one.

### 2. [I Have the Alzheimer's Gene. Now What? - The Next #73](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kqsz51bf1d1sdrh9ma4g1f5v) — *substack.com* · *May 4, 2026*

**What we know (and don’t) about Alzheimer’s biology**
- Alzheimer’s is the most terrifying of Peter Attia’s “Four Horsemen” because it progresses silently for decades. Healthcare costs ~$700B and are projected to surpass $1T by 2050.
- The amyloid hypothesis has been the dominant theory for 30 years, but skepticism flared after a 2022 fraud scandal in a key paper, and amyloid-targeting drugs have disappointed. The reality is likely multifactorial — inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, vascular damage, insulin resistance all tangled together.
- Scott Alexander’s two-part series on the amyloid hypothesis is referenced as a deeper dive worth reading.

**Prevention — lifestyle dominates, and a few therapeutic leads are worth watching**
- The 2024 Lancet Commission estimates ~45% of dementia is preventable through lifestyle.
  - Exercise: A Nature study found 3,000–5,000 steps/day delayed cognitive decline by ~3 years; 7,500 steps delayed it by ~7 years.
  - Sleep: The glymphatic system clears amyloid-beta during deep sleep — brain’s own dishwasher.
  - Diet: Type 2 diabetes roughly doubles Alzheimer’s risk; metabolic dysfunction is tightly correlated.
- Beyond the basics: red/near-infrared light applied to the skull shows promise in animal studies. Peptides like Cerebrolysin (approved in 50+ countries, 6 RCTs in Alzheimer’s) and Semax (Russian data, mouse models) are interesting but unavailable or unapproved in the US. GLP-1s show observational risk reductions (~31% in Cleveland Clinic data) but oral semaglutide failed an RCT for cognitive decline — likely preventative, not therapeutic.
- Mares is working with doctors and friends on a program to help Alzheimer’s patients improve symptoms; replies welcome.

### 7. [The Women's Health Paradox: Record VC, Federal Retreat](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kr9g1n7jvt17nq6cpkep5ja6) — *Healthcare Huddle* · *May 10, 2026*

**Private capital is pouring in, and the category is maturing**
- Women’s health VC hit records in Q1 2026: Midi Health (menopause) and Pomelo Care (maternal/women’s primary care) both crossed unicorn valuations. Midi raised $100M Series D at $1B+; Pomelo $92M Series C at $1.7B.
- But the 4–6% share of total healthcare VC for half the population persists. The World Economic Forum calls it a structural mispricing.
- Five sub-markets are pulling the most capital: maternal/women’s primary care platforms (virtual-first, payer-contracted), fertility/reproductive health (shifting to therapeutics and lab automation), women’s oncology, cardio-metabolic, and autoimmune.
- The common thread among the hottest companies: category-defining unicorn moments, category-resetting M&A (Hologic’s $18.3B take-private by Blackstone/TPG), or a credible AI/automation angle (Midi positioning as “AI company that happens to do menopause,” Overture Life’s robotic embryology).

**Federal research and reproductive policy are moving the other way**
- The 2024 Biden administration built significant infrastructure: $200M+ for NIH women’s health research, ARPA-H’s $100M Sprint for Women’s Health, $300M in global health equity pledges from Gates/Novo Nordisk/Wellcome.
- The second Trump administration has largely reversed that posture: hundreds of grants canceled, CDC instructed to scrub terminology, HHS briefly terminated Women’s Health Initiative contracts before public backlash forced a 24-hour reversal. A lasting chill remains.
- NIH now uses computational text analysis to flag grant applications with terms like “gender” before approval. Grant spending is ~$1B behind historical pace, with women’s health, cancer, and mental health taking the steepest hits.
- Private capital can supplement this work but cannot replace 30-year cohort studies. The evidence pipeline behind future women’s health products thins while the products themselves get richer.

**The abortion-medication legal battle and its clinical consequences**
- Four years post-*Dobbs*, 13 states enforce total bans. Total U.S. abortions have actually risen (telehealth medication abortion driving the increase).
- That access is under active threat: the Fifth Circuit reinstated in-person dispensing requirements for mifepristone nationwide. Justice Alito issued a temporary stay; SCOTUS will decide. If the ruling stands, mail-based mifepristone collapses overnight.
- State-level moves add pressure: Mississippi criminalizes manufacturing/distributing abortion pills (felony, up to 10 years), Louisiana reclassifies them as controlled substances, Texas lets private citizens sue providers.
- The physician training pipeline is bending: OB/GYN applications to ban-state programs dropped 10.5% in the first post-*Dobbs* cycle, and the gap widened in the next. Idaho lost ~22% of its practicing obstetricians in 15 months. Today’s application gap is tomorrow’s maternity-care desert.
- For internists, hospitalists, and EM docs in ban states, this means a shrinking consult bench — the same states with the worst maternal outcomes are losing the specialists they’d call.

**Dashevsky’s three things to watch in the next 90 days**
1. SCOTUS on *Louisiana v. FDA* — whichever way the Court rules, the FDA will look unstable on this drug class for the rest of the year.
2. FY2027 NIH appropriations — the moment where the gender-flag text-screening posture either becomes permanent or gets walked back.
3. Late-stage women’s health funding rounds — if Series B and late-C deals don’t unfreeze in H2 2026, Q1’s unicorn pop was a top, not a turn.

### 10. [Guest Post: Before You Build for Dual-Eligibles, Understand Who You're Building For](https://read.readwise.io/read/01krh7zms76915m6zr0qkam48h) — *healthtechnerds.com* · *May 13, 2026*

**The gap between coverage and care, in one story**
- A dual-eligible woman with cognitive decline was placed in a memory-care facility by her health plan’s care management vendor. Her coverage — state Medicaid plus a group MA plan — looked robust on paper. Then the plan denied retroactive authorization and the facility issued an eviction notice.
- The denial logic was perverse: the plan required a certain number of nursing facility days/ED visits to qualify for the benefit. She didn’t meet it because the plan’s own prevention program had kept her out of those settings. The prevention was denied because it had worked. The family was left with six-figure arrears and no legal aid capacity to help.
- This is the operating-model failure beneath adequate insurance architecture. “The system she could not get to work for her was, by every measurable metric, working as designed.”

**Why duals are a different animal from standard MA**
- ~13M dually enrolled Americans, ~$549B in combined program spending. But “dual-eligible” isn’t one eligibility category: it’s the intersection of Medicare with one of several Medicaid assistance levels (FBDE, QMB, SLMB, QI, QDWI) — each with different benefit packages, cost-sharing structures, and relationships between Medicare and the state.
- D-SNPs alone span a spectrum: Coordination-Only (still ~65% of plans, and per JAMA 2025, produce no measurable outcome improvement for duals with dementia), Highly Integrated (HIDE), Fully Integrated (FIDE — which did show a 1.2 pp reduction in preventable hospitalizations, 7.2 pp reduction in 30-day readmissions), and Applicable Integrated Plans (AIP, the newest regulatory tightening).
- Then there’s PACE (county-locked, frailty-adjusted risk scores, full-risk capitation, fundamentally different architecture), C-SNPs, I-SNPs, and look-alike plans being squeezed by CMS thresholds. CMMI models (Financial Alignment Initiative, LEAD) layer on top.
- Treating all of this as one product with different stickers is how seed-stage companies disappear in year two.

**What changes when you cross into duals: the SMAC and the state layer**
- Beyond the standard MA Bid Pricing Tool, a D-SNP requires a State Medicaid Agency Contract (SMAC) under 42 CFR 422.107. The SMAC is where the state defines what integration actually means — data expectations, care coordination requirements, Model of Care alignment, and increasingly, outcome expectations.
- Plans can pass procurement with a signed SMAC and still fail operationally. The gap shows up months later in grievances, appeals, missed HRAs, and sentinel events — by which point the plan has already assumed full financial and clinical risk for a population it can’t actually manage.

**The fiscal squeeze and where the work is going**
- Medicare MLRs for publicly traded plans (including duals books) are in the high-80s to low-90s. The CY2026 rate process completed the V28 phase-in; underlying model trajectory remains compressive. Federal matching-rate reductions force states to cut supplemental rates and HCBS funding, directly squeezing the Medicaid component of FIDE-SNP capitation. Work requirements add eligibility churn without reducing clinical acuity.
- The next decade of value in duals is likely condition-focused, person- and caregiver-centered. Condition-specific VBC models can reduce total cost of care 8–10% (McKinsey). AI can automate high-volume, judgment-light administrative work (documentation, FHIR mapping, prior auth narratives), freeing care coordinators for clinical judgment — but it can’t catch the internally contradictory denial logic that broke the opening story.

**The discipline required**
- Caruncho-Simpson closes with a portfolio-management approach: define your duals sub-segment, the problem you’re solving (quality, access, cost), the vehicle (which D-SNP variant or adjacent model), how you finance it, your defensible outcome metric, and your sunset criterion. If you can’t answer whether your solution would have changed the outcome for the family in the opening story, you’re not ready.

### 12. [🏥 The Treatment We're Refusing To Prescribe](https://read.readwise.io/read/01krkhpe5sjgs5cxftq9gzbqnr) — *workweek.com* · *May 14, 2026*

**The public health case for autonomous vehicles**
- 36,000+ U.S. road deaths annually, 5.1M serious injuries, $1T+ economic toll — larger than the Medicare budget. We’ve normalized this as the price of mobility.
- Waymo’s data now covers 170M+ fully driverless miles: 92% reduction in injury crashes vs. matched human baselines. Pedestrian injury crashes down 92%, cyclist crashes down 85%, intersection crashes down 96%.
- The medical analogy: if a drug trial showed this magnitude of mortality reduction, the data safety monitoring board would terminate the study early — continuing the placebo arm would be unethical. No existing U.S. body is empowered to “stop the trial” for human driving.
- A group of 20 founding clinician signatories (Slotkin, Topol, Walker, Carmona, and others) published an open letter calling for standardized federal AV safety reporting, a clearer federal accounting of motor vehicle deaths, and evidence-based regulatory frameworks to replace barriers.

**The evidence gap is a reporting problem**
- NHTSA’s Standing General Order requires crash reporting but not miles-driven data. Without a denominator, crash counts can’t be normalized to rates. Tesla redacts crash narratives as confidential business information; Zoox publishes nothing comparable. Only Waymo provides a full picture.
- The letter’s core ask: standardize reporting so clinicians can evaluate every operator on the same evidentiary footing. Not picking a winner — demanding comparable evidence.

**The political obstacles are real and often unspoken**
- Deploying AVs at scale threatens organized labor (driver unions) and the plaintiffs’ bar (fewer crashes mean fewer lawsuits). Special interests are actively blocking deployment in Boston, DC, New York, Washington state, and Minnesota.
- The letter deliberately avoids partisan fights to make it signable by clinicians across the spectrum. Slotkin: “If I want a nurse in Boston to put her name on it, she needs to be able to do that without taking a position on every political fight in her city.”

**Workforce displacement is the honest tension**
- Millions of Americans drive for a living. The letter acknowledges that “displacement that is foreseeable and unaddressed is its own kind of preventable harm.” But workforce planning isn’t a reason to slow-walk a mortality-reducing technology; it’s a reason to start planning for transition.
- Walker’s closing frame: we’ve normalized dangerous things before (smoking in hospitals, leaded gasoline, drunk driving). Our children will look back and ask what we were thinking.

### 13. [🏥 Oura’s Healthcare Pivot](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ks0spaamzrv911066n5cwc5v) — *workweek.com* · *May 19, 2026*

**The engagement moat is the story**
- In a partnership with Essence Healthcare (MA plan), Oura saw 6x the engagement of prior programs. 77% of members active 5+ days/week, average daily ring wear 23.8 hours. App opens 3–4x/day.
- The difference: Oura started as a consumer product people genuinely wanted. Sleep tracking was the entry point — universal, non-threatening. The healthcare use case got layered on top of an already sticky experience. It’s a pull model; most digital health is push. That distinction is everything when targeting a 72-year-old MA member.

**The deliberate march from wellness perk to clinical risk signal**
- Oura has 40+ PhDs/MDs, an IRB-approved hypertension study headed for FDA submission, a live Dexcom CGM integration, and longitudinal GLP-1 data collection. That’s a clinical infrastructure buildout, not consumer dabbling.
- Once a wearable crosses into FDA-cleared territory with demonstrated cost reduction, the economics shift — payers and employers will fully subsidize it. The hypertension FDA play is the tell.

**Women’s health as an underappreciated strategic wedge**
- The ring’s finger placement gives ~100x better signal than the wrist, and continuous temperature tracking captures hormonal fluctuations that wrist wearables miss. Oura can predict menstrual cycles, identify ovulation, track pregnancy by gestational age, and surface perimenopause patterns.
- A custom women’s health LLM, vetted by in-house clinicians, is intentionally tuned to be non-dismissive and emotionally supportive — addressing one of women’s most persistent complaints about traditional clinical interactions.

**The Cigna partnership as a payer signal**
- Cigna is offering Oura through employer wellness programs with ring cost covered. But the real intent: “redesign chronic care and member engagement around continuous, worn, high-quality biometric data.” That’s upstream of traditional RPM, which focused on acute/post-discharge.
- Dorothy Kilroy’s data-interoperability philosophy: “filtered intelligence, not data transfer.” Clinicians get actionable trend deviations and risk signals, not 23.8 hours of raw biometrics. Explicit consent, no data selling.

**Behavior change that actually works**
- Oura sees ~20% reduction in alcohol intake among members who discover the impact on their own physiology — elevated resting heart rate, tanked HRV, degraded sleep. Seeing your own body’s response creates a feedback loop that warnings and physician counseling rarely achieve.

- Over time the tech commoditizes; differentiation shifts to expertise, trust, and strategic alignment. The biggest unlock ahead is clinical AI, but adoption will be incremental — attrition, not layoffs.

## Themes

**MA economics are compressing from every angle, forcing a re-examination of how plans manage risk and capture revenue.**  
The prisoner’s dilemma in PPO bidding (article 1), the shift to prospective risk capture in 2027 (article 6), the carrier exits from ACA exchanges after subsidy expiration (article 3), and the V28 risk adjustment changes pushing payors toward partners that actually bend the cost curve (article 9) all point to a structural margin reset. Plans can no longer paper over medical cost trends with coding and revenue-side levers — the pressure to manage care for real is showing up across product design, network strategy, and delegation choices.

**AI deployment is accelerating but creating adversarial arms races and new operational complexity before it delivers system-level savings.**  
The “AI Bot Wars” framing (article 14) is supported by PHTI’s finding that payer AI prior auth drives up costs because providers respond with their own AI to appeal denials (article 3). At the same time, AI is finding genuine utility in reducing clinical administrative burden (article 11, article 15) and in consumer engagement platforms (article 13). The gap between point-solution AI and system-level orchestration — and the trust and workforce dynamics that slow clinical adoption — sits at the center of Blake’s orchestration-era bet (article 14).

**Consumerization is gaining a real foothold, but in bounded, specific chunks rather than a broad retail transformation.**  
Oura’s pull-based engagement and clinical pivot (article 13), the bounded-consumerism framework applied to primary care and AI-native search (article 14), the Cuban-Humana partnership attacking the PBM model (article 3), and direct employer contracting moves by major health systems (article 14) all share a common thread: market dynamics are being injected into select, price-transparent, lower-urgency domains while the rest of the system remains structured around institutional contracts and information asymmetry.

**The health system model is breaking at the edges — M&A, outpatient pressure, and financial bifurcation are reshaping the landscape faster than policy can keep up.**  
WakeMed’s sub-scale problem despite a demographically attractive market (article 5) is the flip side of the outpatient migration and academic encroachment Blake details in his Health System 2.0 bet (article 14). Hospital M&A is at a six-year Q1 high but 68% divestiture-driven — selling off pieces rather than empire-building (article 3). The divide between price-maker systems running 8–12% margins and the median nonprofit barely above breakeven is widening, and the systems that can invest in ambulatory density and direct channels will compound the gap.

**Women’s health and dual-eligible care are both seeing a collision between private capital momentum and retreating public infrastructure, exposing the limits of what VC can replace.**  
The women’s health paradox — record venture dollars and first unicorns against NIH grant cancellations and a destabilized FDA (article 7) — mirrors the dual-eligible challenge where well-capitalized entrants misprice the operational complexity of SMACs, state procurement, and care coordination that requires public infrastructure (article 10). Private capital can build products and platforms; it can’t run 30-year cohort studies or replace a functional federal regulator. Both domains show that the evidence base and the operating model matter as much as the funding.

## Notable Perspectives

- **“The industry priced PPO aggressively as a growth engine at the expense of margin”** (Jim Rechtin, Humana — article 1). A concise diagnosis of why MA PPOs became a trap: incentives, competitive dynamics, and the seduction of membership growth conspired against margin. - **“The system she could not get to work for her was, by every measurable metric, working as designed.”** (Caruncho-Simpson, article 10). The dual-eligible eviction story is the most visceral illustration in this batch of how adequate insurance architecture can still fail operationally. It’s a test case any founder or plan executive building for duals should be able to answer against.

- **“If a drug trial showed this magnitude of mortality reduction, the data safety monitoring board would terminate the study early.”** (Jon Slotkin, via Blake Madden — article 12). Reframing AV safety data as a medical intervention with an ethical obligation to stop the placebo arm. A powerful heuristic, even if the infrastructure to act on it doesn’t exist yet.

- **“Filtered intelligence, not data transfer.”** (Dorothy Kilroy, Oura — article 13). The design principle for integrating consumer-generated biometric data into clinical workflows without burying clinicians. The difference between success and inbox overload for any wearable or RPM company attempting the clinical bridge.

- **“Are we using AI to entrench already misaligned incentives, or to actually collaborate?”** (Blake Madden — article 14). The central question of the AI Bot Wars bet. Not just whether AI works, but whether its deployment vectors in healthcare reinforce zero-sum payer-provider conflict or enable new alignment.

- **Bounded consumerism** as a framework (article 14). The idea that consumerism can’t apply universally in healthcare — but can work in bounded domains where prices are transparent, decisions are non-urgent, and data is portable — is a sharper formulation than the standard “healthcare will be like retail” or “it never will.” Primary care as the first viable testbed.
