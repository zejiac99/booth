The second installment of Madden’s hospital bear-market series. His thesis: 2H 2026 onward gets materially harder for health systems, with 2028 the worst year once OBBBA Medicaid provisions land fully. The piece walks payer mix deterioration, CMS rate compression, and volume resilience side by side, arguing this won’t be a collapse but the operating pain will be unevenly distributed.

- **Payer mix is rotting from multiple directions.** ACA effectuated enrollment already fell 22.1M → 19.2M (projected full-year average ~17.5M), with average premiums up 58% and deductibles up 37%. Those who stayed mostly traded down to bronze — functionally self-pay. HCA flagged the acceleration before Q2 print.

Molina’s investor day offered a flicker of rate relief in 2027 since re-pricing will jump off 2025 data that caught the acuity inflection.
- **CMS built a deflationary flywheel.** The Physician Fee Schedule carries no inflation mandate; CMS is using it to reallocate — CY2027 claws ~7% from orthopedics toward primary care and ACO-affiliated E/M. OPPS keeps its market-basket update and dismantles the inpatient-only list by 2029. Hospitals lose on the physician side while ambulatory sites gain.
- **ACA disenrollment stealth-attacks DSH and 340B.** Fewer Medicaid days lower a hospital’s disproportionate patient percentage, dragging Medicare DSH and potentially knocking facilities below the 340B eligibility line. Meanwhile OPPS proposes cutting 340B drug payment to ASP minus 33.4%.
- **Volume is “fine in aggregate, soft where coverage bites.”** Vizient’s 10-year forecast: outpatient +20%, inpatient +7%. Intuitive’s Q2 showed da Vinci growth slipping from 14% to 12%, concentrated in deferrable cases, with a “modest adverse impact” from ACA subsidy loss.
- **A raft exists:** the Rural Health Transformation Program allocates ~$200M per state annually over five years, with funds flowing in the back half of 2026. “Use it or lose it.”

### 2. [Meta Raybans x Healthcare? Some thoughts](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ky2kv95s16yrrff2tefpnzn7) — *Out-Of-Pocket (Nikhil Krishnan)* · *Jul 21, 2026*

Krishnan contrasts his 2013 Google Glass experience with wearing Meta Raybans in 2026 at his own hardware hackathon, then extrapolates toward healthcare use cases. It’s a product-form-factor think-piece, not an investment thesis, but the patient-journey and clinician-workflow implications are concrete.

- **What’s improved in 13 years:** bone conduction audio (private earpiece), decent object-recognition AI, usable voice controls, and frames that don’t scream “mug me.” The new model adds an in-lens screen and an armband for hand-gesture control.
- **Why the form factor might matter for healthcare:**
  - **“Guardian angel” tech** — real-time transcription and whisper-back for visually impaired patients, or for surfacing follow-up questions during a doctor/pharmacist conversation without pulling out a phone.
  - **Hands-free environments** — wound triage by nurses wearing gloves, lab protocols viewed while pipetting, inventory/bar-code scanning in mail-order pharmacies or sterile processing.
  - **POV capture** — field clinicians (home health, hospice) could reconstruct OASIS assessments and visit notes from glasses capture rather than reconstructing after the fact. This bleeds straight into RCM.
- **What’s still broken:** finicky hand controls, voice commands that draw groans (“Hey Meta, take a video”), AI latency and insufficient granularity, and a context-window problem — real-time video interpretation demands cloud GPU farms that make always-on operation impractical.
- **Cultural norms are the real bottleneck.** The recording-indicator light spooks people. And Krishnan wonders aloud whether ubiquitous in-lens screens will turn conversations into the in-person equivalent of someone checking another tab on Zoom.

- **Reimbursement got hit from both service mix *and* payer mix.** Net revenue per adjusted admit fell 0.5%. Uninsured/self-pay visits rose 110bps to 6%+ of visits. The ACA disenrollees kept showing up at the ER; CHS assumed some commercial pickup and got none.

### 6. [🏥 The $48B payor nobody contracts with](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kyhy3emtjd3z11abf93a422e) — *Hospitalogy (Blake Madden)* · *Jul 27, 2026*

A sponsored deep dive with Cedar that doubles as a genuinely useful framing of a structural shift: the patient is now functionally a top-three payor for most health systems, and almost nobody runs revenue cycle like that’s true. The piece builds the macro case, then walks through Cedar’s behavioral model and the CFO diligence questions that matter.

- **The patient payor is growing fast, expensive to collect from, and yields terribly.** Health systems spend 25–30 cents to collect a patient dollar versus 4–5 cents for a commercial dollar, and collect 30–50% of what’s owed versus 95–97% from insurers. In 2025, patient responsibility rose to 7.3% of net revenue while yield fell to 42.4%. A commercially insured patient with a $12,000 catastrophic bronze-plan deductible is labeled “good insurance” on every payor-mix dashboard but is de facto self-pay.
- **The macro accelerant:** In 2005, ~2% of Americans had a high-deductible plan. By 2023, a majority did. Patient out-of-pocket grew faster than medical trend for a decade. Then 2026 landed: ACA subsidies expired, average marketplace deductibles jumped 37% to $3,786, and CBO projects the uninsured increase going from 1.3M in 2026 to 5.2M in 2027 when Medicaid work requirements and 6-month redeterminations hit. Combined, this strips $68.5B from hospital revenue across 2026–2027, with uncompensated care rising 38.6% to $44.9B.
- **Propensity-to-pay as a credit score cosplaying as strategy.** Cedar’s study found accounts with *high* external propensity-to-pay scores yielded 51% *less* than bills with no score at all. The typical model uses credit history and zip codes — static, non-intuitive inputs blind to the actual reason a bill goes unpaid (e.g., an EOB mismatch).
- **Cedar’s product architecture:**
  - 80+ dynamic behavioral attributes replace static scores, refreshed in real time.
  - Engagement fragments by need: a $20 balance gets a one-tap text; a $7,000 balance leads with payment plans and financial assistance; an uninsured patient gets routed to Medicaid enrollment or copay assistance before billing.
  - The AI voice agent Kora absorbs 20–30% of call volume, cuts handle time ~25%, and is QA’d by other AI agents.
  - **Cedar Cover**, launched at HLTH with Novant, Baystate, and ApolloMD, proactively handles Medicaid enrollment and redetermination paperwork — a 97% application success rate, converting would-be write-offs into paying government accounts.
- **Baseline economics:** 22% average first-year collection lift, 89% patient satisfaction, 81% of collected dollars through self-service digital channels.
- **Diligence questions worth retaining:** interrogate the satisfaction number on accounts that *didn’t* resolve; demand cohort-level attribution so you can separate lift Cedar caused from lift the coverage environment disguised; calibrate against your own write-off data; and check whether the vendor aligns with your patient-experience culture.

### 7. [How HIPAA might be changing](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kymqh45r3jvmx2qh58zh8yr4) — *Out-Of-Pocket (Nikhil Krishnan)* · *Jul 28, 2026*

A sponsored Q&A with Vanta’s Faisal Khan that’s more practical than the headline suggests. It covers the proposed HIPAA Security Rule overhaul, the current state of AI governance in health systems, and a handful of concrete mistakes startups make when scoping their compliance environment.

- **HIPAA’s Security Rule is getting its first major revision in over a decade.** The proposed rule removes the “addressable” vs. “required” distinction and mandates: encryption of all ePHI at rest and in transit; multi-factor authentication for systems accessing ePHI; annual written verification from business associates that controls are in place; and mandatory compliance self-audits every 12 months. The goal is to raise the bar to match the current threat landscape.
- **AI regulation is fragmenting along familiar lines.** The EU AI Act classifies anything processing PHI as high-risk and starts enforcement in August 2026. Colorado’s original AI law got repealed and rewritten as a lighter version (SB 189) after industry pushback. The US is trending toward federal-level law rather than a patchwork of state initiatives.
- **Enterprise AI vendor vetting is acquiring a standardized shape.** Health systems are layering an AI-specific review on top of standard security diligence: what the AI does and whether it’s clinical or administrative; whether prompts, outputs, or patient data are used for training; model provenance and validation in healthcare settings; and whether human oversight exists.
- **“Sign a BAA with the foundation model provider” is not the full answer.** Using LLMs with PHI requires the entire workflow to be designed for PHI — every vendor and subprocessor in the data path, configuration requirements, and controls around prompts and outputs, not just a BAA checkbox.
- **For startups: scope your PHI environment intentionally.** A common mistake is assuming PHI lives only in the production database while it’s also being written into application logs or copied into a support tool, leaving major parts of the environment outside the risk analysis, access controls, and vendor review. The Supabase example is instructive: “Supabase is HIPAA-compliant” is not enough — you need the BAA *and* the HIPAA add-on enabled with PHI usage constrained to those services.

### 9. [🏥 Q2 Public Hospital Operator Deep Dive](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kynt2bt7mzcxj3v8zmwsts79) — *Hospitalogy (Blake Madden)* · *Jul 29, 2026*

Madden’s definitive Q2 ranking and cross-operator analysis. The four public hospital operators printed same-store adjusted admissions inside a tight 30bps band (2.6%–2.9%) yet produced EBITDA growth ranging from +16.3% to -13.2%. Mix and cost structure did all the work.

**Operator ranking:**
1. **Tenet:** offset a 17% decline in exchange revenue with volume growth and expense discipline. Hospital adjusted EBITDA margin hit 18%; USPI’s high-acuity ASC strategy is the engine. “Begging for an EBITDA multiple re-rate.”

3. **HCA:** exchange attrition produced a $400M pretax hit, partially offset by a supplemental payment true-up. Guidance cut $250M at the midpoint even after supplemental assumptions improved ~$550M. Resiliency efforts and demand fundamentals looked better than the headline suggests.
4. **CHS:** the worst operator by every metric. Net revenue per adjusted admit went negative. Half of admission growth came from uninsured patients. Bankruptcy risk is live.

**Macro themes:**
- **Exchange attrition converted to uninsured at roughly 1:1 across all four operators.** Nobody got the expected commercial pickup. Those former ACA enrollees kept showing up at the ER and stopped paying. HCA’s uninsured passed 10% of equivalent admissions; CHS moved self-pay past 6% of visits from under 5%.

- **Elective surgery softening is real but not uniform.** HCA inpatient surgeries -2.3%, outpatient -3.4%. CHS named orthopedics as the largest decliner. Tenet rejected the consumer-pullback read. UHS blamed site-of-care migration to ASCs and freestanding imaging. “All three explanations can be true in different markets.”
- **Professional fees are the new margin frontier.** With contract labor normalized, the conversation shifted to physician subsidies — anesthesia and radiology are the biggest inflation points for HCA, Tenet, Ardent, and others.
- **Nobody has an AI story.** Not one operator quantified an AI-driven margin dollar or disclosed a platform. Tenet came closest by placing automation inside its 240bps hospital margin expansion without isolating its share. “For a sector that spends considerable conference airtime on ambient documentation, the distance between the podium and the earnings call remains enormous.”

### 11. [Guest Post: The Layer Beneath: Healthcare Data Infrastructure in an Age of Commodity Intelligence](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kysq4gzpeqbn3wxnq3693st1) — *Health Tech Nerds (Ezra Mehlman)* · *Jul 30, 2026*

Mehlman, managing partner at Health Enterprise Partners, argues that as frontier AI becomes available to anyone through an API, the durable value in healthcare IT shifts one layer down — to the data infrastructure companies that control trusted access, integration, regulatory permissions, and network reach. It’s an investor thesis, not neutral analysis, but it names a dynamic that shows up elsewhere in this month’s reading.

- **The paradox:** AI captured 54% of digital health venture funding in 2025, but by Q1 2026 Rock Health stopped tracking “AI-enabled” as a separate category because it had become table stakes. When intelligence is abundant, scarcity moves to what hasn’t been democratized: governed data, workflow integration, and regulatory standing.
- **Data liquidity as a margin lever.** Every AI company pays an integration tax — long implementations, custom interfaces, services-heavy deployments — that shows up in delayed time-to-value and churn. Infrastructure networks that have already connected endpoints and normalized data can amortize that cost across many applications. When OpenAI and Perplexity launched healthcare consumer experiences in 2026, they didn’t build longitudinal record retrieval from scratch; they paid for access.
- **Regulation as moat, not friction.** TEFCA, the Cures Act information-blocking rules, and CMS interoperability requirements raise the cost of participating credibly. Regulated infrastructure combines the durability of a utility with the growth profile of software.
- **The HEP portfolio lens:** Bank of America acquired AxiaMed for provider-side payment infrastructure; Siemens acquired MobileMD for its health-information exchange. In both cases the strategic value extended beyond a product feature — the acquirer was buying a position in a regulated, networked market.
- **Risks to the thesis:** platform capture by Epic and Oracle, and commoditization — regulation may produce utility-like pricing. The best infrastructure companies will need more than connectivity; they need differentiated reach, normalized data, high-value workflows, and network effects that deepen with usage.
- **Implication for capital allocation:** application-layer diligence must identify a moat independent of the model (workflow ownership, proprietary feedback loops, regulatory standing). Infrastructure should be evaluated on network economics — connection density, data rights, transaction growth, pricing power, and reach into fragmented settings like post-acute and behavioral health.

### 14. [Pregnancy — a personal story about healthcare](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kz4nq5ce19p6epvabapw9jxq) — *Out-Of-Pocket (Nikhil Krishnan)* · *Aug 3, 2026*

The most human piece in the batch and the one that tests the reader’s healthcare mental models against a raw patient journey. Part 1 of 3; this installment covers finding a doctor and the birth itself.

- **The shoppability problem is real, even for a health-tech insider.** Krishnan has spent years arguing for healthcare shopping but admits he didn’t know what to optimize for: the OB or the hospital? Privacy or complication-handling? Amenity marketing (private rooms, Chanel bags, “Beyoncé gave birth here”) gives patients things they can evaluate but may not lead to the right match for their clinical situation. Like most people, he defaulted to friends’ experiences.
- **C-sections as an F1 pit crew.** A routine labor turned emergency C-section when the baby’s heart rate dropped. The transition was “one minute Ozuna is playing, the next a red button is pushed and 14 people run in.” From decision to baby crying: under 6 minutes. The competence was total.
- **Hospitals market to birth plans but the real value is handling what goes wrong.** People warn against C-section-prone hospitals, but Krishnan’s takeaway is the opposite: you want the hospital that does a lot of C-sections because they’re excellent at them.
- **Postpartum is the abandonment zone.** After two days, “the hospital just… gives you the baby and tells you good luck.” No bathing instructions, no feeding guidance, no list of “things that seem like medical issues but are totally normal” (hemangiomas, divergent infant eye movements). The contrast with Korea’s postpartum recovery hotels and the Netherlands’ *kraamzorg* home maternity nursing is stark, especially given >40% of postpartum maternal deaths occur within 42 days of birth.
- **The partner’s experience is mostly reading the room’s tension.** You have no clinical reference frame. “Everything was very terse until the baby came out and people started having casual conversation — that’s the only way to read if things are normal or not.”

### 15. [🏥 The Becker's Healthcare blueprint](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kz75799mazzz4t22mjq0rtgh) — *Hospitalogy (Blake Madden)* · *Aug 4, 2026*

Madden interviews Scott Becker about three decades of building healthcare media. The conversation yields several transferable frameworks — the M&M scoreboard for doubling down on what’s working, the internal war for short-form content won by data, and the 10-to-20 relationship rule — plus a supply-side healthcare thesis that cuts against both political orthodoxies.

- **The M&M scoreboard:** when you have three initiatives running and the red one is lapping the field, the instinct to pour energy into the lagging ones is wrong. Becker’s operating philosophy: kill the red one, eat the red one, be the red one. He tested surgery centers, disease management, and cardiovascular simultaneously; the surgery center business returned 4× the client work, so that’s where he went.
- **The short-form battle:** Becker’s editorial team fought him on writing six short items a day instead of one deep piece a week. What turned it wasn’t an editorial mandate — it was readership data. Long strategy pieces: 50 clicks. Short roundup items: 200–300 clicks. CFQs wrote in saying they loved a format they could digest in 10 minutes.
- **Thought leadership is distribution, not creation.** “Writing an article itself is useless.” The piece is an excuse to get in front of the 20–50 people who actually matter to your business. The newsletter isn’t for everyone; it’s for the handful you need to stay in front of.
- **The 10-to-20 rule:** your career rests on 10–20 relationships, not thousands of followers. Becker travels to see a health system CEO even when it loses money because the relationship matters intrinsically.
- **The supply-demand diagnosis:** the central crisis in US healthcare is a shortage of physicians, advanced practitioners, and triage capacity, and almost all political energy is directed at the wrong problem. Coverage without supply “is like writing a check you can’t cash.” Medicare Advantage has been “a massive disaster” — more spending per enrollee, worse access. The solution isn’t a payment model; FFS and capitation have their respective abuse vectors. The solution is more capacity.
- **Founder evolution:** stage one is doing everything yourself; stage two is doing it with people but you’re the ceiling; stage three is hiring people who can do every individual job better than you ever could. Becker’s warning: don’t let a bad hire make you gun-shy. “You can’t grow if your team stops swinging.”

## Themes

**The uninsured wave is no longer a forecast — it’s in the earnings calls, the revenue-per-admit numbers, and the self-pay percentages.**
Across three weeks of Q2 prints, every hospital operator and several payers named the same dynamic: post-subsidy ACA disenrollees didn’t shift to commercial or Medicaid; they became self-pay and kept using the ER. HCA’s number was ~100% conversion, not the 85% it modeled. CHS saw half its admission growth come from patients who pay pennies on the dollar. Cedar’s platform data confirms uninsured patients are 10–15% of volume but nearly 40% of outstanding patient dollars, a share that grew 54% in three years — *before* the 2026 policy changes fully landed. CBO projects the uninsured increase quadrupling from 1.3M to 5.2M from 2026 to 2027. This is a structural payer-mix shift with a second wave — Medicaid work requirements and redetermination churn — arriving before the first wave is absorbed.
*Articles: 1, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 13*

**Cost structure and payer mix are widening a rift between the haves and have-nots among hospital operators.**
All four public operators printed same-store adjusted admissions inside a 30bps band (2.6%–2.9%) yet EBITDA growth spanned from +16.3% (Tenet) to -13.2% (CHS). The difference came down to which operators had high-acuity ASC exposure, disciplined cost structures, and favorable local market composition — and which were stuck with ERs filling up with uninsured patients while elective orthopedic and cardiac procedures softened. Tenet’s ASC strategy let it reject the consumer-pullback narrative that CHS is living. The implication for non-public systems: the community hospital operating at a 2% margin with no revenue-cycle scale is in a materially worse position than any of these four.
*Articles: 3, 5, 9, 10*

**Supplemental payments became the swing factor in Q2 profitability, and that crutch is about to shrink.**
HCA’s headline revenue-per-admit growth of 6.4% flips to roughly -0.9% when DPP is stripped out. Florida’s program approval floated several operators this quarter. But HCA’s own guidance implies a $100–300M supplemental headwind in H2; UHS hasn’t forecast anything beyond Q2 because CMS hasn’t approved the expanded program past September. The proposed $246B provider-tax clampdown and the deferral of $1B+ in Medicaid payments to California and Minnesota suggest the policy environment is turning against the mechanism that just saved Q2. Systems that grew structurally reliant on unpredictable supplemental revenue face a reckoning.
*Articles: 5, 9, 10*

**The AI conversation at the application layer is loud; at the earnings-call layer it’s silent.**
Not one public hospital operator quantified an AI-driven margin dollar this quarter. Tenet came closest by attributing part of a 240bps hospital margin expansion to automation, without isolating the share. The disconnect between conference-stage ambition and operator-level disclosure is gaping. Ezra Mehlman’s guest post provides the intellectual framework: as AI becomes commoditized and available through an API to anyone, the durable value shifts to the infrastructure layer — governed data access, workflow integration, regulatory standing. Centene’s Sarah London echoed this from the payer side, describing an intentional bet on data infrastructure before plug-and-play AI applications. The emerging consensus is that infrastructure gets built first and the AI returns come later, which means the current earnings silence is not necessarily bearish — it just reflects the timeline.
*Articles: 9, 10, 11, 13*

## Notable Perspectives

- **The patient as a payor class.** Blake Madden and Cedar’s team argue that the patient is now functionally a top-three payor for most health systems, growing faster, costing more to collect from, and yielding worse than any insurance contract. A commercially insured patient with a $12,000 bronze-plan deductible is labeled “good insurance” on every payor-mix dashboard but is de facto self-pay for most encounters. The framing makes visible a structural shift that conventional payor-mix reporting obscures.
  *Article 6*

- **Becker’s supply-side diagnosis of US healthcare.** Scott Becker argues that the central crisis is a supply-and-demand imbalance in clinical capacity, and that both the left’s coverage-expansion approach and the right’s market-model arguments miss the point. Expanding coverage without expanding the number of physicians, advanced practitioners, and triage points is “like writing a check you can’t cash.” His read of Medicare Advantage as “a massive disaster” — more spending per enrollee, worse access — is a sharper verdict than most industry insiders would state publicly.
  *Article 15*

- **The infrastructure-layer thesis for durable healthcare IT returns.** Ezra Mehlman’s argument that the application layer will generate enormous value but the infrastructure layer will capture the durable returns is the most explicit capital-allocation framework in this batch. Infrastructure companies that have already paid the integration cost and secured regulatory permission become more valuable as AI creates more uses for healthcare data, not less. The risk is platform capture by Epic and Oracle, and the counterargument is that the hardest exchange problems sit outside the acute-care core — in post-acute, behavioral health, and home-based settings where dominant EHR footprints are thinner.
  *Article 11*
