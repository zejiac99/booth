# Behavioral Prep — The 20% That Matters

Synthesized from the WSP RedBook behavioral section and the BIWS 400 Questions fit/behavioral section.

---

## The core insight

You do not prepare 120 answers. You prepare **four assets**, then map every question onto them.

| Asset | What it is | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Your Story** | The resume walkthrough | Short: 100–150 words · Full: 200–300 words |
| **3 Short Stories** | Success · Failure · Leadership | ~100–150 words each (under 60 seconds) |
| **3 Strengths / 3 Weaknesses** | Real but not disqualifying | 1–2 sentences each, tied to a story |
| **Your Real Weakness defense** | The obvious objection to your profile | 3–4 sentences |

Both guides converge here. Everything below is how to build these four and where they get deployed.

---

## Asset 1: Your Story

The single most important answer in any interview. "Walk me through your resume," "Tell me about yourself," and "Why are you here today?" are the same question.

**The four-beat structure:**

1. **The Beginning** — hometown, university, or business school
2. **The Finance Spark** — the specific person, event, or experience that made you interested
3. **Growing Interest** — how you built relevant skills and experience over time
4. **Why You're Here Today** — why this firm and this group fit your plan

The WSP framing is the same structure as a chain of "Why?" questions: why this school, why this major, why are you qualified, why are you sitting here.

**What makes it work:** the Spark must be specific. "I've always liked business" is dead on arrival. A particular deal you followed, a family business, one class, one conversation — that's the beat people remember, and it's the one that makes the rest of the story feel inevitable rather than assembled.

**Time budget:** 1–2 hours minimum. This is the highest-ROI hour of your entire prep.

---

## Asset 2: The three short stories

Pick these once. Re-use them everywhere.

**Success story** — ideally from work. Something you drove to a concrete outcome. Use the STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result), but weight it toward Action and Result.

**Failure story** — must be a *real* failure. A B+ is not a failure. A business that folded, a club that didn't grow, an internship that went badly, a project that missed. The interviewer is listening for the lesson, not the wound. If you narrate how bad it was without what changed, you've failed the question about failure.

**Leadership story** — must involve working *with others* inside an organization. That's the only thing separating it from the success story. Focus on the specific challenge you overcame.

**Coverage check.** These three cover, at minimum: team projects, teams that broke down, ethical dilemmas, difficult leadership moments, informal leadership, biggest failure, biggest mistake, greatest strength, greatest weakness, most impressive resume entries, feedback you received, unmanageable workload, and conflict resolution. That's roughly 15 questions off three stories.

## Asset 3: Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths worth citing:** quantitative/analytical ability, client management, capacity for long hours, attention to detail, work ethic, team/leadership skills, accounting and finance knowledge.

The WSP move: lead with a *general* adjective, then immediately tell a story that proves it at a level most people can't match. The adjective is not the point. The story is. Starting vague gives the story room to show three or four qualities at once.

**Weaknesses that work:** not delegating well, taking too long to decide, not speaking up when you see a problem, not giving teammates useful feedback.

**Weaknesses that end the interview:** poor math, weak attention to detail, trouble with long hours, difficulty with financial statements. Also avoid medical conditions — anxiety, ADHD, insomnia. Those aren't fixable-with-effort, and that's the whole test.

The pattern: pick something real, fixable with effort, and adjacent to management rather than core analyst work. Back it with a story. Add one line on what you're doing about it — but keep it short, or the answer bloats.

## Asset 4: Your real weakness

Bankers are risk averse. Their ideal candidate went to a top school, has high grades, relevant finance internships, interesting hobbies, and is personable. **Anything in your profile that departs from that will be asked about directly.**

Common ones: low GPA, unknown or non-target school, no finance coursework, no relevant work experience, an employment gap, no return offer, a non-business major, a career switch.

**Two tools:**

- **Improvement trend** — the thing was true, here's the tangible evidence it changed. Best for grades.
- **Compensatory factor** — you lack X, but you have Y, and here's how Y transfers. Best for missing experience or coursework.

**The rule both guides repeat: less is more.** The more you explain a flaw, the more it looks like you're hiding something. Acknowledge, pivot to evidence, stop talking.

**On not getting a return offer:** admit it. Nobody believes the alternative. Then explain what you did next and why it prepared you.

## The Big 5

If these five are strong, everything else is downhill.

1. **Walk me through your resume** — Asset 1.
2. **Why investment banking?** — See the section below; this is the one people underrate.
3. **Why our bank?** — Requires actual homework. Name people you've spoken with. Name a recent deal. Generic reasons ("the culture," "client interaction") apply to every bank and signal you did none of the work.
4. **What are your strengths and weaknesses?** — Assets 2 and 3, 2–3 of each, kept concise.
5. **Explain your [real weakness]** — Asset 4.

## "Why investment banking?" done properly

If your story is built well, this shouldn't need asking. It gets asked anyway, mostly as a consistency check against what you already said.

**The durable answer** (BIWS is emphatic that it's the only one): you want to work deals start to finish, and banking is the field that offers that in both depth and breadth. Private equity and VC touch deals too, but they kill most of them early. Your prior internships took you *part* of the way and were missing specific pieces — name them. If you did Big 4 work, maybe you only touched one slice of a transaction, like purchase price allocation. You want the whole process, and you want volume: many deals, many reps.

**WSP's complementary angle** — make three things unmistakable:

- You're not here for consensus, prestige, or money
- You know the actual day-to-day (modeling, transaction support, updating deliverables), not a fantasy of it
- You think the skills are worth the hours, and you're not pretending the hours aren't real

The framing that works: personal growth and the relationships you build with the people next to you in the trenches. It signals you understand the difficulty and want it anyway.

**Answers that hurt you:** "I want to learn a lot." "The exit opportunities." "I thrive in high-stakes environments." Prestige. Money. Each of these is either true of dozens of jobs or actively disqualifying.

**Where the guides differ slightly:** BIWS wants a narrow, almost single-track answer. WSP allows more room for the growth-and-challenge framing. Use BIWS for the logical spine and WSP for the warmth.

**Related variants to expect:** "Where do you see yourself in 5–10 years?" (at Analyst level you can be open-ended; at Associate level and above, the only acceptable answer is full commitment to a long-term banking career). "How do we know you won't leave for the buy-side?" — don't overclaim lifelong devotion; the interviewer may be recruiting for the buy-side themselves. Say it depends on how the next two years go, which implies a two-year commitment without stating it awkwardly, then pivot to what you expect to gain.

## Market fluency: one deal, one industry

Neither guide gives this a dedicated question with a framework, which understates how often it comes up. Both treat market awareness as ambient evidence of interest rather than a set-piece answer. Where it actually surfaces:

- **"Tell me about a deal our firm has completed."** (WSP) Note this is *their* deal, not one you picked. Choose a recent close — it's fresh for the team, and it becomes a real conversation if your interviewer was staffed on it.
- **"What do you know about our firm?"** (WSP) At minimum: history, industry and product groups, recent deal news. Don't recite Wikipedia. Lead with a positive trend you've noticed, then name a specific deal you found interesting.
- **"Our group focuses on [industry]. Which verticals are you most familiar with?"** (WSP) The closest thing to a trends question. Come with recent developments, companies you enjoy following, and — at a bare minimum — the revenue and expense drivers of that industry.
- **"Why our bank?"** (BIWS) The sample answer name-checks a specific recent transaction. Deal awareness is baked in rather than asked for directly.
- **Summarizing recent market and M&A activity** appears in the BIWS Understanding Banking intro as a topic you should know cold, though it never gets a numbered question.

**What to actually build.** Two things, and they're small:

**One deal you can discuss fluently.** Ideally one the firm worked on, or one in the group's sector. Know the buyer, the target, roughly the size, the strategic rationale, and — this is the part people skip — what you find *interesting* about it. Having an opinion is what separates this from a headline you memorized.

**One industry you follow.** Pick something you have a real connection to rather than whatever seems most prestigious. Know its revenue and cost drivers, a few companies you track, and one or two recent developments. WSP names the free sources worth using: primers from McKinsey, Bain, and Deloitte, plus the monthly M&A and valuation commentary that middle-market banks publish.

**One caution, from BIWS.** When explaining why you prefer a particular industry group, don't lead with industry fundamentals. Saying you're drawn to real estate banking because real estate hedges inflation sounds strange. Lead with the personal thread — a family business, an internship, a class, a case competition — and hold the fundamentals in reserve for when they're asked. Know them cold; just don't open with them.

Summer is the right time for this because it compounds. A light weekly habit now means that by the fall you'll have months of context instead of a cram session, and the difference is audible.

## Recurring techniques worth internalizing

**Reframe the question you're answering.** WSP's central move. When a question can't be answered in a way that makes you stand out, briefly answer it and shift to the adjacent question that can. "Why did you choose your university?" becomes "What did you get out of your time there?" "Why is your GPA low?" becomes "How have you grown since freshman year?" This is a conversation; small deviations are normal.

**Show, don't tell.** Never assert a quality. Assert it and immediately prove it with a specific anecdote. This applies to multi-tasking, presentation skills, work ethic, all of it.

**Frame everything positively.** Never criticize a past employer, school, or experience. Interviewers will bait you into it — "your school is a non-target, right?", "I heard that firm's culture is terrible." They want to see whether you take the bait.

**Roll with the punches.** Confrontational questions ("your GPA is below our bar," "this isn't the strongest resume") test composure first and substance second. Getting rattled is the failure mode. These are low-downside, high-upside: the interviewer is telling you the exact objection blocking your offer, which is a gift.

**Don't sound rehearsed.** Interviewers know the guides — many used them. On technical questions, a guide-sounding answer is fine because it's correct. On behavioral questions, it's fatal. Prepare frameworks, not scripts.

## Second-tier questions

Real but lower-leverage. Mostly derivative of the four assets.

**Recruiting process.** Which other banks — name firms at the same tier; it's social proof, and mixing tiers signals you don't think you'll clear theirs. Which groups — say you'd be grateful for any group, then name a couple of preferences including the interviewer's. Have you received offers — say so if yes, it doubles your desirability.

**Understanding banking.** Know the deal processes cold: sell-side M&A, buy-side M&A, IPO, debt issuance, LBO, what's in a pitch book, industry vs. product groups. These are closer to technicals than fit questions, and they're binary — you either know them or you don't.

**Firm-specific.** Know the firm's history, groups, and one recent deal you can discuss with genuine interest.

**Elevator / airport test.** Would they want to be stuck with you? Have real hobbies. Have a book that isn't Rosenbaum & Pearl. Don't overthink "tell me something interesting" — the only bad answer is a blank stare.

**Outside the box.** Personal Beta, if you were an animal, $1 million you can't invest, the 30-year headline. You can't prepare these. Stay light, show personality, don't try to force banker-traits into every answer.

## Questions you ask at the end

Near-certain, and it usually closes the interview. People remember beginnings and endings, so this one carries disproportionate weight.

**Good territory:** their career path and how they chose this group, what surprised them most, what made past interns successful, a specific deal, an industry view they hold that others don't.

**Avoid:** anything Googleable, anything answerable with yes or no, compensation, generic questions like "what qualities do you look for in a candidate."

Ask real follow-ups that show you listened. The goal is a conversation where they enjoy talking about themselves and their career — end on that note.

**Read the signal:** if this section is cut short after one question, the interview probably didn't go well. Not always, but usually.

## What to actually build

In priority order:

1. Story — short and full versions
2. Three short stories
3. Real-weakness defense
4. Three strengths, three weaknesses
5. "Why banking" — the portable half
6. One deal and one industry you can speak to, then kept warm weekly
7. Deal process walkthroughs (sell-side, buy-side, IPO, debt, LBO, pitch book, groups)
8. Per-firm research — recent deals, people you've spoken with, why this bank

Items 1–5 are firm-agnostic and portable across every interview you'll sit. Build those once, well, and the marginal cost of each additional bank is small. Item 6 is the one that rewards starting early, because it accrues rather than gets crammed. Item 8 is the only piece you repeat per bank.

## The traps, condensed

- Reciting your resume instead of explaining the *why* behind each decision
- A "failure" that isn't one
- Weaknesses that disqualify you or that are medical
- Over-explaining a flaw
- Any negativity about a past school, employer, or experience
- Generic "why our firm" that could apply anywhere
- Sounding scripted
- Prestige, money, or exits as your stated motivation
- Bashing a teammate in a teamwork-gone-wrong story
- Claiming a quality without proving it
- Naming a deal you can't discuss past the headline
- Opening with industry fundamentals instead of the personal reason
- Overclaiming lifelong commitment to banking when asked about the buy-side
